# Facilitator script

The run sheet for a calibration session. Written to be usable standing up, by someone who
has not facilitated one before.

The facilitator's job is not to have opinions about people. It is to make sure every rating
in the room is supported by evidence held to the same standard, and to make sure the person
with the most authority in the room does not become the standard by default.

## Before the room

Thirty minutes of preparation is the difference between a session that finds things and a
session that confirms what managers already wrote.

1. **Build the agenda from the pre-session checks, not from the alphabet.** Order by what
   needs discussion. Put two or three genuinely contested cases early — the room learns
   its norms from the first fifteen minutes, and if the first three names are waved
   through, everything after is waved through.
2. **Read every rationale.** Mark the ones that contain no specific evidence, the ones
   built entirely on the last six weeks, and the ones that describe a person rather than
   their work. These are your interventions and you want them ready, not improvised.
3. **Note the outliers you will name.** Which manager's distribution departs most from the
   population. Which level's ratings cluster. Which people have not been discussed in any
   previous cycle.
4. **Decide where you will spend the time** and write the minutes against the names. You
   will overrun on the first contested case; knowing the budget lets you take it back.
5. **Check the room's power dynamics.** Who outranks whom, who is new, whose team is under
   scrutiny for other reasons. If one person in the room can materially affect the careers
   of the others, plan to take their view last on every name.

Have to hand: the framework or level definitions, the rating scale definitions, the
distribution shape, the pre-session cuts, and last cycle's ratings for the same population.

## Opening the session

Say this, or a version of it in your own words. It takes three minutes and it sets every
norm the session depends on.

> We are here to make sure a rating means the same thing in each of our teams. That is the
> only purpose. We are not deciding pay, we are not doing succession, and we are not
> ranking teams against each other.
>
> The standard is the level expectations, not our private standards and not each person's
> own history. The question for every name is: does the evidence meet the expectation for
> the level they are at?
>
> I will ask everyone the same three questions: what did they do, compared to who, and what
> would have made this a higher rating. If I ask you those, it is not a challenge to your
> judgement — I am asking everyone.
>
> A rating that cannot be evidenced does not survive this room. That applies to high
> ratings as much as low ones, and it applies to mine as much as yours.
>
> Two ground rules. Everything said here stays in this room, including the fact that a
> rating changed. And nobody may say "I wanted to give them a higher rating but the room
> stopped me" — if you leave here with a rating you cannot defend, tell me now and we will
> keep working on it.
>
> We have [X] minutes and [Y] people. Some names will take five minutes and some will take
> twenty seconds. I will say out loud when I am confirming a name without discussion, and
> anyone can stop me.

Then state the distribution guidance and what it is for, in one sentence: *the shape
describes the company, not your team; if your team departs from it I will ask you why, and
"because they are strong" is a fine answer if you can evidence it.*

## The per-person protocol

Run the same sequence for every person who is being discussed. The sameness is the point —
it is what makes the questions feel like process rather than suspicion.

**1. Facilitator states the name, level, and proposed rating.** Not the manager. The
facilitator reading it flattens the presentation quality difference between managers, which
is otherwise a real source of rating variance — the manager who presents well gets their
ratings through.

**2. Manager gives ninety seconds of evidence.** Hold the time. What the person did, at
what scope, with what outcome. Cut off narrative about the person's character, their
potential, or how hard they work.

**3. Facilitator asks the evidence questions.** In order, escalating only as needed. See
below.

**4. Room contributes.** Ask specifically: *who else here has worked with this person and
seen the work?* Untargeted "any thoughts?" produces either silence or the loudest voice.
Named cross-functional evidence is the highest-value input in the room and it needs to be
asked for.

**5. Facilitator states the decision and the reason.** Out loud, in one sentence. "Rating
stands at 3, evidence is the migration delivery and the two incident reviews." Or "moving
to 2 — the evidence covers the last quarter only and the first half was flagged as
off-track."

**6. Note-taker records it** with the reason. Then move.

For names being confirmed rather than discussed: read the name, the rating, pause two
seconds, say "confirmed" and move. The pause is not a formality — say explicitly at the
start that anyone can stop a confirmation, and mean it.

## The evidence questions

These three do most of the work. They move a discussion from impression to evidence without
accusing anyone of anything.

**"What did they do?"**

The base question. Asked when the rationale is adjectival — "great year", "really stepped
up", "solid". Ask it flatly and then wait. Silence after this question is enormously
productive and most facilitators fill it too early.

Follow with: *what was the situation, what did they specifically decide or do, what
happened as a result?* If the manager cannot get past "they were involved in", the evidence
is about the team and not the person.

**"Compared to who?"**

The calibration question. A rating is a comparative claim whether or not the manager
intends it to be, so make the comparison explicit.

Variants to use depending on what you are testing:

- *Who else in this room is at the same level, and where does this person sit against them?*
- *You have three people at this level. Rank them and tell me what separates first from
  third.* Useful when a manager has rated a whole cohort identically — the ranking usually
  exists in their head and reveals the rating does not reflect it.
- *Is this the same performance you rated a 4 last year?* Tests scale drift over time.
- *If [named person from another team, same level] had done exactly this, would you rate
  them the same?* This is the sharpest version and the one to hold in reserve. It converts
  an abstract standard into a concrete comparison the room can adjudicate.

**"What would have made this a higher rating?"**

The most useful question in calibration and the most underused. Ask it of every rating,
including the top one.

It works because it forces the manager to describe the boundary of the rating, and a
manager who cannot describe what better would look like does not have a standard — they
have an impression. It also produces, as a by-product, the development conversation the
person should have been having all year.

For a top rating, the version is *what does the level above look like, and what is missing?*
A manager who cannot answer that has rated on affection or on the level above's work
already being done, which is a promotion conversation rather than a rating.

**Supporting questions, used as needed:**

- *What is the evidence from the first half of the year?* The single most effective
  recency interrupter.
- *What would you have said in month four?* Same purpose, different door.
- *What did they get wrong this year, and how did they handle it?* A rationale with no
  failures in it is an incomplete observation, not a flawless person.
- *Who else saw this work?* Tests whether the evidence is visible or an inference.
- *What would this person's peers say if I asked them?* Useful where the manager's view and
  the room's instinct diverge.
- *Is this rating about the work or about the volume of the work?* Catches the effort-for-
  outcome substitution, which is where people on reduced hours get penalised.

## Difficult moment: the manager who cannot evidence a rating

This is the central moment of calibration and it needs handling that gets the right outcome
without humiliating anyone. A manager who feels ambushed defends harder, and every other
manager in the room learns to stop being candid.

**The sequence:**

1. **Ask the base question once more, more specifically.** *Give me one situation. What did
   they do and what happened.* Sometimes the manager has the evidence and presented badly.
2. **Name what is missing, not what is wrong with them.** "I do not have anything here I
   can compare against the other 3s. What am I missing?" — puts the gap in the record, not
   in the manager's competence.
3. **Offer the honest exit.** "It may be the evidence exists and is not written down. Do
   you want to bring it back at the end of the session?" This is genuinely better than
   forcing a decision on thin information, and it preserves the manager's standing.
4. **If nothing comes back, hold the standard.** "We cannot support this rating with what
   we have. It moves to [X], and the reason recorded is that the evidence presented did not
   distinguish this from [X]." Say it evenly. Do not soften it into ambiguity.

**What not to do.** Do not let the room fill the gap for the manager — when three other
people supply reasons a rating might be justified, you have manufactured consensus around
no evidence. Do not accept "you had to be there". Do not accept tenure, likeability,
availability, or hours worked as evidence of performance.

**Say this once, early in the cycle, and once in the room:** the inability to evidence a
rating is usually a management gap rather than a performance gap. The person may well be
excellent. What has failed is that nobody wrote down what they did. That framing is true,
it lands better, and it produces better rationales next cycle.

## Difficult moment: the dominant voice

Usually the most senior person present, sometimes the most confident, occasionally just the
loudest. The damage is specific: their standard becomes the room's standard without ever
being examined, and everyone else's ratings get adjusted toward it.

**Structural prevention, which beats intervention:**

- **Take their view last, on every name.** Establish this in the opening as a rule for
  everyone, so it does not read as aimed at one person. Once a senior voice has spoken,
  the independent reads in the room are gone.
- **Poll before discussing on contested names.** Ask each manager to write a rating on
  paper, then reveal. Disagreement that is visible gets discussed; disagreement that is
  suppressed gets absorbed.
- **Have them present their own team's names first,** early in the session, and hold them
  to the same three questions in front of everyone. It sets the norm that nobody is exempt
  and it costs the facilitator nothing.

**In-the-moment interventions, in escalation order:**

- *Let me hold that for a second — I want [manager]'s read first, then yours.*
- *That is a view on the person. What is the evidence?* Applied to a senior voice in front
  of the room, once, this resets the standard for the whole session.
- *You have made that case. Who sees it differently?* Then wait. Do not rescue the silence.
- *We are relitigating. The decision was [X] and the reason was [Y]. If there is new
  evidence I will reopen it; if not we are moving on.*
- **Offline, at the break:** "You are shaping every rating in the room before the managers
  get a chance. I need your view later in each discussion so I can see what they actually
  think." Most senior people accept this immediately when it is put as a mechanics problem
  rather than a behaviour problem.

**The variant to watch for:** the dominant voice who is right. A senior leader with genuine
cross-team visibility often does have the best evidence in the room. The problem is not
their accuracy, it is that their speaking first destroys everyone else's independent
signal. Take their evidence — take it last.

## Difficult moment: the silent room

A room that agrees with everything has either perfect ratings or has learned that challenge
is expensive. It is almost always the second.

**Diagnose the cause before intervening:**

- Managers do not want their own ratings challenged, so they challenge nobody. This is a
  trade the room makes silently within about twenty minutes.
- The first challenge went badly and everyone watched.
- The people in the room have no visibility of each other's teams and genuinely have
  nothing to say.

**Interventions:**

- **Ask a named person a specific question.** *You worked with them on the platform
  project — what did you see?* Beats "any thoughts" every time.
- **Assign a challenger role.** Before the session, ask one manager per group to prepare a
  challenge on someone else's rating. It makes the first challenge a role rather than an
  attack, and after two or three the room usually unlocks by itself.
- **Model it on your own or on a senior person's team first.** Challenge a rating from the
  most senior manager present, visibly, and let them answer it well. The room learns that
  challenge is survivable.
- **Name the pattern.** "We have agreed with every rating for forty minutes. Either these
  are the best-calibrated ratings I have seen, or we have stopped saying things. Which is
  it?" Said lightly, this works surprisingly often.
- **Where the room genuinely lacks visibility,** stop asking it for opinions and use the
  time on the evidence questions instead. A session where the facilitator interrogates the
  rationales is still worth running.

## Difficult moment: the trade

Two managers reach an accommodation: *I will not push on yours if you do not push on mine.*
Sometimes explicit, more often a pattern you notice across three or four names.

Trades are corrosive because they look exactly like consensus. The signal is a room where
challenge is distributed by relationship rather than by evidence quality.

**Interventions:**

- **Break the reciprocity by ordering.** Interleave names so that a manager's own people
  are never discussed immediately after they have supported someone else's.
- **Name it neutrally when you see it.** "We have accepted the last four ratings without a
  question and two of them had no first-half evidence. I am going to go back to [name]."
- **Return to a settled name.** Nothing deters a trade like the knowledge that agreement
  does not close a case if the evidence was thin.
- **The most senior version** — an executive telling the facilitator beforehand which
  ratings must survive — is not a facilitation problem, it is a governance problem. Take it
  out of the room: put the request on the record, put it to the session as an input like
  any other, and if it is overridden, escalate it through the People lead rather than
  absorbing it.

## Difficult moment: the decision that reopens

A rating gets settled, and forty minutes later a manager returns to it — usually because
the discussion on a later name made theirs look harsh by comparison.

The instinct to reopen is often legitimate. Handle it with a rule stated in the opening,
not a judgement made in the moment.

**The rule:** a decision reopens only on new evidence, not on new feeling. Comparison to a
later case counts as new evidence — it is exactly what calibration is for — but it reopens
both cases, not one.

**The script:** *That is a fair comparison. It means one of these two is wrong. We will
take both back at the end with the evidence side by side. It is on the list.*

Keep a visible parking list and genuinely return to it. A parking list that is never
revisited teaches the room that parking means burying, and the next challenge will be
fought harder because nobody trusts the deferral.

## Difficult moment: the rating defended on potential

*"They are going to be exceptional."* Common, well-intentioned, and it breaks the scale.

A performance rating describes the period that has ended. Potential is a different
assessment with a different purpose and it belongs in a different meeting. Merging them
means high-potential people get inflated ratings for work they did not do, which is unfair
to peers, and it is also the mechanism by which "potential" — the least evidenced judgement
in the whole system, and the one most exposed to similarity bias — leaks into pay.

**The script:** *That is a potential judgement and I want it, but not here. For this rating
I need what they did between [dates]. Put the potential view in the talent review.*

The same applies in reverse to a low rating defended on the person being "not a fit" or
"not going to make it here". Rate the period. Whatever comes next is a separate decision
that has its own process and, if it heads toward exit, its own legal review.

## Difficult moment: the surprise

A rating in the room is materially worse than anything the person has been told all year.

Stop and ask directly: *does this person know?* If the answer is no, that is a finding
about the manager, not only about the rating.

**Handling:** the rating is not softened to spare the manager the conversation — that
compounds the failure and makes next year worse. But the delivery gets support: the People
partner works with the manager on the conversation, and the manager's own review should
reflect that they let a performance problem run unspoken for a year.

If the rating carries a formal consequence — a performance process, a pay outcome, anything
on an exit path — and the person has had no prior notice, flag it for review before it is
actioned. In several jurisdictions a first-notice-at-outcome sequence is a procedural
weakness that matters.

## Closing a decision so it stays closed

Every discussed name closes with the same three elements, said aloud by the facilitator:

1. **The decision.** "Rating is 3."
2. **The reason, in evidence terms.** "The scope of what she owned matches the level; the
   delivery evidence is the migration and the vendor renegotiation."
3. **The status.** "That is closed unless someone has new evidence."

Then a genuine two-second pause, then the next name.

Why all three: a decision stated without a reason gets remembered differently by everyone
in the room and reopens by default. A reason stated without a closing marker leaves the
room uncertain whether it is still discussing. And the pause is the last legitimate moment
to object, which is what makes the closure fair.

Where a rating changed, add: *and [manager] will be supported on how to deliver that.* The
manager must not leave the room with a changed rating and no plan for the conversation.

## Closing the session

Five minutes, and do not skip it.

1. **Read back every changed rating and its reason.** Confirms the record and gives the
   last chance to correct a mishearing. Changes get miscommunicated more often than they
   get contested.
2. **State the final distribution** and ask the room whether it looks like the population
   they know. Not to adjust individuals — to test whether the session did what it set out
   to do.
3. **Confirm the communication timeline and the rule about attribution.** Repeat it:
   nobody blames the room.
4. **Note what to fix next cycle.** Where the framework was ambiguous, which parts of the
   scale nobody could distinguish, which evidence was consistently missing. This takes two
   minutes and it is the only mechanism by which calibration quality improves.
5. **Say what happens next and when.** Checks re-run, records finalised, manager briefings,
   communication window, appeals window.

## What the facilitator records

- Every rating as submitted and as final.
- The reason for every change, in evidence terms.
- Names parked and how they resolved.
- Which managers could not evidence ratings, and which patterns recurred — for their own
  development conversation, not for punishment.
- Ambiguities in the framework or scale that caused genuine disagreement.
- Any request for a rating outcome that came from outside the room.

Write these as if they will be read by someone else, because they may be. Factual, about
work, no speculation about anyone's character, health or circumstances.

## Phrase bank

Short lines that do a lot of work in the room.

| Situation | Line |
|---|---|
| Adjectival rationale | "What did they do?" |
| Rating with no comparison | "Compared to who?" |
| Any rating, including top | "What would have made this a higher rating?" |
| Last-quarter evidence only | "What is the evidence from the first half?" |
| Effort substituted for outcome | "That is effort. What was the outcome?" |
| Personality substituted for work | "That describes them. What did they deliver?" |
| Whole team rated identically | "Rank them and tell me what separates first from last." |
| Room agreeing too fast | "Who sees this differently?" then wait. |
| Senior voice going first | "Hold that — I want the manager's read first." |
| Potential defence | "That is a potential judgement. What did they do this period?" |
| Unevidenced high rating | "I cannot distinguish this from the 3s. What am I missing?" |
| Reopened decision | "New evidence, or new feeling?" |
| Time slipping | "Two minutes on this, then we park it." |
| Closing | "Decision is X, reason is Y, closed unless there is new evidence." |
