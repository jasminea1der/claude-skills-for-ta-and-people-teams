# Bias interrupters

Each pattern below is written to be used, not taught. For each: how it presents in the
room, the words that signal it, the intervention the facilitator makes in the moment, and
the statistical check that detects it before and after the session.

Two framing points to hold throughout.

**Do not run this as awareness training.** Telling a room that bias exists changes almost
nothing about what happens in it. What changes outcomes is a structural interruption at the
moment the judgement is made — a required question, a required piece of evidence, a
required comparison. Everything below is written as an interruption for that reason.

**Interrupt the process, never the individual rating on a protected basis.** These checks
exist to find where the process is producing inconsistent judgement. The fix is applied to
the evidence and the standard. Changing any individual's rating because of their sex, race,
age, disability, or any other protected characteristic is unlawful, including when done
with the intention of correcting an imbalance.

## Recency

**What it is.** The rating reflects the last six to eight weeks rather than the period.
This is the most common distortion in calibration and the least contested when named,
because managers recognise it immediately in themselves.

**How it presents.** The rationale is entirely built from recent work. A person who
delivered heavily in Q1 and had a quiet Q4 gets rated down; someone who shipped something
visible three weeks before the deadline gets rated up. A recent mistake dominates a strong
year. The manager can describe the last project in detail and nothing before it.

**What it sounds like.** "Really strong finish to the year." "Recently they have been much
more…" "The last project was a bit of a mess." Detailed narrative on one recent thing and
generalities elsewhere.

**The intervention.**

- *What is the evidence from the first half of the period?* Ask it of every rating, not
  only suspicious ones. Making it universal is what stops it reading as an accusation.
- *What would you have said about this person in month four?* Different door to the same
  room; useful when the first question gets a defensive answer.
- If the manager cannot produce first-half evidence, say so plainly: this rating covers a
  quarter of the period. Either find the evidence or the rating reflects a quarter.
- **The strongest interrupt happens before the room:** require the pre-work rationale to
  include at least one substantial piece of evidence from the first half. This single
  requirement removes more recency distortion than any in-session technique.

**The check.** Correlate rating against timing of the person's most visible delivery, where
you can identify it. More practically: sample the rationales and count how many cite
nothing before the midpoint. If a large share of a manager's rationales are built only on
the second half, that manager's whole set needs re-examination, not just one name. Also
check whether people with heavy Q1 delivery cycles (annual planning, seasonal peaks) rate
systematically lower than people whose work lands late in the year — that is a structural
recency signature, not a coincidence.

## Similarity to the manager

**What it is.** Higher ratings for people who work like the manager works, communicate the
way the manager communicates, or resemble them in background. It is the hardest bias to
name in a room, because it is invisible to the person doing it — the manager genuinely
experiences it as a quality judgement.

**How it presents.** A manager's high ratings share a style: the direct communicators, the
people who bring solutions rather than problems, the ones who work the same hours, the ones
from the same functional background. The people rated lower are often delivering
comparably by different means — the careful one, the quiet one, the one who raises risks.

**What it sounds like.** "They think like I do." "I never have to worry about them." "They
just get it." "Easy to work with." "Low maintenance." Judgements about how someone operates
rather than what they achieved.

**The intervention.**

- *Is that a judgement about the outcome, or about the style?* The most useful single
  question here, and it is not accusatory.
- *If someone got the same result by a completely different route, would you rate them the
  same?* Forces the standard to be about outcome.
- *What did they deliver?* Return to the base evidence question and stay there.
- When a manager's high ratings share an obvious style, name the pattern rather than the
  individual rating: "Your top three all get described as low-maintenance. What did each of
  them actually deliver?"

**The check.** Look at each manager's rating distribution against the demographic and
background composition of their team where lawfully monitored. Look also at
non-demographic style proxies — function of origin, tenure route (internal versus external
hire), location, remote versus office-based. A manager whose high ratings cluster on one
axis is the finding; the response is examining the rationales, never adjusting an
individual.

## Halo and horns

**What it is.** One highly visible event determines the whole rating. A launch, an outage,
a good presentation to the executive team, one difficult meeting. Everything else gets
interpreted through it.

**How it presents.** Halo: an excellent rating across every dimension including ones the
manager has no evidence on, all traceable to one success. Horns: a person whose whole year
is coloured by one incident, often one that was not primarily theirs.

**What it sounds like.** "After the launch, everything else fell into place." "Ever since
that incident I have not been confident in them." A rationale where every dimension is
rated the same. The same event mentioned three times in one discussion.

**The intervention.**

- *Take that event out. What is left?* The sharpest interrupt available. It works because
  it is a concrete instruction rather than a challenge.
- *Where did they fall short this year?* for a halo rating, and *what went well?* for a
  horns rating. A rationale with no counter-evidence in it is an incomplete observation.
- *How much of that outcome was theirs?* Big visible events usually have many
  contributors. The person who presented it is not necessarily the person who did it — and
  the person who did it is frequently not in the room being talked about.
- Where the rating covers multiple dimensions, force them to be rated separately and note
  that identical scores across dimensions are a signal, not an achievement.

**The check.** Look for people connected to a single high-profile success or failure in the
period and check whether their ratings cluster tightly at one end. Post-session, check
whether the rated dimensions correlate almost perfectly with one another — near-perfect
internal correlation across dimensions usually means one impression is being rated
repeatedly rather than several things being assessed.

## Proximity and visibility

**What it is.** People the manager sees more get rated higher. This is not favouritism; the
manager simply has more evidence about them, and evidence abundance reads as performance.
It hits remote workers, people in other offices and timezones, people on secondment, and
people whose work is inherently less visible — maintenance, enablement, risk, quality,
internal platform work.

**How it presents.** A distributed team where the office-based people rate higher. A
manager who has detailed evidence for four people and thin evidence for three. Ratings that
track how often someone appears in a meeting the manager attends.

**What it sounds like.** "I see a lot of them." "They are always around when something
needs doing." "I do not have much visibility on them, but I think…" — that sentence should
stop the discussion every time.

**The intervention.**

- *"I do not have much visibility" is a rating problem, not a rating.* Say it directly.
  Where does the evidence come from — peers, stakeholders, output?
- *Who else in the room has worked with this person?* Cross-functional evidence is the
  direct antidote to proximity, and it has to be actively solicited or it does not arrive.
- *Is the work invisible or absent?* Work that prevents problems produces no visible
  events. A quiet quarter in a reliability role can be the best possible outcome.
- Where a manager has thin evidence on someone through no fault of the person, park the
  name and gather evidence rather than rating on the thin file.

**The check.** Cut ratings by location, by office versus remote, and by whether the person
sits in the same physical or timezone group as their manager. Cut by function within a
mixed team, separating build work from maintenance and enablement work. Persistent gaps
along these lines are structural and should be fixed in how evidence is gathered, not in
individual ratings.

## Language asymmetry in written reviews

**What it is.** The same performance described in systematically different language
depending on who is being described. This is not an external finding to apply to the room;
it is a reading of your own cycle's text. Pull the written rationales and compare the
language used across groups: whose contributions are described as effort, luck or support
received, and whose are described as judgement, ownership and results. Look for identical
behaviour described in different terms for different people — the same assertiveness as
"decisive" in one rationale and "abrasive" in another; more references to attitude, effort
and manner, and fewer to results, in one set than another; and vaguer, shorter,
lower-specificity feedback given to people the manager is less comfortable with, which
deprives them of the concrete guidance others get.

A pattern found this way is a signal to investigate the process — the standard, the
rationales and the evidence behind them. It is never a basis for adjusting an individual's
rating, which on a protected basis is unlawful in either direction.

**How it presents.** Read the rationales side by side and it becomes visible immediately.
One set is full of verbs about what was delivered; the other is full of adjectives about
what the person is like. Critical feedback in one set is specific and actionable; in the
other it is vague and characterological.

**What it sounds like.** Descriptions of manner: abrasive, aggressive, emotional,
difficult, too nice, not assertive enough, needs more executive presence, needs to be more
confident, rubs people the wrong way. Communal framing: supportive, helpful, great team
player, always willing — used as the whole rationale rather than alongside outcomes.

**The intervention.**

- *That is a description of a person. What did they deliver?* The universal reset.
- *Would you write that about [named comparable person]?* Concrete and hard to deflect.
- *What is the specific behaviour and what was its effect on the work?* If "abrasive"
  cannot be reduced to observable behaviour with a work consequence, it is not usable
  evidence and should not carry a rating.
- **"Executive presence", "gravitas", "polish", "confidence" and "cultural fit" carry
  almost no evidentiary content and reliably encode similarity.** Where they appear,
  require them to be restated as an observable behaviour with a business consequence, or
  struck from the rationale entirely.
- Brief this before managers write, not after. It is much easier to prevent than to
  unpick, and the pre-work brief is the right place.

**The check.** This is the most productive text analysis available to a People team, and it
does not need sophisticated tooling. Take the submitted rationales and, at aggregate level
across the whole population:

- Count personality and manner terms versus achievement and outcome terms per rationale,
  and compare the ratio across groups.
- Count the specific manner words above and see how they distribute.
- Measure rationale length and the count of concrete, verifiable claims per rationale.
  Systematically shorter or vaguer rationales for one group is itself a finding.
- Compare the specificity of the development feedback — actionable versus characterological.

Report at aggregate level only. Do not attribute an inference about bias to a named
manager on the basis of a word count; use it to open a conversation about the rationales
they wrote, which is a defensible and much more useful discussion.

## Leave, reduced hours and flexible arrangements

**What it is.** A rating that turns on time absent or hours worked rather than on the work
done. It affects people who were on parental, medical, carer's or other leave during the
period, people working reduced hours, and people with flexible or adjusted arrangements,
and several mechanisms push the same way at once: their output volume is compared to a
full-period colleague's; their reduced availability is read as reduced commitment; they are
absent from the visible moments; and the manager's evidence about them is thinner, which —
see proximity — reads as weaker performance. Whether it is happening here is answerable
from your own cuts, below.

This is the pattern with the sharpest legal edge of any in this file. In most
jurisdictions, less favourable treatment connected to pregnancy, maternity, part-time
status, or a disability-related adjustment is unlawful, and a performance rating is
treatment.

**How it presents.** The four-day-a-week person rated below a five-day colleague who
delivered proportionally the same. Someone back from six months' leave rated on a
whole-year expectation. A rating rationale that mentions availability, responsiveness, or
"being around" as a factor.

**What it sounds like.** "Hard to judge — they were out for a chunk of it." "Not as
available as the rest of the team." "Less throughput than the others." "Hard to give them
the stretch work with their hours." That last one is a management decision being charged to
the employee.

**The intervention.**

- *Pro-rate the expectation, not the person.* State the rule out loud: performance is
  assessed against what could reasonably be expected in the time and at the hours worked,
  compared like for like.
- *Is that a judgement about the rate of work or the amount of it?* Amount is a function of
  hours and is not a performance signal.
- *Were they given comparable work?* Very often the under-rating started months earlier, in
  who got the visible project. That is the real finding and it belongs in the record.
- *Would this rating be different if they had worked full hours and delivered exactly this
  rate?* If yes, the rating is measuring hours.
- Where an adjustment is in place for a disability, that adjustment is part of how the role
  is performed and is not a performance deficit. Where a rating appears to turn on one, stop
  and get it reviewed before it lands.

**The check.** Cut ratings by: full-time versus part-time, contracted hours band, any leave
of a month or more during the period, return-from-leave within the period, and formal
flexible or adjusted arrangements. Compare against the population distribution. Cut this
before the session so the affected names are on the agenda for discussion, and again after.
Because of the legal exposure, take a material adverse pattern here to counsel rather than
handling it internally as a process observation.

## Central tendency

**What it is.** Everything rated in the middle. Not a bias about people but about the
manager's willingness to make a claim, and it destroys the scale as effectively as
inflation does.

**How it presents.** A manager whose entire team sits on one rating point. Ratings with no
variance where the room's own knowledge says the team has real range.

**What it sounds like.** "They all had a solid year." Rationales that are interchangeable
between people.

**The intervention.**

- *Rank them, and tell me what separates first from last.* The ranking almost always exists
  privately. Once it is spoken, the identical ratings become hard to defend.
- *Who on this team would you most want to keep if you lost half the team, and why?*
  Different route to the same information when the direct ranking gets resisted.
- *What would each of them have to do to move up a point?* If the answers differ, the
  ratings should.

**The check.** Variance of ratings within each manager's team. A manager with near-zero
variance across a team of eight is a flag, as is a manager whose variance is dramatically
higher than everyone else's.

## Tenure and familiarity

**What it is.** Long-tenured people accumulate reputational credit and get rated on their
history; new joiners get rated on an incomplete period as if it were a whole one, or get
default-rated in the middle because nobody knows.

**How it presents.** Someone whose strong performance was three years ago still rated on
it. A new joiner four months in given a mid rating that means nothing and carries a pay
consequence anyway.

**What it sounds like.** "They have earned it." "Everyone knows what they can do." "Too
early to say, so let's put them in the middle."

**The intervention.**

- *What did they do this period?* The base question does most of the work again.
- For short-tenure people, decide the policy in advance rather than in the room — either
  not rated, or rated against a proportionate expectation with the tenure recorded. Ad hoc
  handling of new joiners produces the most inconsistent ratings in any cycle.
- *"Too early to say" is not a mid rating.* Say so; a default-middle rating is a claim,
  and it carries pay.

**The check.** Cut ratings by tenure band. Look particularly at people under a year and
people over five years, both of which distort in predictable directions.

## Attribution asymmetry

**What it is.** Success attributed to the person for some and to circumstance for others;
failure attributed to circumstance for some and to the person for others. The same events,
two different causal stories.

**How it presents.** Two rationales covering the same kind of outcome: "person A was lucky
with that project" alongside "person B turned that project around". "The market made it
hard for person B" alongside "person A did not handle the market well". The contrast to
read for is effort, luck and support received on one side; judgement, ownership and result
on the other.

**What it sounds like.** Causal language that shifts by person: *they delivered it* versus
*it landed well for them*; *A made the call* versus *B got support on that*.

**The intervention.**

- *What specifically did they contribute to that outcome?* Applied consistently to every
  name, including the ones the room already agrees on.
- *You described the same situation differently for two people. Which framing is right?*
  Direct, and effective when the two cases are close together in the session.
- Ask about failure explicitly for every person. A room that only probes causation on the
  low ratings has already made its decision.

**The check.** In the rationales, compare how often success is written in the active voice
with the person as the agent, versus passive or circumstantial framing, across groups. This
is part of the same aggregate text analysis as the language check.

## Anchoring on last year

**What it is.** The prior rating sets the starting point and the discussion becomes about
whether to move it, rather than what the evidence supports. It produces ratings that are
stable regardless of performance and it makes a bad rating very hard to escape.

**How it presents.** Almost nobody moves. Movement is treated as an event requiring
justification while stability requires none.

**What it sounds like.** "They were a 3 last year and nothing has really changed." "I did
not want to drop them."

**The intervention.**

- *Rate the period on its own evidence. What does it support?* Ask before the prior rating
  is mentioned; keep last year's ratings off the working document the room is looking at.
- *Nothing changed is a finding too — is that the right rating for what they did this
  year, or is it last year's rating repeated?*
- *If this person were new to you and you saw only this year, what would you rate them?*

**The check.** Year-over-year movement rate for the population and per manager. Very low
movement is a signal that the process is copying forward rather than assessing. Also check
whether people who received a low rating ever recover — a rating floor that nobody escapes
is a serious problem, both for fairness and because it is visible in a claim.

## Running the checks

**Before the session.** Run every cut, build the agenda from the flags, and bring the cuts
into the room as the facilitator's working document. Their purpose before the session is to
decide where the time goes.

Cut by: manager, level, function, tenure band, location and remote status, full-time versus
part-time and contracted hours, leave during the period, manager change mid-cycle, and
prior-year rating. Then, separately, by any demographic dimension lawfully monitored.

**After the session.** Re-run every cut on the final ratings. Two questions: did anything
get worse, and did the session correct what it was convened to correct? A pattern that
survived the session is a finding about the session, and it should change the design of the
next one.

**Suppression thresholds.** Do not report a demographic cut for a group small enough to
identify individuals. Set the minimum group size in advance and hold it. Report small
groups only as part of a larger aggregate.

**What a concerning result looks like.** There is no threshold that makes a pattern a
finding, and any number quoted as one would be invented. Judge it on: whether the gap is
larger than the variation between other comparable groups, whether it recurs cycle over
cycle, whether it concentrates in particular managers or functions, and whether the
rationales in the affected group are materially thinner or more characterological than
elsewhere. Recurrence and concentration are the two most useful signals.

**What to do with a demographic pattern.** Re-examine the rationales in the affected group
against the standard. Look at whether the work allocation that preceded the ratings was
equitable, because unequal access to visible work produces unequal ratings entirely
legitimately at the rating stage and entirely illegitimately overall. Look at whether
specific managers account for it. Fix the process for the next cycle. Take a material or
recurring pattern to counsel, and run the analysis at counsel's direction where the
jurisdiction offers privilege for it.

**What not to do.** Do not move an individual's rating to improve a distribution on any
demographic dimension. It is unlawful, it is unfair to the individual whose rating is moved
in either direction, and it destroys the evidentiary value of the whole exercise.

## The in-room card

One page for the facilitator to have in front of them.

| Signal in the room | Say this |
|---|---|
| Evidence only from recent weeks | "What is the evidence from the first half?" |
| "They think like I do" / "easy to work with" | "Is that about the outcome or the style?" |
| One event explaining everything | "Take that event out. What is left?" |
| "I do not have much visibility on them" | "Then that is not a rating yet. Who has seen the work?" |
| Personality language, manner words | "That describes them. What did they deliver?" |
| "Abrasive", "too confident", "needs presence" | "What was the behaviour, and what did it cost the work?" |
| Leave, part-time, reduced hours mentioned | "Pro-rate the expectation, not the person. Rate the work done." |
| "Less available than the others" | "Availability is not performance. What was the output rate?" |
| Whole team on one point | "Rank them. What separates first from last?" |
| "They have earned it" | "What did they do this period?" |
| "Too early to say" | "Then it is not a mid rating. Decide the policy, not the person." |
| Same events, different causal story | "You described that differently for two people. Which is right?" |
| "Nothing has changed since last year" | "Rate this year on this year's evidence." |
| Potential offered as justification | "That is potential. What did they do?" |
| Any rating at all | "What would have made this a higher rating?" |
