# Analysis method

How to read a set of engagement survey results so that the conclusions survive being
challenged by someone who understands data — and, more importantly, so that the priorities
that come out of it are the right ones.

## Principles

**Every number reported is either in the data, derived from the data with the working
shown, or absent.** There is no fourth category. Do not supply an industry average, a
"typical" score, a benchmark, or a normal range from general knowledge. If the user has a
comparator, name it and use it. If they do not, say the comparison is unavailable. A
fabricated comparator that reaches a board is unrecoverable.

**Report the uncertainty at the same volume as the finding.** These results get repeated in
rooms the analyst is not in. A finding stated flatly travels; the caveat only travels if it
is in the same sentence.

**Prefer the finding that leads somewhere.** Two readings of the same data are often equally
defensible. Where one of them names something the organisation can act on and the other
does not, lead with the actionable one — and say that is what you have done.

**Aggregate always.** Every legitimate finding in an engagement survey is a finding about a
group. If an analysis only works by looking at one person's answers, it is not an analysis
the survey is permitted to produce.

## Response rate: read it first

The response rate is the frame around every other number. Read it before opening the score
tab, because it determines what those scores are evidence of.

### The four cases

| Response rate | Scores | What it most likely means |
|---|---|---|
| High, stable or rising | Any | The scores mean what they say. Analyse normally. |
| High, but falling from a higher base | Any | Early erosion of belief in the process. Worth naming even at a still-good absolute level. |
| Low or sharply fallen | Poor | Bad, and probably understated — the most disengaged still did not answer. |
| Low or sharply fallen | Good | The trap. An unknown result with a flattering sample. Do not report it as a good result. |

### Why a falling response rate outranks the scores

It has only a few plausible causes, and all of them are more serious than a score movement:

- People concluded that last cycle changed nothing, so answering is not worth the time.
- People stopped believing the confidentiality promise — often traceable to a specific
  incident where someone was, or believed they were, identified from results.
- Survey fatigue from too many instruments asking the same things.
- A group has disengaged far enough that they no longer participate in company processes at
  all, which is the most serious version and usually shows as a collapse concentrated in
  one or two segments rather than an even decline.

Distinguish between them by cutting the response rate itself by segment and by comparing
the decline's shape: an even decline across the company points at fatigue or at last
cycle's inaction; a collapse in one function points at something that happened in that
function.

### Response-rate skew

Cut the response rate by every dimension you will later cut the scores by. Where
participation varies materially between segments, the company-level score is a weighted
average of unequal voices, and it should be reported that way: "the company score is
substantially a reflection of [segment], which responded at [x]% against [y]% in
[segment]".

Two specific traps:

- **The quiet function.** A function with a much lower response rate is often the one with
  the most to say. Treat low participation in a segment as a finding about that segment,
  not as an absence of data.
- **Small teams with 100% response.** Perfect participation in a very small team sometimes
  means participation was observed. Where a manager can tell who has and has not responded,
  the answers from that team are worth less, not more. If the platform shows completion by
  team to managers, note it as a design issue for next cycle.

### What to write

State the response rate, the direction of travel, the headcount base, and the skew, in the
first paragraph of the pack — before any score. If the rate fell materially, that is the
headline finding and the action plan needs an item aimed at it, which is almost always
"visibly deliver and communicate the actions from this cycle" rather than a communications
campaign about the next survey.

## Comparing to the previous cycle

Direction of travel is usually more useful than level. But a year-on-year comparison is only
valid if the thing being compared is comparable, and it frequently is not.

### Check these before reporting any movement

**Did the population change?** The most common invisible cause of score movement.

- *Leavers.* If the least engaged people left, every score rises without anything improving.
  This is arithmetically certain and routinely reported as progress. Where attrition was
  material, say that some of the movement is compositional.
- *Joiners.* New joiners typically answer more positively in their first months. A company
  that grew 40% has a materially different respondent base, and its improvement may be
  entirely new-joiner optimism. Cut by tenure to see: if the improvement disappears among
  people present for both cycles, it was composition.
- *Restructures and exits from the population.* A function that was outsourced, sold or
  closed takes its scores with it.

The clean test, where the data supports it: compare only the population present in both
cycles. Say when you have done this, because it is a different and better number than the
headline movement.

**Did the instrument change?** A reworded item, a changed scale (5-point to 7-point,
agreement to satisfaction), a different favourability cut-off, a new vendor, or a
re-baselined benchmark all break the comparison. Vendors sometimes change the calculation of
a favourability score without changing the item. Where the instrument changed, say the
comparison cannot be made rather than making it with a footnote — a footnoted number gets
quoted without the footnote.

**Did the context change?** A pay freeze, a redundancy round, a return-to-office mandate, a
leadership change, an acquisition, a funding event, a public incident, or a survey run three
weeks after a restructure announcement. None of these invalidate the results; all of them
change what the results are measuring. Note them next to the scores they most plausibly
touch, framed as context, not as an excuse — the distinction matters, because a reader who
smells excuse-making stops believing the rest of the document.

### What counts as a material change

Resist calling every movement a trend. A useful discipline in the absence of a formal
significance test:

- Compare the movement on the item to the typical movement across all items in the same
  cycle. An item that moved two points in a survey where everything moved two points has
  not done anything distinctive; the whole survey moved, which is its own finding.
- Look for movement that is consistent across segments — a shift appearing in most functions
  is more likely real than a large shift in one small group.
- Prefer a smaller movement repeated across two cycles to a large one-cycle jump. Direction
  sustained beats magnitude once.
- Where the segment is small, expect noise. The smaller the group, the larger the movement
  that means nothing.

If the platform reports statistical significance, use it and say what test it applied. Do
not manufacture one.

## External comparison

Use only comparators the user actually has: their vendor's benchmark set, an industry panel
they subscribe to, a group they belong to, or their own historic data. Name the comparator
and its basis every time you cite it — "against the vendor's [sector] benchmark, [n]
companies, updated [date]".

Two cautions worth passing to the user:

- **Benchmark sets are self-selecting.** Companies that buy engagement platforms and run
  surveys are not a random sample of employers. A score at benchmark means "normal among
  companies that measure this", which is a narrower claim than it sounds.
- **Benchmarks are the weakest basis for an action.** They tell you where you sit, not what
  to do. A below-benchmark score on an item nobody in the organisation cares about is not a
  priority; an at-benchmark score on the thing driving your attrition is. Use internal
  comparison — between segments, and against your own last cycle — as the primary lens and
  the benchmark as context.

Never estimate what a benchmark "would probably be".

## Distribution, not means

The single highest-value analytical move available, and the one most results packs skip.

### The core case

A mean of 6 on a 10-point scale can be:

- **Uniform** — nearly everyone answering 5, 6 or 7. One shared, mediocre experience. The
  action is systemic and applies to everybody.
- **Bimodal** — a cluster at 3 and a cluster at 9. Two organisations inside one company. The
  action is to identify who is in each group and what differs, and the company-level mean is
  actively misleading — it describes an experience almost nobody is having.
- **Skewed with a tail** — most people fine, a meaningful minority at the bottom. The action
  is targeted at the tail, and the mean will barely move even if the intervention works,
  which needs saying before the intervention is judged on the mean next year.

These demand different responses. Reporting only the mean makes them indistinguishable.

### What to look at

- **The proportion at the bottom of the scale**, not just the favourable percentage. A
  strongly negative answer costs the respondent something to give and carries more
  information than a neutral one.
- **The proportion neutral.** A large neutral middle often means the item was not understood,
  is not salient, or is being answered safely. Persistent high neutrality on a
  psychologically risky item — trust in leadership, ability to speak up — is itself a
  finding.
- **Whether the shape is bimodal**, and if so, whether the split maps onto a segment. Usually
  it does, and that segment is the finding.
- **Where an improved mean came from.** A shrinking bottom tail and a growing top are
  different results with the same mean movement. Shrinking the tail is usually the more
  valuable one and is often driven by leavers rather than by change — check against
  attrition before claiming it.
- **Variance by team, on the same item.** High variance between teams on an item means the
  item is locally determined — which tells you it belongs in the manager cascade, not in a
  central programme. Low variance means it is structural and central action is appropriate.
  This single test is the most reliable way to sort organisation-level actions from
  team-level ones.

## Segment cuts

Company-level engagement is rarely actionable, because the company is not where the
experience happens. Cut, in roughly this order of usual yield:

1. **Team and function.** The largest and most actionable variance almost always sits here.
2. **Manager population as its own segment.** Managers' own scores are a leading indicator
   for their teams'. A strained, unsupported manager layer shows up in everyone else's
   results next cycle. Cut managers separately, and cut by span of control if available.
3. **Tenure band** — under 6 months, 6–24 months, 2–5 years, 5+ years. The classic shape is
   high early, a dip somewhere in the first two years, then either recovery or a settled
   lower level. Where the dip is early and sharp, the problem is onboarding or a gap between
   what was promised at hire and what was found — which is a recruitment finding as much as
   an engagement one.
4. **Level or grade.** Senior populations usually score higher; a senior population scoring
   below the company is a serious finding and often precedes visible attrition.
5. **Location, and office versus remote versus hybrid.** Watch for a location's results being
   dominated by one function based there — the two dimensions confound each other constantly.
6. **New joiners versus the population present last cycle**, for the comparability test above.
7. **Demographic dimensions the organisation lawfully monitors**, under the guardrails in the
   SKILL.md and the group-size rules below.

### How to read a segment finding

- **Look for the segment that differs most from the company, not the one that scores lowest
  in absolute terms.** A function that scores 10 points below its own last cycle is a more
  urgent finding than one that has scored low and stable for three years — though the second
  is a finding too, of a different kind, and it usually means the organisation has stopped
  noticing.
- **Check whether the segment finding is really a manager finding.** A function with poor
  scores is often two teams inside it with poor scores. Always go one level down before
  drawing a conclusion about a function, where group sizes allow.
- **Check confounds before naming a cause.** Location cuts often carry function. Tenure cuts
  often carry level. Level cuts often carry function. Say which confound you could not rule
  out.
- **A segment that scores high is information too.** Where one team scores well on an item
  everyone else struggles with, that is a practice to find and spread, and it makes a much
  better action than a central programme.

## Minimum group size

Non-negotiable, and worth restating in the output every time a cell is suppressed.

**The rule.** Nothing is reported for a group below the organisation's stated minimum.
Commonly this sits somewhere in the 5–10 range. Ask what the organisation's policy is and
apply it. Where there is no policy, apply 5 as an absolute floor, and recommend they set a
policy at 5 or above before the next cycle.

**Why it is absolute.** Below the threshold, results deanonymise respondents. In a team of
four, "one person strongly disagreed that they are treated with respect" identifies a person
to three colleagues and a manager, who between them can usually work out which. It takes one
instance of someone being identified — or believing they were — for the confidentiality
commitment to be gone, and it does not come back. The next survey then measures who is
willing to be honest, which is not the thing anyone wanted to measure. This is why the rule
holds even when an executive asks for the cut, and even when the intent behind the request
is entirely benign.

**Where the rule is most often breached: cross-cuts.** Each dimension can clear the
threshold while their intersection does not. "Women in engineering at director level" or
"remote employees in the Berlin office with under a year's tenure" can be two people. Apply
the threshold to the reported cell, not to its parent dimensions.

**Suppression by subtraction.** Suppressing one cell is not enough if the total and the
other cells are published — the suppressed value is then recoverable by arithmetic. Suppress
enough neighbouring cells to prevent the derivation, or report the parent group only. State
that secondary suppression was applied, so the reason is visible rather than looking like an
inconsistent omission.

**Free-text is stricter.** A comment can identify its author regardless of group size, so
group size is a necessary condition for reporting free text, not a sufficient one. See
`free-text-analysis.md`.

**What to do instead of reporting a small group.** Roll up to the next level and say so;
report the parent group with a note that sub-groups could not be reported; or give the
manager qualitative guidance and a conversation structure rather than numbers. For a manager
whose team is below the threshold, the honest message is: "your team is too small for us to
report results without identifying people, so you get the [function] results and here is how
to run the conversation with your own team". Managers accept this readily when the reason is
given; they resent it when it looks like withholding.

## Driver identification

The goal is to find the items most connected to the outcome the organisation cares about,
because the lowest-scoring item is frequently not the most important one.

### First, name the outcome

Ask the user which outcome matters: intent to stay, discretionary effort, eNPS or
recommendation, or overall engagement if the instrument has a composite. Drivers are only
meaningful relative to a named outcome, and different outcomes produce genuinely different
priority lists — the items that predict staying are not always the items that predict effort.

### If the platform provides a driver analysis

Use it. Then establish and state three things, because they change how much weight it
carries: which outcome it was run against, whether it is a correlation or a regression, and
whether it was run on this organisation's data or on the vendor's benchmark population. A
driver ranking derived from the vendor's global data is a statement about employers in
general, not about this employer, and it should be labelled as such.

### If there is no driver analysis

The comparison-of-groups method works with most exports and is honest about being crude:

1. Split respondents by the outcome item — those answering unfavourably on intent to stay
   against those answering favourably.
2. For each other item, compare the two groups' scores.
3. The items with the largest gaps are the candidate drivers.

State the limits plainly: this is association, not causation; it cannot separate items that
travel together (pay and recognition, manager and clarity, workload and wellbeing tend to
move as bundles); and it inherits any bias in who responded. It is directionally useful and
usually enough to prioritise between five candidate actions, which is all that is being
asked of it.

Where the export does not permit even that — no item-level data per respondent — fall back
to judgement, and say so explicitly: identify the items that plausibly connect to the
outcome, mark them as inferred rather than measured, and note that a respondent-level export
would replace inference with evidence.

### Sense-check every driver finding against what is known

Drivers that contradict everything the organisation knows about itself are usually artefacts.
Check the candidate list against exit interview themes, attrition patterns by segment,
recruitment feedback and what managers are saying. Where they agree, confidence rises
sharply. Where they disagree, say so and treat it as a question rather than resolving it in
the deck.

## Impact against agency

The final filter before the action plan, and the one that keeps it honest. Place every
candidate item on two axes:

**Impact** — how strongly connected to the outcome, from the driver work above.

**Agency** — can this organisation materially change this within about two quarters?

|  | High agency | Low agency |
|---|---|---|
| **High impact** | The action plan. Everything here competes for the three to five slots. | The communication. Name it, give the real reason, say what will happen instead and when it might change. |
| **Low impact** | Cheap wins. Worth one slot if visible and fast — visible delivery rebuilds belief in the survey itself. | Note and move on. Do not build a workstream. |

The top-right cell is where honesty pays. A low score on office location under a long lease,
on pay during a freeze, on equity value, on a customer-driven shift pattern, or on a strategy
decision already made, is real and worth acknowledging — and putting it on the action plan
produces a workstream that quietly dies, which teaches employees that the plan is decorative.
Say it will not change, say why in real terms, and say what the organisation will do about
the consequence instead. That reads as respect. A vague commitment reads as management.

Two tests before an item goes in the top-left cell and onto the plan:

- **Is there a named person who can actually do this?** Not a function. If no individual has
  the authority, it is not high agency yet; the first action is getting a decision, and that
  is the thing to date.
- **Will an employee be able to tell it is happening within about six weeks?** If not, either
  find an earlier visible milestone or accept it is a slower structural item and pair it with
  something visible.

## Working with a PDF or a pasted summary

Common, and workable. What to do:

- **Extract what is there systematically**: response rate and base, item scores, any
  comparison, any segment tables, any theme summary. Put them into a table before
  interpreting, so gaps are visible.
- **Note what the pack does not show**, because vendor packs routinely omit the things that
  matter most: distributions, response rate by segment, and item-level segment cuts. The
  absence is not the analyst's fault and should be stated rather than worked around silently.
- **Do not reverse-engineer numbers the pack does not contain.** Favourability percentages
  cannot be turned back into distributions. Say the distribution is unavailable.
- **Ask whether the underlying export exists.** It nearly always does — the vendor has it
  even when the internal team has only seen the PDF — and requesting it is usually a short
  email rather than a project. This is often the single highest-value thing the user can do
  in the next hour, and worth saying in those terms.

## What to say about confidence

Give the pack a short, plain statement of what the analysis can and cannot support. It
protects the user in the room and it makes the strong findings stronger by contrast.

Cover: the response rate and its skew; whether year-on-year comparison is valid and why;
which segments were suppressed and why; whether drivers are measured or inferred; which
confounds could not be separated; and the one or two data items that would most change the
conclusions.

Then state the findings you are confident in without hedging them. Uniform hedging is the
same as no hedging — if every finding carries a caveat, the reader discounts all of them
equally and the genuinely uncertain ones get treated exactly like the solid ones.
