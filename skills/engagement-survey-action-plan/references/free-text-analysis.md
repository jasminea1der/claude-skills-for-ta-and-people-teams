# Free-text analysis

Comments are where the survey stops being a scorecard and starts explaining itself. They are
also the point at which the confidentiality promise made to respondents is most easily
broken, and where the most serious disclosures an organisation receives all year sometimes
arrive with no other route in.

Read this file in full before opening a comment file.

## The three rules

Everything below follows from these.

**1. Report at theme level, never at individual level.** The unit of analysis is the theme:
what a number of people said, how strongly, and what it points at. No finding in a results
document is about one person — not about one respondent, and not about one person who was
written about.

**2. Nothing published may identify a respondent.** Not by name, not by role, not by a
described event, not by a circumstance, and not by an accumulation of details that separately
identify nobody and together identify one person. Judge this against the reader who knows the
team, not against a stranger.

**3. Disclosures of harm go to a channel, not into a document.** Harassment, discrimination,
bullying, retaliation, safeguarding concerns and anything suggesting a risk to someone's
safety are routed privately to the user for referral to the proper internal channel. They do
not appear in a themes summary, a results deck or an action plan. See
[the escalation protocol](#the-escalation-protocol).

## Before you start: what you are allowed to produce

Establish two things before the first pass, because they change what the output can contain:

- **What respondents were told.** Whether comments were described as anonymous or
  confidential, whether they were told comments would be read by their manager, whether they
  were told comments might be quoted, and what minimum group size was promised. That promise
  governs this analysis regardless of what anyone asks for afterwards. If the survey said
  managers would never see raw comments, then raw comments do not go to managers even when a
  manager asks well and means no harm.
- **Who will see each output.** A themes summary for the exec team, a manager pack, and an
  all-company message have different exposure. A quote that is safe in one may identify
  someone in another — an anonymous comment about a team's workload is anonymous in a
  company-wide summary and identifying in a five-person team's pack.

Then say plainly which outputs you are producing and what each may contain.

## Theme extraction

A method that is reproducible and does not just surface whatever the reader already believed.

**1. Read a sample before coding anything.** Take a broad sample across segments and read it
without categorising. The goal is to hear the language people actually used — the words they
choose for a problem are usually better than the words a framework would supply, and using
their language in the write-up is what makes employees recognise themselves in it.

**2. Code inductively first, then map to the survey items.** Let the themes come from the
comments rather than sorting comments into the survey's existing categories. Do the mapping
afterwards. This matters because the most valuable comment themes are frequently the ones
the questionnaire did not ask about — that is precisely why people wrote them in.

**3. Keep themes at a usable grain.** "Communication" is too broad to act on and will absorb
half the file. "Nobody knows what the strategy means for their team", "decisions get made and
then reversed without explanation" and "we hear about changes from customers first" are three
different problems with three different owners. Split until each theme implies a distinct
action, then stop.

**4. Distinguish the problem from the proposed solution.** Comments often arrive as
solutions — "we need a new tool", "bring back the offsite". Code the underlying problem as
the theme and keep the proposed solutions as a separate list. Solutions are useful input to
the action plan but a poor basis for a theme, because ten people can propose ten different
solutions to the same problem and the theme then looks like ten small issues.

**5. Code the positive too.** Comment analysis skews negative because people write in to
complain, and a themes list that is entirely negative is both inaccurate and easy for an exec
team to dismiss. Report what people said was good, at the same grain — and pay attention to
positives that are specific and local, because they name the practices worth spreading.

**6. Segment the themes.** A theme carried entirely by one function or one tenure band is a
different finding from one distributed across the company, and it usually has a different
owner. Apply the same minimum group size to a theme-by-segment count as to a score.

**7. Look for what is not said.** Where an obvious topic is absent — no comments at all about
leadership, or a segment that wrote nothing — that is worth noting as a question. Silence can
mean satisfaction, or that people did not believe it was safe to write. Do not resolve it in
the pack; raise it.

**8. Check the coding for drift.** Re-read a sample against the finished theme list at the
end. Themes drift as they are built, and early comments frequently end up in categories that
have moved. Where a comment could sit in two themes, say so rather than forcing it.

## Frequency and intensity

Report both. They answer different questions and either alone misleads.

**Frequency** — how many comments carry the theme, as a count and as a proportion of comments
made (not of the population; most people do not comment). Frequency tells you how widespread
something is.

**Intensity** — how strongly it is expressed. Signals: length of the comment, whether the
writer describes a consequence rather than a preference, whether it is about leaving,
whether the language is emotional rather than descriptive, whether someone took the risk of
writing something specific, and whether the person raised it despite the question asking
about something else.

Report them together, in plain language: "workload appeared in [n] comments — moderate
frequency — but with the highest intensity in the file: several described sustained hours and
their effect on health, and it is the theme most often connected to considering leaving."

Why both matter:

- **High frequency, low intensity** is often an irritant: real, widely felt, cheap to fix, and
  fixing it visibly buys credibility out of proportion to its importance.
- **Low frequency, high intensity** is where the serious findings hide. Five comments
  describing something with force may matter more than fifty mild ones about parking — and a
  low-frequency high-intensity theme concentrated in one segment is a priority signal, not a
  rounding error.
- **High on both** is the top of the plan.

Be careful with proportions on small comment counts, and never let intensity language imply a
count. Write "several comments" or "a small number of comments, strongly expressed" rather
than converting a handful into a percentage that sounds like a measurement.

## Anonymisation and quote selection

Quotes make a themes section land — a single sentence in an employee's own words does more
than a paragraph of summary. They are also the single most common route by which a
confidentiality promise gets broken. Select them under discipline.

### The test

Before using any quote, ask: **could a colleague who knows this team work out who wrote
this?** Not a stranger — a colleague. If the answer is yes, or maybe, paraphrase it or drop
it. There is always another way to make the point and there is not always another way to
repair the trust.

### Never usable, at any level of the organisation

- Anything naming or describing a role held by one person: "as the only [role] in [team]",
  "our new [job title]", "the person who runs [function]".
- Anything referencing a specific identifiable event: a named project failure, a particular
  meeting, a specific incident, a departure, a promotion decision, an individual's return
  from leave.
- Anything referring to the writer's own health, disability, family circumstances,
  pregnancy, caring responsibilities, religion, sexual orientation, gender identity, race,
  immigration status, union involvement or finances — even where it makes the point
  powerfully, and even where the writer clearly volunteered it.
- Anything naming or clearly identifying another individual, including a manager. See the
  next section.
- Anything from a segment below the minimum group size, and anything from a small team where
  the quote references team-specific detail.
- Distinctive writing: an unusual turn of phrase, a language pattern, a known personal
  bugbear, or a style colleagues would recognise. This one is easy to miss and easy to be
  wrong about — when in doubt, paraphrase.

### Prefer paraphrase, and label it

The safest and often the clearest approach is a short paraphrase presented as a paraphrase:

> Several comments described the same pattern: priorities are set at the start of the
> quarter, changed within weeks, and the change is never explained.

That carries the finding with none of the exposure. Where you use a genuine quote, mark it as
a verbatim quote and keep it short — a single sentence, stripped of any identifying clause.
Do not silently edit a quote into safety and still present it as verbatim; either quote it or
paraphrase it, and say which.

### Redaction that actually works

Replacing a name with `[manager]` is not anonymisation when the surrounding detail identifies
the person anyway. If a quote needs more than one redaction to be safe, it is not a quote,
it is a paraphrase — treat it as one. Never publish a comment file with names replaced and
everything else intact; the pattern of detail is usually enough.

### Volume discipline

Two or three quotes per theme, maximum. A long quote list reads as evidence but functions as
a raw comment dump, and the more comments are published the higher the chance one of them
identifies someone. Choose the ones that add something the summary cannot say.

## Named individuals in comments

Every survey with free text produces comments about specific people — usually managers,
occasionally executives, sometimes peers.

**Do not name any individual who is complained about, in any analysis document.** Not in the
themes summary, not in the exec pack, not in the appendix, not in an anonymised form that a
reader can decode. The reasons are practical as much as principled: the comment is untested
allegation, the person has had no opportunity to respond, and a named complaint circulating in
a results document is both unfair to them and, in several jurisdictions, a data protection
problem in its own right — the comment is their personal data too.

What to do instead:

- **Report the behaviour as a theme without the person**: "comments in [function] described
  managers making decisions without consultation" rather than naming who.
- **Where multiple comments concern the same individual**, that is a pattern and it goes to
  the user privately, for handling through employee relations or the individual's own
  manager — not into the pack. Say what you observed factually, without characterising the
  person, and stop there.
- **Where a comment alleges specific misconduct**, use the escalation protocol below.

The distinction that matters: the survey can legitimately tell an organisation that a
management practice is a problem in a function. It is not a mechanism for finding a specific
person guilty of something, and using it as one is unfair, unsound, and quickly learned by
the population, who will then write differently or not at all.

## Health, circumstances and special-category content

Comments frequently contain health information, mental health disclosures, descriptions of
family and caring circumstances, and references to disability, religion, race, sexual
orientation or union membership. Most of it is volunteered in the course of making a point
about work.

How to handle it:

- **Report at theme level only.** "A number of comments connected workload to effects on
  health" is a legitimate and useful finding. Anything more specific is not.
- **No quotes.** Even where a health-related comment cannot identify the writer on its face,
  the combination of a health disclosure and any other detail usually can, and the writer
  disclosed it to explain a workload problem, not to have it printed.
- **Do not infer.** Do not characterise anyone's health, mental health, family situation or
  protected characteristics from what they wrote, and do not build a segment out of inferred
  characteristics. A comment mentioning exhaustion is not a diagnosis and must not be
  reported as one.
- **Where an individual appears to be describing a personal difficulty and may need
  support**, that is a signal to make sure support routes are well communicated to everyone —
  not to identify them. If the comment suggests risk of harm, use the escalation protocol.
- **Keep it out of the raw file that circulates.** Where a comment export is shared with
  anyone beyond the analyst, strip special-category content first, or do not share the raw
  export at all — which is the better default.

## The escalation protocol

Some comments are not analysis material. They are disclosures, and the survey happened to be
the route that felt safe enough to use.

### What triggers it

A comment that appears to describe or allege:

- Harassment, sexual harassment, or bullying.
- Discrimination on any protected ground.
- Retaliation or victimisation for raising something.
- Abuse of power, coercion, or an abusive relationship at work.
- A safeguarding concern, or a concern about a vulnerable person.
- Physical danger, an unsafe working practice, or a serious health and safety failure.
- Fraud, corruption, regulatory breach, or another whistleblowing matter.
- Anything suggesting risk of self-harm or of harm to others.

Err toward flagging. A false positive costs the user two minutes of reading. A missed
disclosure can be extremely serious for a person and, separately, is the kind of failure that
ends the organisation's credibility on speak-up entirely.

### What to do

1. **Do not put it in the analysis.** Not as a theme, not as an example, not as a quote, not
   in an appendix, not in the file that goes to the exec team. Summarising a disclosure of
   harm as a theme buries it among workload complaints; including the detail in a circulating
   document exposes the person who wrote it. Both are worse than doing nothing with it.
2. **Raise it with the user directly and privately, in the conversation**, separately from
   the analysis output, as soon as it is found — not at the end of the project. Say that a
   comment appears to disclose something requiring a different route, give only what the user
   needs to route it, and note where it sits in the source file so it can be retrieved.
3. **Name the likely channel** rather than deciding the matter: the speak-up or whistleblowing
   route, employee relations, the Head of People, legal, safeguarding, or health and safety.
   Note that several of these have defined timescales and their own confidentiality rules,
   and that whistleblowing disclosures attract legal protections in many jurisdictions that
   are lost or compromised if the report is handled informally.
4. **Do not investigate.** Do not attempt to identify the author, do not try to match the
   comment to a known situation, do not contact anyone, and do not draw a conclusion about
   whether the allegation is true. The analysis role ends at routing.
5. **Recommend legal involvement where the comment alleges discrimination, harassment or a
   regulatory breach.** These carry legal exposure that begins the moment the organisation is
   on notice, and how the organisation responds from that point is frequently more
   consequential than the underlying allegation.
6. **Record that it was routed, minimally.** Who it went to and when — not the content. This
   matters if anyone later asks whether the disclosure was acted on, and the answer needs to
   be yes with a date.

### The pattern case

Where several comments in one segment describe the same kind of mistreatment, that is a
pattern, and it is both an escalation and a finding. Route the pattern through the proper
channel and, in the analysis, report only that a serious concern in that area has been
raised through the appropriate route — no detail, no quotes, no segment small enough to
identify people. Where the pattern is material, recommend that the investigation and any
analysis of it run at the direction of counsel; in several jurisdictions that attracts
privilege that a spreadsheet built in the People team does not.

### What the user needs to hear alongside it

Say plainly, once: the fact that someone used a survey comment box to raise this usually
means the normal routes did not feel available or did not feel safe. That is itself a
finding about the organisation, and it belongs in the action plan as "make the speak-up
route work" — which is a legitimate, ownable action and does not require disclosing anything
about the comment that prompted it.

## Reporting themes to managers

Manager packs are where free-text confidentiality most often fails, because the group is
small and the manager knows everyone in it.

- **Apply the minimum group size to comments as a floor, not a ceiling.** A team of eight
  clears a threshold of five and can still be a team where one comment identifies its author
  immediately.
- **Give managers themes, not comments**, unless the survey explicitly told respondents their
  manager would see raw comments. Where the survey did say so, still strip anything that
  identifies an individual.
- **Never pass on a comment about the manager themselves in a form they could attribute.** If
  a manager can work out who criticised them, the criticism will not be made next year — and
  the manager knowing this is why they ask.
- **Give the manager the rule and the reason**, so it does not read as the People team
  withholding: their team is small, the promise made to respondents was specific, and the
  survey only keeps working if the promise holds. Managers almost always accept this when the
  reason is given.
- **Tell every manager, in the same words, not to ask who said what.** It is the single most
  damaging thing a manager can do with survey results — it converts one uncomfortable set of
  scores into a permanent lesson about what is safe to write down.

## What to write in the pack

For each theme:

- **A name in employees' own language**, not in HR categories.
- **Frequency** — count and proportion of comments, stated as a count where the number is
  small.
- **Intensity** — described, with the signals that justify the description.
- **Segment concentration**, where group sizes permit — or a note that it is distributed.
- **A short paraphrase or one carefully-selected quote** that passes the colleague test.
- **What it points at** — the underlying problem, not the proposed solution.
- **Whether it is corroborated by the scores.** A theme that matches a low-scoring item is on
  firmer ground; a theme with no corresponding item is often the most valuable thing in the
  file, because it names something the questionnaire never asked about.

And for the section as a whole:

- **How many comments there were**, and what proportion of respondents wrote one. Low comment
  volume is itself a signal about whether people believe writing is safe or worthwhile.
- **A plain statement of the anonymisation rules applied**, so readers understand why quotes
  are sparse and why some segments are absent. Stating the rule makes the analysis look
  disciplined rather than thin, and it reassures the population, which is the point.
- **Nothing that has been escalated.** Escalated material is absent from the pack, and it is
  the user's decision — not the document's — whether and how its existence is acknowledged.
