## 1. Why you are getting this

Your team told us something. The survey does not change anything on its own — the
conversation you have with your team, and the one or two things you change afterwards, is
the part that does.

Most of what shapes how people feel about work is local: how the team is run, how workload
lands, whether people know what good looks like, whether their contribution is noticed.
Central actions address the structural things. Everything else is yours, and it is the larger
half.

## 3. If your team is too small to report

Your team is below the minimum reporting group size of [n], so you are receiving the
[parent group] results instead of your own.

This is not us withholding your data. Below that threshold, results identify individuals —
in a team of four, "one person disagreed" is a person three colleagues can name. We told
everyone their answers would be confidential and that promise is the only reason the survey
tells us anything true. It holds even when the manager asking is entirely well-intentioned.

You can say exactly that to your team, and it is worth saying, because they will wonder.

You still hold the conversation. Use the [parent group] results as the starting point and
ask your team what of it is true for them. In a small team this conversation is usually
better than the numbers anyway.

## 5. The team conversation

Sixty to ninety minutes. No slides needed.

**1. Thank them, and mean it.** [x]% of the team responded. Say what the response rate was.

**2. Share the actual results, including the uncomfortable ones.** Put the real numbers up.
Editing them is the fastest way to lose the room — they know roughly what they said.

**3. Say what you notice, briefly.** Two or three observations. Not conclusions, and not
defences.

**4. Ask what is behind it — then stop talking.** The single most useful thing you do in this
meeting is leave a silence long enough for someone to fill. Useful questions:

- What is behind this score? What were people thinking about when they answered it?
- What did I miss in how I read this?
- What is it like on a bad week here?
- What do we do that gets in the way of the actual work?

**5. Ask the priority question:** if we could change one thing about how this team works,
what would make the biggest difference?

**6. Commit to one or two things you can actually control.** Say them out loud, with a date.
Do not commit to anything that depends on a decision you do not own.

**7. Say what you will take upward**, to whom, and when you will report back. Then actually
report back — including if the answer is no.

**8. Close by saying when you will return to this.** Put the date in the calendar in the room.

## 6. Things not to do

- **Do not ask who said what.** This is the single most damaging thing a manager can do with
  survey results. Ask it once — even lightly, even as a joke — and the team learns that
  writing something down is risky. That lesson lasts for years and it cannot be undone by
  reassurance.
- **Do not try to work out who wrote a comment**, and do not speculate with anyone about it.
- **Do not defend the scores.** The results are how people experienced the year. You can
  disagree privately; arguing in the room converts a difficult result into a worse one.
- **Do not explain the results away in the meeting.** Context is fair and often true — a
  reorganisation, a hard quarter, a departure. Say it once, briefly, and then get back to
  what you are going to do.
- **Do not promise what you cannot deliver.** An unkept commitment made in this meeting is
  worse than the original low score, because it proves the process is decorative.
- **Do not skip the meeting.** A team that gets no conversation concludes the results were
  bad and are being hidden. They are usually right about the first part.

## 7. If your own scores are poor

Read this section properly if this applies to you. It is the part of the cascade that most
often goes wrong.

**Your scores are information about your team's experience, not a verdict on you.** A low
score for a manager who has just delivered a restructure, absorbed two teams, inherited a
team mid-year, or held a group together through a hard period is very often a measure of the
situation rather than the person. That is worth saying out loud to yourself before you read
the numbers a second time.

**You still hold the conversation, and you hold it on time.** A manager who quietly does not
cascade poor results confirms everything the results said. The team already knows roughly
what they wrote. The silence is louder than the scores.

**How to open it when the scores are bad:**

> These are not good, and I have read them properly. I am not going to explain them away and
> I am not going to ask who said what. I want to understand what is behind them, and then I
> want to pick one or two things I can actually change. Tell me what I am missing.

Then be quiet. It will be uncomfortable. Sitting in that discomfort without defending
yourself is the entire intervention — teams give a manager enormous credit for it, and it is
usually the point at which the following year's scores start moving.

**Do not do it alone.** Your manager or your People partner will help you prepare, and will
sit in if that would help. Ask before the meeting rather than after it goes badly — support
is available now, and asking for it is read as good judgement, not as weakness.

**What happens next.** Poor results are not a performance process. They are the starting
point of a conversation about what support you need — which might be workload relief in your
team, a clearer remit, help with a specific situation, or coaching. Where results point to
something more serious than a hard year — a pattern across cycles, or comments describing
behaviour rather than circumstances — your own manager will talk to you about it separately
and directly. That conversation happens face to face, not through a survey report, and you
will not learn about it from a document.
