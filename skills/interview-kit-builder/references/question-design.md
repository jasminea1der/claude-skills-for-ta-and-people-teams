# Question design and rating anchors

How to write behavioural questions that produce comparable evidence, how to probe past a
rehearsed answer, how to spot the candidate describing someone else's work, and how to write
rating anchors that two different interviewers apply the same way.

## The unit of a good question

A question is working if the answers it produces can be **compared across candidates** and
**scored against a written standard**. That is the whole test.

Two questions fail it constantly:

- Questions where the interesting variation is in how the candidate speaks rather than what
  they did. These measure presentation.
- Questions so broad that two candidates answer entirely different things. "Tell me about
  your leadership style" produces a philosophy from one person and an anecdote from another,
  and there is no defensible way to rank them.

Design against both by anchoring the question to **one specific past situation** and by
knowing, before you ask it, what a strong answer contains.

---

## Question forms, ranked

**1. Behavioural — past specific.** "Tell me about a time you had to ship something before it
was ready." Best evidence available in a conversation. Past behaviour under comparable
conditions is the closest thing to observing the work.

**2. Work sample.** Not a question at all, and better than any of these when the role has an
observable output. See the work sample section of SKILL.md.

**3. Situational — realistic and role-specific.** "You're three weeks into the quarter and
engineering tells you the migration will take twice as long. Walk me through what you do."
Useful as a *supplement* for a capability the candidate has genuinely never had the chance to
exercise — a first-time manager, a career changer, a new domain. Weaker than behavioural,
because articulating a good answer is easier than producing one. Never let situational
questions carry a competency on their own.

**4. Technical / domain probing.** "Walk me through how you'd approach X, and where it breaks."
Strong for depth, weak for everything else.

**5. Self-assessment.** "How would you rate your own commercial judgement?" Measures
self-presentation and confidence calibration. Rarely worth a slot.

**6. Brainteasers, riddles, trick questions.** Measure the ability to do brainteasers. Cut.

**7. "What's your greatest weakness?"** Everyone has prepared for it, so it discriminates
between candidates who have interview-prepped and candidates who have not. If you want
self-awareness, ask about a specific failure and what they changed afterwards.

## Writing the primary question

Construction rules, each with the reason:

- **Open with the situation, not the trait.** "Tell me about a time a project you owned went
  badly off track" rather than "tell me about your resilience". Naming the trait tells the
  candidate what to perform.
- **One situation, not a survey.** "Tell me about a difficult stakeholder" beats "tell me
  about how you manage stakeholders", which invites a summary of everything and evidence of
  nothing.
- **Constrain the difficulty.** "A time you disagreed with your manager on something that
  mattered" is better than "a time you disagreed with someone", because the second lets the
  candidate pick a trivial example. Put the difficulty in the question so they cannot dodge it.
- **Set the recency window when level matters.** "In the last two years" prevents a candidate
  from reaching back eight years to the one time they operated at the level you need.
- **Do not signal the answer.** "How did you make sure everyone was aligned before you
  launched?" tells them alignment is the right answer. Ask "how did you decide it was ready
  to launch?" and see whether alignment appears on its own. Unprompted appearance is far
  stronger evidence than a prompted one.
- **Ask the same primary question of every candidate.** Comparison needs a common baseline.
  Probes vary with the answer; the primary does not.

**Bank a second question per competency** for the interviewer whose first question hits a
candidate with no relevant example, and for repeat interviews at the same stage.

## Probing

Probing is where structured interviewing produces its advantage. The first answer is the
prepared one; the evidence is in the second and third layer.

**The four probes that do most of the work:**

1. **Specificity.** "What exactly did *you* do in that?" — separates the actor from the
   audience. Ask it early, before the story has momentum.
2. **The alternative.** "What else did you consider, and why did you reject it?" — a
   real decision has a discarded option. A retrofitted one does not.
3. **The cost.** "What did that trade-off cost you? Who was unhappy?" — every real decision
   has a bill. Candidates who cannot name the cost were not the ones who paid it.
4. **The correction.** "What would you do differently?" — the answer separates people who
   have reflected from people who have narrated. "Nothing" is a weak answer even when the
   outcome was good.

**Two more for depth:**

- **The counterfactual.** "What would have had to be true for the other option to be right?"
  Hard to answer without actually having reasoned about it.
- **The named other.** "Who disagreed with you? What was their case, put as they'd put it?"
  A fair account of the opposing case is strong evidence of genuine engagement; a caricature
  is evidence of none.

**Probing technique:**

- **Follow the specific, not the abstract.** When a candidate offers both a principle and an
  instance, always chase the instance.
- **Use silence.** Three seconds after an answer ends usually produces the more useful half.
  Untrained interviewers fill the gap and lose the evidence.
- **Go three layers on one story rather than one layer on three.** Breadth is comfortable and
  produces nothing. The evidence lives at depth.
- **Probe the good answers too.** Probing only the answers you doubt turns the interview into
  a test of your first impression.
- **Do not argue.** You are collecting evidence, not winning. If they say something you think
  is wrong, ask them to explain the reasoning — that is more informative than the correction.

## Rehearsed answers

Rehearsal is not dishonesty and preparation is not a negative signal. The problem is that a
rehearsed answer is optimised for narrative, so it hides the parts you need.

**How to recognise one:** unusually fluent relative to the rest of the conversation; clean
three-act structure; numbers that arrive pre-rounded; the answer fits the question
approximately rather than exactly, because it was prepared for a nearby question.

**How to get past it:**

- Ask for the **second** example. The first is prepared; the second rarely is.
- Ask about a part of the story the narrative skips — the middle, the six weeks where nothing
  worked, the meeting where it was nearly cancelled.
- Ask for a **failure inside the success**. Every real project has one.
- Change the axis: if they told you what they did, ask what someone else in the story did,
  and why that person behaved that way.
- Ask about timing. "When did you first know this was going wrong, and how long before you
  acted?" Prepared stories are weak on chronology.

## Secondhand answers

The most common false positive across every competency: the candidate describing work they
were near rather than work they did. It is usually not deception — team-fluent language is
normal, and the boundary erodes honestly.

**Signals:**

- Persistent "we" with no "I", especially around decisions.
- Detail is rich on outcomes and thin on mechanism — they know what happened, not how it
  was decided.
- Cannot answer why an alternative was rejected. Participants remember the debate; observers
  remember the result.
- Depth collapses at exactly one layer down, consistently, across otherwise different stories.
- Timeline is vague. People who did the work remember roughly how long the awful part lasted.

**The separating probes:**

- *"What was your specific contribution? What would have been different if you'd been on
  leave that month?"*
- *"Who made the final call on that?"* — asked neutrally. A confident "I did" or a clear
  "my director did, and my part was X" are both fine answers. A blurred one is the signal.
- *"Walk me through the part you personally built/wrote/negotiated."*

Ask these of every candidate, not only the ones you suspect. Asking selectively is how bias
enters a probe set.

## Evidence standards

For each competency, write what strong and weak evidence look like **before** the interviews,
in observable terms an interviewer can check against while listening. Two rules:

- **Describe behaviour, not impression.** "Named the two options rejected and what each would
  have cost" is checkable. "Showed good judgement" is a score dressed as evidence.
- **Write the weak side properly.** Most kits write a good strong-evidence list and then
  "weak = the opposite", which is useless in the room. Weak evidence has its own shape:
  vagueness at a specific layer, absence of a cost, outcome-only reasoning.

## Rating anchor design

**Use 1-4 with written anchors. Do not use an unanchored 1-5.**

The two reasons, both worth stating to a hiring manager who objects:

- **The midpoint problem.** Given a 3, a large share of interviewers use it — it is the
  costless option when the evidence is mixed or when they would rather not disagree with the
  hiring manager. A midpoint score carries no information, and the decision it defers gets
  made in the debrief by whoever speaks first and loudest. Four points require a side.
- **The anchor problem.** A scale with no anchors is a personality test for the interviewer.
  One person's 4 is another's 2, the average is meaningless, and nobody can tell whether the
  disagreement is about the candidate or about the scale. Anchors move the scale from opinion
  to observation.

**The shape of a good anchor set** (write these per competency, not once for the kit):

| Score | Means | Written as |
|---|---|---|
| **4** | Exceeds the bar. Evidence at a level above what the role requires. | The specific thing a candidate above this level does. |
| **3** | Meets the bar. Would succeed at this competency in this role. | The evidence that constitutes a yes. |
| **2** | Below the bar. Some relevant evidence, not at the required level or scope. | What the near-miss looks like — usually right behaviour, wrong scale. |
| **1** | No evidence, or evidence of the opposite. | What the clear no looks like. |

**Writing rules:**

- **Anchor to observable evidence**, not to quality adjectives. Not "3 = good stakeholder
  skills" but "3 = brought a peer function along on something that cost them, can name the
  other party's interests and what was conceded."
- **Anchor to the level you are hiring at.** A 3 for a lead is a 4 for a senior. Write the
  anchors from the level differentiators in `competency-library.md`, or the panel will score
  against a generic idea of "good" and hire a level down.
- **4 is not a unicorn.** If nobody can score a 4, the scale is a 3-point scale. Write 4 as a
  reachable description of someone genuinely above the bar.
- **1 must be distinguishable from "did not assess".** Give interviewers an explicit
  not-assessed option. Forcing a score on something that never came up manufactures data.

**Separate the recommendation from the scores.** Ask for hire / no hire / lean, with one
sentence of reason, as its own field. Scores do not sum to a decision — a candidate can be
3s across the board and still be a no, and a 2 on a competency the role lives on outweighs
4s elsewhere. Making the recommendation explicit forces the interviewer to state the weighting
they are applying instead of hiding it in an average.

**Calibrate before you run the loop.** Take one recent hire and one recent decline everyone
knows, and have the panel score them against the new anchors. Where they disagree, the anchor
is ambiguous. Fifteen minutes here saves the first three debriefs.

## Note-taking standard

Put this in the kit, because it is the difference between a scorecard that holds up and one
that says "great culture add".

- **Write what was said, during the answer.** Specifics: decisions, numbers, names of
  trade-offs, short quotes. Memory reconstructs toward the impression you formed in the first
  five minutes.
- **Separate evidence from interpretation.** Two columns or two blocks. Evidence: "said they
  cut the scope by half and shipped in six weeks; couldn't say who signed off." Interpretation:
  "may have been executing someone else's call."
- **Note what was missing.** An absent answer is evidence. "Asked twice about cost; both times
  moved to outcomes" belongs in the scorecard.
- **Score immediately after, before your next meeting.** Delay converts evidence into a
  general impression.

## Debrief mechanics

The best-designed kit is undone by a badly run debrief.

- **Every scorecard submitted before the debrief opens.** Non-negotiable. One senior person
  saying "I loved them" before the others have committed collapses four independent reads
  into one. Independence is the entire value of running multiple interviewers.
- **Speak in reverse seniority.** Most junior interviewer first, hiring manager last, for the
  same reason.
- **Discuss evidence, not conclusions.** "What did they say that led you to a 2?" The
  disagreements are the most valuable part of the debrief; a debrief where everyone agrees
  immediately has usually told you nothing you did not know after the first stage.
- **A split is information, not a problem to be smoothed.** Locate it: is it different
  evidence (they showed one interviewer something they did not show another), or the same
  evidence read differently (an anchor problem)? Different evidence often warrants a targeted
  follow-up on the one competency in question. The same evidence read differently is a
  calibration issue to fix in the kit.
- **The decision-maker is named in advance.** Consensus-by-exhaustion selects for
  inoffensiveness. Say who decides and what the others' scores are for.

## Questions to cut

- **"Why do you want to work here?"** Tests preparation, and rewards flattery. If you want
  motivation, ask what they are looking for in their next role and what would make them
  leave one.
- **"Where do you see yourself in five years?"** Answers are performances of ambition.
- **"Tell me about yourself."** Fine as a warm-up, worthless as a scored item. Do not let it
  eat ten minutes.
- **Stress or trick questions.** Measure how someone handles a contrived hostility they will
  never meet again, and cost you good candidates who conclude the company is unpleasant.
- **Anything about family, health, age, nationality, religion or personal circumstances.**
  See the legal section of SKILL.md for the compliant version of each underlying concern.
- **Anything you would not ask every candidate for this role.** Selective questioning
  destroys comparability and is the mechanism by which most interview bias operates.
