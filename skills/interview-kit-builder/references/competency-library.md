# Competency library

Role-agnostic competency definitions with level differentiators. Use this to **select** a
set of 4-6 competencies and to **calibrate the level** you are hiring at, then rewrite every
definition in the language of the specific role. A generic definition produces generic
questions and generic questions produce answers you cannot compare.

## How to use this library

Each entry gives you:

- **Definition** — the generic version. Rewrite it for the role.
- **What it predicts** — why it earns a slot. If you cannot say what a competency predicts
  about performance in this role, cut it.
- **Level differentiators** — what good looks like at IC, senior, lead and executive. This is
  the part most kits are missing, and it is why loops hire a level down: the panel assesses
  whether the candidate is good, not whether they have operated at the scope required.
- **Strong signal / weak signal** — observable evidence, not impressions.
- **The false positive** — the candidate who presents brilliantly on this and has not done
  the thing, plus the probe that separates them.

Rewriting rule: replace every abstract noun with something from the role. "Manages
stakeholders" becomes "gets a sceptical finance team and two engineering leads to agree a
sequencing they each dislike parts of." The second version writes its own questions.

## The level ladder

The four levels below are used consistently across every competency. Map them to your own
framework — the labels matter less than the scope distinction.

| Level | Scope of the problem owned | Typical marker |
|---|---|---|
| **IC** | A defined piece of work with a known shape. Others set the direction. | Delivers well against a brief. |
| **Senior** | A problem area with an unclear shape. Sets their own direction inside a set goal. | Others' work improves because of how they work. |
| **Lead** | A system, team or domain. Accountable for outcomes produced through others. | Owns the definition of what good is, not just the execution. |
| **Executive** | A function or a business line, across a multi-year horizon, with resource trade-offs against other functions. | Decides what the organisation will not do. |

Two calibration notes worth carrying into any kit:

- **Title inflation is real and asymmetric.** A "Head of" at a 40-person company and a "Head
  of" at a 4,000-person company have almost nothing in common. Assess scope through what
  they decided, what they controlled, and who they had to convince — never through the title.
- **The level is in the ambiguity, not the difficulty.** Senior people are not distinguished
  by harder problems; they are distinguished by problems that arrived without a definition.

## Judgement

**Definition.** Makes good decisions with incomplete information, and can explain why they
were good decisions at the time rather than in hindsight.

**What it predicts.** The proportion of a role's decisions that nobody will review. It is the
highest-leverage competency for any role where the person will be trusted to act alone, and
the hardest to assess from a CV.

**Level differentiators**

- **IC** — recognises when a decision is above their line and escalates with a recommendation
  rather than a question. Chooses sensibly between options they were given.
- **Senior** — generates the options rather than choosing among them. Can name the trade-off
  they accepted and what it cost.
- **Lead** — decides with a horizon: distinguishes decisions that are cheap to reverse from
  the ones that are not, and spends proportionate time accordingly. Sets the decision rights
  for others.
- **Executive** — makes the calls where every option is bad, holds the line under pressure
  from people who outrank them, and changes course publicly when the evidence turns.

**Strong signal.** Reconstructs the state of information at the time. Names options they
rejected and why. Distinguishes a bad outcome from a bad decision — and can give an example
of each. Says what they would need to see to change their mind.

**Weak signal.** Every decision described was correct. Reasoning is reconstructed from the
outcome ("we shipped it and it worked"). Cannot name an alternative that was seriously
considered. Escalates everything, or escalates nothing.

**False positive.** The articulate narrator. Tells decision stories with a clean arc because
they have told them before, but the arc is retrofitted to the result. Probe: *"What did you
believe at the time that turned out to be wrong?"* and *"What would have had to be true for
the other option to be right?"* A retrofitted story has no answer to the second question.

## Ownership

**Definition.** Treats the outcome as theirs past the point where their formal
responsibility ends, and closes gaps nobody assigned them.

**What it predicts.** How much management overhead the person carries. High-ownership hires
reduce the number of things a manager must track; low-ownership hires are competent and
still expensive.

**Level differentiators**

- **IC** — finishes things. Raises blockers early with a proposed route round them rather
  than as a status update.
- **Senior** — owns the gap between their work and the next person's. Notices the thing
  falling between two teams and picks it up or gets it assigned.
- **Lead** — owns outcomes delivered by people they do not control, and does not use "that
  team didn't deliver" as an explanation.
- **Executive** — owns the failure publicly and the credit sparingly. Accountable for a
  result across a horizon long enough that they could plausibly blame their predecessor.

**Strong signal.** Uses "I" for decisions and mistakes and "we" for results, rather than the
reverse. Describes work they were not asked to do. Follows a story past the handover point
— they know what happened to the thing after it shipped.

**Weak signal.** Every failure has an external cause. Scope of the story ends exactly at the
job description. Does not know the outcome of their own project. Heavy passive voice around
the parts that went wrong.

**False positive.** The proximity claimant. Was on the team, in the room, or reporting to
the person who did it, and uses "we" throughout so the boundary is never visible. Probe:
*"What was your specific piece? What would have been different if you'd been on holiday
that month?"* Ownership survives that question; proximity does not.

## Technical depth

**Definition.** Genuine command of the craft the role requires — the ability to do the work
at the standard the role demands, and to tell good work from adequate work.

**What it predicts.** Whether they can actually do the job, and whether they will raise or
lower the standard of everyone around them. Also the most commonly over-assessed competency:
loops often spend three stages on depth and none on the thing the role fails on.

**Level differentiators**

- **IC** — competent in the core tools and methods. Knows the standard practice and follows it.
- **Senior** — knows why the standard practice is standard, and when to depart from it.
  Their work has a point of view.
- **Lead** — sets the standard for others. Makes the architecture, methodology or approach
  decisions the team will live inside for years. Depth is now mostly used to evaluate other
  people's work.
- **Executive** — depth sufficient to be un-bluffable by their own function and to represent
  it credibly outside. Not necessarily current in the tools.

**Strong signal.** Goes a layer deeper than asked without being prompted. Has opinions with
reasons attached, and can argue the opposing case fairly. Knows the failure modes of their
preferred approach. Says "I don't know" cleanly and then reasons from what they do know.

**Weak signal.** Vocabulary without mechanism — names the tools and frameworks but cannot
explain what they do or why they were chosen. Answers stay at the level of abstraction the
question was asked at. Depth collapses under the second follow-up.

**False positive.** The recent-context candidate. Fluent in the terminology of an
environment where the hard problems were solved before they arrived, so they describe
sophisticated systems they used but did not build or understand. Probe for a decision, not a
description: *"What in that setup would you have built differently, and what did the
existing design cost you?"*

**Note on depth for senior hires.** Do not skip it because someone is senior. Skipping it is
how organisations hire an executive who cannot tell whether their own function is doing good
work. Assess it at the level the role needs — evaluation of others' work rather than
personal execution.

## Stakeholder influence

**Definition.** Gets outcomes through people who do not report to them and are not obliged
to help, including when those people initially disagree.

**What it predicts.** Everything in a matrixed organisation. The most common cause of a
technically excellent hire failing in the first year, and the competency loops most often
leave to a vague "collaboration" question at the end of a call.

**Level differentiators**

- **IC** — works well across a team. Asks for what they need clearly and early.
- **Senior** — brings a peer function along on something that costs that function
  something. Handles a "no" without escalating immediately.
- **Lead** — builds the coalition before the meeting. Negotiates priority against other
  leaders with competing goals, and preserves the relationship for the next negotiation.
- **Executive** — moves a peer executive or a board. Manages upward and sideways
  simultaneously, and holds a position that is unpopular with people who can hurt them.

**Strong signal.** Can describe the other party's actual interests, in their terms, not as a
caricature. Changed their own position at some point in the story. Describes what they gave
up. Names the specific person and what mattered to that person.

**Weak signal.** Influence is described as communication ("I explained the data and they
came round"). Every disagreement resolved by escalation or by being right. The other party
appears in the story only as an obstacle.

**False positive.** The authority-borrower. Got the outcome, but the mechanism was a senior
sponsor or a mandate, and the story is told as persuasion. Probe: *"What would have happened
if [the sponsor] hadn't backed you? Who did you have to convince who could have said no and
made it stick?"*

## Commercial reasoning

**Definition.** Understands how the organisation makes money, and connects their own
decisions to it — including choosing not to do valuable work because something else is worth
more.

**What it predicts.** Whether the person will prioritise sensibly without being told what to
prioritise. Relevant far outside commercial functions: it is what separates a people leader
who is a business partner from one who is an internal service desk.

**Level differentiators**

- **IC** — knows how their work reaches a customer and what it costs.
- **Senior** — makes trade-offs with cost and value in view. Can justify their prioritisation
  in business terms rather than effort terms.
- **Lead** — owns a budget or a P&L line, or the resource allocation for a team. Kills work
  that is going nowhere. Can build the case for investment and the case against it.
- **Executive** — allocates capital and headcount against competing bets, defends the
  allocation to a board, and is measured on the result years later.

**Strong signal.** Uses numbers unprompted, and they are the right numbers — the ones that
drive the business, not vanity metrics. Describes work they stopped or declined. Understands
the cost side, not just the revenue side. Knows their previous employer's business model in
detail.

**Weak signal.** Success described purely in activity or delivery terms. No sense of what
anything cost. Cannot explain how their former employer made money. Every project was
worth doing.

**False positive.** The metrics reciter. Quotes impressive figures from a company deck
without a causal link to their own work. Probe: *"How much of that was you? What would that
number have been if your project hadn't happened, and how do you know?"*

## People leadership

**Definition.** Builds a team that performs better than the sum of its individuals, and
handles the hard parts — underperformance, disagreement, departures — without avoidance.

**What it predicts.** For any role with reports, the retention and output of everyone
underneath. The largest single multiplier in the kit, and the one where a false positive is
most expensive because the damage takes a year to surface.

**Level differentiators**

- **IC** — not applicable. Assess mentoring or informal leadership instead if it matters.
- **Senior** — mentors, onboards, raises the standard around them. May have led a project
  team without formal authority.
- **Lead** — manages individuals directly. Hires, develops, gives hard feedback, and has
  exited someone. Runs a functioning team process.
- **Executive** — manages managers. Builds the operating model rather than the team. Their
  succession bench is the evidence: who did they develop who now runs something.

**Strong signal.** Talks about specific people and what happened to them — promotions,
departures, the person who was struggling and what they did. Can describe feedback they gave
that was hard to give, including the words they used. Owns a hiring mistake. Has a stated
view on how they manage, and it is consistent with the examples.

**Weak signal.** Leadership described as philosophy without instances. Never had a
performance problem. Everyone on the team was great. Cannot name what they are working on in
their own management. Attrition described as the market's fault.

**False positive.** The pleasant manager. Well-liked, and has avoided every hard conversation
in their career; the team was fine because it was already fine. Probe: *"Tell me about
someone on your team who was not performing. What did you do, when did you first notice, and
what was the gap between noticing and acting?"* Avoidance shows up as a long gap and a
resolution that arrived from elsewhere — reorg, resignation, HR.

## Execution under ambiguity

**Definition.** Makes progress when the problem is undefined, the goal is contested, or the
information is missing — rather than waiting for clarity that is not coming.

**What it predicts.** Performance in early-stage companies, new functions, turnarounds, and
any role created for the first time. The most under-assessed competency when a company that
has become structured hires someone from a company that never was, or vice versa.

**Level differentiators**

- **IC** — starts without a complete brief. Asks the clarifying question and then proceeds
  rather than stalling.
- **Senior** — defines the problem well enough to be worked on. Sequences into steps and
  ships something small to learn from.
- **Lead** — creates structure others can operate inside: a plan, a metric, a process where
  none existed. Knows when to stop exploring and commit.
- **Executive** — sets direction with genuinely insufficient information and commits the
  organisation to it, then keeps everyone moving while the direction is still being tested.

**Strong signal.** Describes how they narrowed an unclear problem, not just what they
delivered. Made a decision to proceed on partial information and can say what they accepted
as unknown. Ran something small first. Comfortable saying "we got that wrong and changed it
in week six."

**Weak signal.** Every example had a clear brief and a defined success measure. Progress
required someone else to decide something. Describes ambiguity as an organisational failure
rather than a condition of the work.

**False positive.** The chaos veteran. Has genuinely worked in disorder, but survived it
rather than reduced it — motion without convergence. Probe: *"What did you put in place so
that the next person doing this had it easier? What was still ambiguous when you left?"*

## Communication

**Definition.** Makes complex things clear to the specific audience in front of them, and
adjusts when it is not landing.

**What it predicts.** Less on its own than teams assume — fluency is easy to confuse with
capability, and it is the competency most contaminated by accent, first language,
confidence and cultural style. Assess it when the role genuinely depends on it (anything
customer-facing, executive-facing, or writing-heavy), against a job-relevant standard.

**Level differentiators**

- **IC** — clear written updates. Flags problems in time to act on them.
- **Senior** — explains their domain to people outside it without either condescension or
  jargon. Writes documents others use.
- **Lead** — communicates decisions, including unpopular ones, so a team can act on them.
  Handles the room when the news is bad.
- **Executive** — sets narrative. Communicates to a board, a market, or a whole organisation,
  and can hold a message under hostile questioning.

**Strong signal.** Adjusts depth when you signal you have understood, without being asked.
Structures answers — signposts, then delivers. Checks whether it landed. Written work sample
is organised and lands the point in the first paragraph.

**Weak signal.** Same register regardless of audience. Length uncorrelated with importance.
Answers a different question than the one asked. Jargon without translation.

**False positive.** The polished presenter. Fluent, confident, well-rehearsed, and the
content thins on contact. Distinguish presentation from communication by asking for
something unrehearsed: an explanation of a decision they have not told before, or a written
exercise. **Guard against the inverse error too** — quiet, accented, or non-native speakers
are systematically underrated on this competency by unanchored scoring. Anchor it to a
job-relevant standard ("could a customer act on this explanation?") rather than to polish,
and do not score it at all in a stage where it is not the assigned competency.

## Choosing a set that works together

A good set of 4-6 covers three things:

1. **Can they do the work?** — technical depth, or the craft competency for this role.
2. **Have they done it at this scope?** — the level evidence. Usually judgement, ownership,
   or people leadership, assessed explicitly at the target level.
3. **Will they succeed in this environment specifically?** — derived from what makes people
   fail in this org. Usually stakeholder influence, execution under ambiguity, or commercial
   reasoning.

Test the set against three questions before locking it:

- If a candidate scored 4 on every one of these, would we definitely hire them? If not, the
  set is missing something.
- Could someone score well on all of these and still fail in year one? Whatever that failure
  is, it is your missing competency.
- Is any competency here impossible to distinguish from another in practice? Merge them.
  "Ownership" and "accountability" are the same competency with two names, and splitting them
  buys you nothing but a longer loop.

## Competencies to avoid

- **Culture fit.** Unanchored, and it rewards similarity to the interviewer. If the team
  needs specific behaviours, name them. If you cannot write an anchor for it, it is not
  assessable.
- **Passion / enthusiasm / drive.** Measures performed enthusiasm, and selects against people
  who are simply not demonstrative. If you mean "will they still be motivated in eighteen
  months", assess what they choose to work on and why they left previous roles.
- **Intelligence / raw horsepower.** Not directly observable in an interview, and the proxies
  used for it (quick speech, confident argument, prestigious institutions) carry heavy bias.
  Assess judgement and technical depth instead — that is what the label is reaching for.
- **Attention to detail** as a standalone competency. It is a component of the craft
  competency and better assessed in a work sample than a conversation.
- **Anything the panel cannot judge.** If nobody in the loop has done the thing, either add
  someone who has or accept you are not assessing it. A scored competency nobody can judge
  produces confident noise, which is worse than a known gap.
