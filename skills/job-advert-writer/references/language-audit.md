# Language audit

The checklist for inclusive and effective advert language. Each entry gives the pattern to
find, why it costs applications, and what to write instead.

Two framings, and use both — the commercial one is what gets the edit accepted in the
meeting, and the legal one is what makes it non-negotiable:

- **Commercial.** Every pattern below either reduces the number of applications or narrows
  the range of people who send one. Neither raises the quality bar; both shrink the pool you
  choose from, in a direction nobody deliberately chose.
- **Legal.** Job advertising is regulated at the point of publication in most jurisdictions,
  before any application arrives. Wording that indicates a preference on a protected
  characteristic creates exposure without a single candidate involved. This audit flags
  language likely to have that effect and proposes replacements; it does not certify
  compliance, and final wording for a regulated market needs local review.

## How to run the audit

Work through the advert twice.

**Pass one, on the original**, as part of the diagnosis. Quote the exact phrase, name the
pattern, name the cost, give the replacement. Three to six findings is usually right —
listing twenty makes the user defensive and the important ones get lost.

**Pass two, on your own draft**, before delivery. Rewrites reintroduce these patterns
constantly, particularly the superlatives and the passive voice, because they are the
ambient register of recruitment writing.

Do not run this as a word-substitution exercise. Several of these words are fine in
context — "drive" in "you'll drive the roadmap" is unremarkable; "driven, aggressive
self-starter who thrives on competition" is a pattern. What matters is the accumulation and
whether the sentence describes the work or describes a personality type.

## 1. Vague signalling language that narrows your pool

**The pattern.** Adverts that cluster competitive, dominance and lone-hero vocabulary:
*aggressive, dominant, competitive, driven, relentless, fearless, hunter, crush, smash,
dominate, battle-tested, killer instinct, ruthless, best-of-breed, elite*.

**Why it costs applications.** None of it describes the job. It describes a vibe, so the
reader finishes the advert knowing nothing about what they would actually do or whether
they would be good at it — and an advert carrying no information about the work cannot be
self-screened against. What it does instead is signal, and signalling narrows the response
to people who like the signal rather than people who can do the work. That is a smaller
pool and a less relevant one: fewer applications, and more screening time spent on the ones
that do arrive. It also reads as a culture warning to experienced senior candidates, who
are the hardest people to attract and the quickest to disqualify an employer on a single
line. In sales roles particularly, this vocabulary is inherited rather than chosen; nobody
in the business would defend it if asked.

**What to write instead.** Every one of these words has a specific operational fact
underneath it. Write the fact — the work and its measurable outcome, not the temperament of
the person doing it.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "Aggressive hunter mentality, relentless in pursuit of new logos" | "You'll open new accounts from a cold territory — roughly 60% of your number comes from business we do not have today." |
| "Dominate your market" | "Own the UK financial services territory end to end." |
| "Crush your targets" | "Quota is £1.2m. Five of seven AEs hit or beat it last year." |
| "Ruthless prioritisation" | "You'll decide what we do not build this quarter, and defend that in the exec review." |
| "Fearless self-starter" | "You'll be the first person in this seat and will set the priorities yourself for the first quarter." |

Note the pattern in the replacements: every one is *more* specific than the original, which
is why they also convert better. Removing coded language is not softening the advert. It is
making it concrete.

## 2. Superlatives and mythical-creature language

**The pattern.** *Rockstar, ninja, guru, wizard, superstar, unicorn, A-player, world-class,
best-in-class, exceptional, world-beating, elite, 10x, passionate.*

**Why it costs applications.** Three separate costs. It carries no information — every
company claims to want exceptional people, so the words do no discriminating work. It reads
as unserious to senior candidates, who are the audience the advert most needs. And it
screens on self-description rather than on capability: whether someone will call themselves
a rockstar tells you how they talk about themselves, not what they have delivered, so the
advert selects on a trait you would never put in a scorecard. "Ninja" and "guru" also carry
cultural and religious appropriation problems that are simply not worth the argument.

"Passionate" deserves its own note. It is the most common word in job adverts and one of
the emptiest, and where it means anything it often means "willing to work unpaid hours".

**What to write instead.** Delete, and replace with the standard the person will actually be
held to.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "We're looking for a marketing rockstar" | "You'll own demand generation for our two largest product lines." |
| "A-player with a passion for excellence" | "You'll be measured on pipeline created and on how much of it converts — both numbers are published internally every month." |
| "World-class engineer" | "You'll work on the checkout, which processes £400m a year and cannot go down." |
| "Passionate about customer success" | "You'll spend about a day a week in customer conversations, and you'll be the person who decides what we fix first." |

## 3. Degree requirements

**The pattern.** "Bachelor's degree in X or a related discipline", "degree educated",
"2:1 from a top-tier university", "MBA preferred", "Russell Group / Ivy League".

**Why it costs applications.** For most commercial, technical, operational and creative
roles there is no evidence that a degree predicts performance once you can assess the work
directly — and you can, with a work sample. What a degree requirement reliably does is
filter on who had access to a university place, which is not the same question as who can
do the job; university-tier requirements narrow it further, to a handful of institutions.
It excludes the self-taught, career changers, apprenticeship and bootcamp routes, and anyone
whose country's qualifications do not map neatly onto the phrasing. And where a requirement
that is not essential to the role screens on grounds that track a protected characteristic,
it is legal exposure taken on for no gain in predictive power.

A large number of major employers have removed degree requirements from most roles over the
last few years. It is no longer an unusual position and it is easy to defend.

**What to write instead.** Ask what the degree was standing in for, and require that
instead.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "Bachelor's degree in Computer Science or related field" | Nothing. Assess the code. |
| "Degree in Marketing or equivalent experience" | "You've run paid acquisition with a budget you were accountable for." |
| "MBA preferred" | Delete, or if you mean commercial fluency: "You can build and defend a business case in front of a CFO." |
| "2:1 from a top-tier university" | Delete. This filters on institution, not on capability. |

**The genuine exceptions.** Some roles require a specific qualification by law or by
professional regulation — practising lawyers, doctors, chartered accountants, registered
engineers in some jurisdictions, roles requiring a specific licence. Keep those, and state
them precisely: "You must be a qualified solicitor admitted in England and Wales", not
"degree educated with a legal background". Precision keeps the requirement defensible and
stops it doing extra filtering you did not intend.

## 4. Years of experience

**The pattern.** "5+ years", "8-10 years' experience", "minimum 3 years in a similar role",
"at least 5 years post-qualification".

**Why it costs applications.** A years threshold is a proxy for capability and a poor one —
it cannot distinguish someone who has had one year of experience eight times from someone
who has had four intense ones. It is also an age proxy, and in several jurisdictions it is
treated as evidence of age discrimination in an advert, particularly at the "maximum" end
("2-4 years", which reads as "no career changers, no returners, nobody over thirty").

It costs applications in both directions. Candidates below the number do not apply even
when they can do the job — and the drop-off is sharpest among exactly the conservative
self-assessors you want to reach. Candidates well above it read it as a level signal and do
not apply either. And it screens out career changers, returners from a break, and anyone
whose relevant experience came under a different job title.

**What to write instead.** Name the scope, the scale or the specific thing they must have
done. This is more useful to the reader *and* more predictive for you.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "8+ years in product management" | "You've owned a product with real revenue attached and have shipped through at least one major replatform." |
| "5+ years managing teams" | "You've managed managers, and you've handled at least one performance exit." |
| "3-5 years' agency experience" | "You've run several client accounts at once and can talk through one you nearly lost." |
| "Minimum 2 years in SaaS sales" | "You've carried a quota and hit it, and you can talk me through a deal you lost and why." |

If the hiring manager insists on a number, offer the compromise that keeps the signal
without the gate: put the scope statement in the must-haves and add "this usually looks like
five or more years, but we care about what you've owned, not the count." That framing
converts the threshold from a barrier into a calibration hint.

## 5. Physical requirements

**The pattern.** "Must be able to lift 15kg", "able to stand for extended periods", "valid
driving licence required", "must be able to work at height", pasted into adverts for desk
roles because the template had them.

**Why it costs applications.** Where the requirement is not genuinely essential, it screens
out people who could do the job perfectly well, with or without an adjustment, and it
creates direct legal exposure — most disability discrimination regimes require that
requirements be genuinely essential and that adjustments be considered.
It also frequently does not survive contact with reality: the lifting line on a product
manager advert has been copied forward from a warehouse template for six years and nobody
has noticed.

**What to write instead.** Delete anything not genuinely essential. Where it is genuine,
state the task, not the physical capability, and pair it with adjustments.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "Must be able to lift up to 15kg" (desk role) | Delete. |
| "Must be able to lift up to 15kg" (warehouse role, genuine) | "The role involves moving stock in units of up to 15kg for parts of the shift. Tell us if you need an adjustment and we'll work it out." |
| "Valid driving licence required" (visits are occasional) | "The role involves roughly one client site visit a month; we cover travel however you get there." |
| "Valid driving licence required" (mobile engineer, genuine) | Keep it, and say why: "You'll drive between three sites daily; a licence is essential." |
| "Able to stand for extended periods" | Describe the actual pattern of the shift and offer adjustments. |

The test: if you removed this requirement, could a person do the job with a reasonable
adjustment? If yes, it is not essential and it should not be in the advert.

## 6. Culture language as an age or lifestyle proxy

**The pattern.** "Young, dynamic team", "work hard, play hard", "recent graduate", "digital
native", "fresh thinking", "high energy", "our beer fridge and ping-pong table", "like a
family", "always-on culture", "we work hard and we're not for everyone", "fast-paced".

**Why it costs applications.** "Young and dynamic" and "recent graduate" are age
discrimination in most jurisdictions and are read that way whether or not it was intended;
"digital native" is a standard example in age discrimination guidance. Beyond the legal
point, this register describes a lifestyle rather than a job: heavy social expectations,
evening commitments and blurred boundaries. It tells the reader nothing about the work, and
it asks them to opt into commitments outside it — so the people who respond are the ones
who want the lifestyle, not the ones who can do the job. Anyone senior enough to have been
inside one of these cultures before reads it as a warning.

"Like a family" is worth flagging separately — candidates read it as boundary-blurring and
as a warning about how difficult conversations get handled. "Fast-paced" has been used to
mean understaffed so consistently that it now signals that on its own.

**What to write instead.** Describe how the team actually works, in specifics that a reader
can evaluate.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "Young, dynamic team" | "A team of six, most of whom joined in the last two years, working on a product that did not exist eighteen months ago." |
| "Work hard, play hard" | "Core hours are 10-4 and we mean it. There's a team dinner once a quarter and nobody minds if you skip it." |
| "Fast-paced environment" | "We ship weekly and priorities move — you'll change what you are working on more often than in a larger company." |
| "Digital native" | "You're comfortable picking up new tools without training." (Or delete — this is true of almost everyone.) |
| "We're like a family" | "We're a small team and you'll know everyone. We do written feedback quarterly and it is direct." |
| "Must thrive under pressure" | "There are two peak periods a year — January and September — where the workload roughly doubles for a fortnight." |

## 7. Jargon, acronyms and internal vocabulary

**The pattern.** Internal team names ("the Payments Value Stream", "Squad Alpha"), internal
grade labels ("PDT-II", "Band 6"), company product codenames, unexplained industry acronyms,
and consultant vocabulary ("synergies", "leverage", "operationalise", "north star",
"holistic", "end-to-end ownership of the value chain").

**Why it costs applications.** Jargon is a competence test the reader did not sign up for.
An acronym wall tells a capable person who has done exactly this work under different
vocabulary that they are an outsider — and the people most likely to be excluded are career
changers and people from adjacent industries, both of which are pools you probably want.
Internal grade labels are worse: they are meaningless externally and make the role hard to
price and hard to find in search.

**What to write instead.**

- Expand every acronym on first use, or cut it. "SCA (the strong-customer-authentication
  rules that govern European card payments)" once, then use the acronym.
- Replace internal team names with a description: "the Payments Value Stream" becomes "the
  team that owns checkout and payouts".
- Advertise the market-standard title, and keep the internal grade for the offer letter.
- Cut consultant vocabulary entirely. It signals nothing, and a senior reader treats it as
  evidence the writer had nothing specific to say.
- Where an acronym is genuinely the searchable term for the specialism, keep it — a
  Kubernetes or SAP role should say so — but pair it with plain language.

## 8. Corporate passive voice

**The pattern.** "The successful candidate will be responsible for...", "Duties will
include...", "Applicants are required to demonstrate...", "The role holder shall...", "Other
duties commensurate with the position."

**Why it costs applications.** It is the register of a document written for an approvals
process, and it reads that way. It puts the reader at a distance from the work at exactly
the moment you want them imagining themselves doing it. It also makes everything longer,
which costs reader attention on a phone. And "other duties as required" tells a senior
candidate the role is not defined, which is often true and always off-putting.

**What to write instead.** Second person, active voice, present tense.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "The successful candidate will be responsible for managing the roadmap" | "You'll own the roadmap." |
| "Duties will include liaising with stakeholders" | "You'll work with Finance and the two account managers who handle our largest merchants." |
| "Applicants must be able to demonstrate strong analytical skills" | "You can get to the answer in the data yourself." |
| "Other duties commensurate with the role" | Delete. |

A quick check: count "you" and "you'll" against "the candidate" and "the role holder". If
the second group wins, the advert is still an internal document.

## 9. Ability and neurodiversity assumptions

**The pattern.** "Excellent verbal and written communication skills", "high-energy
personality", "thrives in a busy open-plan environment", "confident presenter", "must be
comfortable with ambiguity", "strong attention to detail", "outgoing and personable".

**Why it costs applications.** These blend genuine job requirements with personality and
sensory preferences. Many are not requirements of the work at all — "confident presenter"
appears on adverts for roles that never present. Where the requirement is not real, it
filters on personality and sensory preference rather than on the work, which costs you
capable applicants for nothing — and where the trait it screens on relates to a disability,
it is legal exposure as well. "Attention to detail" is the most-copied bullet in recruitment
and is almost never assessed.

**What to write instead.** Name the specific communication or working task, and say how the
job actually gets done.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "Excellent verbal and written communication skills" | "You'll write a weekly one-page update that the exec team reads, and present the quarterly review — about four times a year." |
| "High-energy, outgoing personality" | Delete, unless you can name the task it maps to. |
| "Thrives in a busy open-plan office" | "The office is open-plan; there are quiet rooms and most people wear headphones. Say if you need something different." |
| "Strong attention to detail" | "You'll own the month-end reconciliation, where a mistake shows up in the CFO's numbers." |
| "Comfortable with ambiguity" | "For the first quarter there is no roadmap and nobody will give you one. You'll write it." |

And put a real adjustments route in the advert: a named email address, an explicit statement
that no explanation is required and that the request does not reach the hiring manager. That
sentence does more for the applicant pool than any inclusion paragraph.

## 10. Gendered and family-status wording

**The pattern.** Gendered job titles (*salesman, foreman, waitress, chairman, handyman*),
"he/she" constructions, "manpower", "the right man for the job", and family-status framing
("ideal for a working mum", "suits a recent graduate", "would suit someone with no
commitments").

**Why it costs applications.** Gendered titles indicate a preference and are unlawful in
most jurisdictions to advertise on. Family-status framing does the same on a different
characteristic, and "would suit someone with no commitments" is a fairly direct statement
about hours that will not survive scrutiny either way.

**What to write instead.** Neutral titles (*salesperson, supervisor, server, chair,
maintenance technician*), second person throughout ("you'll"), and "the team" or "headcount"
rather than "manpower". For anything that was gesturing at flexibility, state the flexibility
as a fact available to everyone: "This role can be done four days a week or compressed hours;
say what you need at first conversation."

## 11. Hidden availability and location demands

**The pattern.** "Must be flexible", "occasional evening and weekend work", "must be able to
travel extensively", "hybrid working" with no detail, "must live within commuting distance
of X", "always available for our US clients".

**Why it costs applications.** Vague availability demands are read as unlimited by cautious
readers and ignored by confident ones, which produces exactly the wrong self-selection.
Undefined "hybrid" is the single most common cause of late-stage drop-out because both sides
assume their own version and find out at offer. Blanket travel and evening requirements are
almost always overstated by the hiring manager, and an unbounded commitment costs you people
who could comfortably have met the real one.

**What to write instead.** Numbers.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "Hybrid working" | "Two days a week in the London office, Tuesdays and Thursdays, because the squad is in together." |
| "Must be flexible" | "Two peak fortnights a year where hours run longer; the rest of the year is 9-5 and we mean it." |
| "Extensive travel required" | "About one trip a quarter, usually two nights, to Dublin or Amsterdam." |
| "Some evening work" | "One evening a month for the customer forum. It's in the diary a year ahead." |
| "Must live within commuting distance" | State the office days; let the candidate work out their own commute. |

## 12. Unverifiable inclusion claims

**The pattern.** "We celebrate diversity", "we're committed to building an inclusive
culture", "we welcome applications from all backgrounds", "we're an equal opportunities
employer" standing alone as the whole inclusion content.

**Why it costs applications.** Not that it deters — it does nothing, which is the problem.
Every competitor has the identical paragraph, so it carries no information and readers skip
it and look for evidence instead. A claim occupies the space where evidence could have
been.

**What to write instead.** Describe practices, which are checkable.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "We're committed to diversity and inclusion" | "Every candidate gets the same four stages and the same questions, scored against a written standard before anyone compares notes." |
| "We welcome applications from all backgrounds" | "We do not require a degree for this role and we do not ask for your current salary." |
| "We support flexible working" | "Everyone here can request compressed or reduced hours from day one; four people in this team of nine work non-standard patterns." |
| "We're an inclusive employer" | "Ask for any adjustment to the process at sam@example.com. No explanation needed and it does not go to the hiring manager." |

Keep any equal-opportunity statement that is legally required — it often is — but put it
last, beneath the described practices. And do not write a practice you do not have. A
candidate who is told the process is structured and then sits through four unstructured
conversations has learned something worse than nothing.

## Reporting the audit

Report findings in a table, in the diagnosis section, with the actual text quoted:

| Phrase in the original | Pattern | Cost | Replacement |
|---|---|---|---|
| "aggressive, driven hunter" | Vague signalling | Says nothing about the work, so it draws fewer and less relevant applications and adds screening time; reads as a culture warning to senior candidates | "You'll open new accounts from a cold territory — about 60% of your number is new business." |
| "8+ years' experience" | Years-as-proxy, age proxy | Deters capable people below the line and strong people above it; legal exposure in several jurisdictions | "You've owned a product with revenue attached and shipped through a replatform." |
| "Bachelor's degree required" | Socioeconomic filter | Excludes self-taught, career changers and apprenticeship routes for no gain in predictive power | Delete; assess the work sample. |

Three to six rows. Pick the ones that cost the most, fix everything else silently in the
rewrite, and say that you have.
