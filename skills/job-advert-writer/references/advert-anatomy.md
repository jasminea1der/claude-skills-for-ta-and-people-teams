# Advert anatomy

The section-by-section structure of a job advert that works on someone who is not looking:
what each section has to achieve, how it usually fails, and a weak-versus-strong worked
example of each.

The running example is a **Senior Product Manager, Payments** at a mid-size B2B software
company, with a second example in the pay and commission sections for an **Enterprise
Account Executive**. Both are invented for illustration. Replace every detail with the
real ones — the whole argument of this reference is that specificity is what persuades,
and a generic advert built from these examples will not work.

## The reading order

Internal specs are written in the order the business thinks: company, department, role
purpose, responsibilities, requirements, benefits. Candidates read in a different order and
they read on a phone, between other things, with the back button one thumb-movement away.

Write in the order they ask:

| Order | The question in their head | Section |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What is this job? | Title + opening |
| 2 | Why does it exist, and why now? | Why now |
| 3 | What will I actually do? | The first year |
| 4 | Who will I work with? | Team and manager |
| 5 | Why would I leave what I have? | The case |
| 6 | Can I do this / am I allowed to apply? | What we're looking for |
| 7 | What does it pay? | Pay |
| 8 | What do I have to go through? | Process |

Everything else — company history, mission statement, values, awards — is background, and
background goes near the end or into a link.

## 1. Title

**What it must achieve:** be findable, and be understood at a glance by someone who has
never worked at your company.

**How it fails:** internal titles nobody searches for, level inflation or deflation that
misprices the role in the reader's head, and appended noise that pushes the real title out
of the visible characters on a job board.

**Weak**

> Product Owner II — Payments Value Stream (Ref: PDT-4471) — Hybrid — Exciting Opportunity!

**Strong**

> Senior Product Manager, Payments

Rules that matter:
- Use the title the market searches for, not the internal grade name. If the internal title
  is "Product Owner II" and the market says Senior Product Manager, advertise the market
  title and explain the internal one at offer.
- One title. No slashes stacking three roles into one req — that reads as a company that
  has not decided what it wants, and it is usually true.
- Nothing decorative. "Exciting opportunity", "rockstar", "guru" and the req number all
  cost characters and credibility.
- Level in the title, location and working pattern immediately below it in a metadata line
  — not buried three paragraphs down.

## 2. The opening — what this job is

**What it must achieve:** in two sentences, tell the reader what they would do and what
would be theirs. This is the whole advert's audition. If the opening is generic, nothing
after it gets read.

**How it fails:** it opens with the company. Founding year, market position, funding round,
values. All of it answers a question the reader has not asked yet.

**Weak**

> Founded in 2014, Acme is a leading provider of cloud-based workflow solutions trusted by
> over 3,000 businesses worldwide. Following our Series C, we are on an exciting growth
> journey and are looking for passionate, driven individuals to join our world-class team.
> We are currently seeking a Product Owner II to join our Payments Value Stream, reporting
> into the Director of Product. The successful candidate will be responsible for
> owning the end-to-end product lifecycle for assigned initiatives.

Ninety words, and the reader still does not know what they would do on a Tuesday.

**Strong**

> Own payments at Acme. You'll take a checkout that works but costs us more than it should,
> and rebuild the parts that lose money — starting with a payment-failure rate that is
> quietly costing about 2% of processed volume.
>
> You'll be the first product manager dedicated to payments here. Today it is split across
> three teams and nobody owns the numbers.

Why it works: it names the actual problem, it says what is broken, and it establishes scope
in a way the reader can picture themselves holding. "First PM dedicated to payments" and
"nobody owns the numbers" do more persuasive work than any adjective available.

Use a real number if you have one and can publish it. If you cannot, use the shape of the
problem: "a failure rate high enough that finance has started asking about it."

## 3. Why the role exists, and why now

**What it must achieve:** convert the reader from "this is a job" to "this is a moment". A
role with a reason is a bet with momentum behind it; a role without one is a backfill.

**How it fails:** it is missing entirely. This is the most under-used section in recruiting
and it is nearly free to write, because the hiring manager always knows the answer — it
just never gets asked for. It is question 1 in `hiring-manager-intake` for that reason.

**Weak**

> Due to continued growth, we are expanding our Product team.

**Strong**

> Two things changed this year. We moved off a single payment provider onto three, which cut
> our fees and tripled the complexity. And payments went from a feature to a line item the
> board asks about, because it now moves £400m a year.
>
> We have been handling it with a shared engineering squad and a spreadsheet. That worked at
> £100m. It does not work now, and the next twelve months decide whether payments becomes a
> capability we own or a permanent tax we pay to someone else.

Handle a backfill honestly. Silence about a departure reads worse than the truth:

> Your predecessor moved into a general management role here after three years. The team is
> stable, the roadmap is funded, and this is not a rescue job — it is a working system that
> now needs a bigger version of what it already has.

If the honest answer is that the last two people left within a year, do not write that in
the advert — but tell the user it will surface at interview, and that having an answer
ready is a better plan than hoping it does not come up.

## 4. The first year

**What it must achieve:** let the reader picture the work in enough detail that they can
tell whether they would be good at it and whether they would enjoy it. This is the
converting section. It is also the section internal specs replace with a list of verbs.

**How it fails:** responsibilities instead of work. A responsibility list describes a job
category; a first-year narrative describes *this* job.

**Weak**

> **Key Responsibilities**
> - Own the end-to-end product lifecycle for assigned initiatives
> - Define and maintain the product roadmap in line with strategic objectives
> - Work cross-functionally with Engineering, Design, Data and Commercial stakeholders
> - Gather and prioritise requirements from internal and external stakeholders
> - Drive continuous improvement through data-led decision making
> - Act as a subject matter expert for the Payments domain
> - Support the wider Product team as required
> - Other duties commensurate with the role

Eight bullets that describe every product manager who has ever lived. Nothing here is false
and nothing here is useful.

**Strong**

> **The first ninety days**
> Learn where the money leaks. You'll sit with the payment-operations team, read the failure
> data nobody has read end to end, and come back with the three fixes worth doing first.
> We expect one of them shipped before day 90.
>
> **Months three to nine**
> Rebuild retry and routing logic across our three providers. This is the big one — done
> well it pays for the role several times over, and it means working closely with an
> engineering team that has strong opinions and good reasons for them.
>
> Alongside it, get a weekly payments number in front of the exec team that everyone agrees
> is correct. That is a harder problem than it sounds; there are currently four versions.
>
> **By month twelve**
> Payments has a roadmap, an owner and a set of metrics the board trusts. You will have
> hired one more PM if the volume argument holds, and you will be the person the company
> asks before it enters a new market.

Rules:
- **Sequence it.** 90 days, mid-year, year one. Sequence implies a plan, and a plan implies
  the company knows what it is doing.
- **Name the hard part.** "An engineering team with strong opinions" is a real signal.
  Candidates who want that will want it more; candidates who would struggle will
  self-select out, which is a win, not a loss.
- **Include one uncomfortable truth.** Legacy code, a stakeholder who is hard work, a
  metric nobody trusts. It buys credibility for everything else on the page, and the
  strongest candidates are specifically looking for a real problem to solve.
- **This is where learnable requirements go.** Anything that was on the requirements list
  but is acquirable in the job belongs here as "you'll pick up X", where it reads as growth
  rather than as a gate.

## 5. The team and the manager

**What it must achieve:** people join teams and managers, and they leave managers. Naming
one converts far better than describing a department.

**How it fails:** an org-chart sentence with no human in it.

**Weak**

> Reporting into the Director of Product, you will work as part of a fast-paced,
> collaborative and high-performing team within our Payments Value Stream.

**Strong**

> You'll report to Priya, our Director of Product, who ran payments at a marketplace before
> this and is the reason we moved to three providers. She runs a weekly written review and
> is not a fan of status meetings.
>
> Day to day you'll work with a squad of five engineers and a designer who has been on
> payments for two years and knows more about our checkout than anyone. Outside the squad
> you'll spend real time with Finance — they are the internal customer for half of what you
> will build — and with the two account managers who handle our largest merchants.

Notes:
- **Name the manager and give one line of their background.** A candidate can then look
  them up, which is what they were going to do anyway. Get the manager's permission first.
- **Say how the manager works.** Written review, no status meetings, weekly 1:1s, async
  first. This is real information and it is the kind that decides whether someone applies.
- **Name the friction points as collaborations.** "Real time with Finance" tells a senior
  candidate exactly what kind of job this is.
- Avoid the stacked adjective list. "Fast-paced, collaborative, high-performing" is three
  words that appear in every advert and therefore carry no information; worse, "fast-paced"
  is widely read as understaffed and "work hard, play hard" as a lifestyle filter — see
  `language-audit.md`.

## 6. Why this is a good move

**What it must achieve:** answer, for someone with a job they are not unhappy in, the
question of what they get here that they cannot get where they are.

**How it fails:** it becomes a benefits list, or a list of company adjectives. Free lunch is
not a reason to change jobs.

**Weak**

> **Why join us?**
> - Competitive salary and benefits package
> - 25 days holiday plus bank holidays
> - Private healthcare
> - Regular team socials and a vibrant office culture
> - Genuine opportunity for career progression in a growing company

**Strong**

> **What's genuinely good about this**
> Payments is a specialism with a small talent market and it travels — running it end to end
> at £400m of volume is a credential that opens doors we do not control.
>
> You will own the P&L impact directly. Most product roles argue for revenue attribution;
> here the number is unarguable, which makes the case for what you do next much easier to
> make.
>
> Priya has hired two people into this team who have since been promoted. Ask her about both
> in your interview — she will tell you what she did and what she got wrong.
>
> **What's harder about it**
> Our payments infrastructure has four years of accumulated shortcuts in it and you will
> spend the first quarter in the detail of them. If you want a greenfield build, this is not
> that.
>
> We are hybrid, two days a week in the London office, and those days are fixed to Tuesday
> and Thursday because the squad is in together. If that does not work for you it will not
> work here.

Why the honest half matters: it is the most persuasive part of the section. Every advert
claims the upside; almost none names a cost. Naming one signals that the rest is true, and
it screens out mismatches before they consume interview time.

Keep the benefits list, but put it in the pay section as facts, not as a sales pitch.

## 7. What we're looking for

**What it must achieve:** help the right person recognise themselves. Not screen — the
screen happens in the process, and a list cannot do it anyway.

**How it fails:** fifteen bullets, mixing must-haves with wishes with things anyone would
learn in a month. See the SKILL.md argument on why this cuts volume and skews who applies.

**Weak**

> **Essential Requirements**
> - Bachelor's degree in Computer Science, Business or a related discipline
> - 8+ years of experience in product management, with at least 5 in a senior capacity
> - Proven track record of delivering complex products in a fast-paced environment
> - Deep expertise in payments, including PCI-DSS, 3DS2, PSD2, SCA and card scheme rules
> - Strong knowledge of SQL and data analysis tools
> - Experience with Agile/Scrum methodologies and tools (JIRA, Confluence)
> - Excellent written and verbal communication skills
> - Strong stakeholder management skills at C-suite level
> - Experience working with distributed engineering teams
> - Ability to thrive in an ambiguous, rapidly changing environment
> - Self-starter with a can-do attitude and exceptional attention to detail
> - Experience in a high-growth SaaS environment preferred
> - MBA or equivalent preferred
> - Must be able to travel up to 25% of the time
> - Must be able to lift up to 15kg

Fifteen items. Perhaps three of them decide anything. The degree line is a socioeconomic
filter with no evidence behind it for this work, "8+ years" is a proxy for capability that
also proxies for age, the lifting requirement has no relationship to the job, and the
acronym run in the payments bullet is a wall to anyone who has done the work under
different names.

**Strong**

> **What you'll need**
> - You've owned a payments or transactional product where failures cost real money, and you
>   can talk through a specific decision you made about routing, retries or fraud
>   thresholds.
> - You can get to an answer in the data yourself. You do not need to write the query, but
>   you do need to know when the number you have been handed is wrong.
> - You've worked closely enough with engineers that they would describe you as a
>   counterpart, not a customer.
> - You can hold a position with a senior stakeholder who wants a different answer, and
>   change your mind in public when the evidence goes the other way.
>
> **Useful, not required**
> Card scheme rules, multi-provider routing, marketplace or platform payments, experience
> hiring a first PM into a team. Nobody has all of these. We have not met anyone who does.
>
> If you meet most of the first list and the role interests you, apply. We would rather read
> your application and decide than have you decide for us.

What changed and why it works:
- **Four must-haves, each written as evidence rather than as a credential.** "You can talk
  through a specific decision about routing" is testable in an interview and is what the
  eight-years line was trying to approximate.
- **Years and degree removed.** Both were proxies. The thing underneath — has this person
  actually owned consequential payments decisions — is now stated directly.
- **The acronym wall is gone.** Someone who has run payments at a company that uses
  different vocabulary is no longer excluded on terminology.
- **The travel line moved** to the working-pattern section as a fact ("roughly one trip a
  quarter to our Dublin office"), where it belongs. The lifting requirement is deleted; if a
  physical requirement is genuinely essential, state it precisely and only then.
- **The explicit invitation to apply at partial match.** This overrides conservative
  self-assessment and it is one line.

## 8. Pay, benefits and working pattern

**What it must achieve:** let the reader decide whether to spend an hour on you. It is the
question they most want answered and the one adverts most often dodge.

**How it fails:** "competitive salary", or a range so wide it is not a range.

**Weak**

> Competitive salary depending on experience, plus excellent benefits.

**Strong (salaried role)**

> £95,000 – £115,000, plus a 10% target bonus and share options.
>
> Most offers at this level land between £100,000 and £110,000. The top of the range is for
> someone already running payments at our scale or larger. We do not ask for your current
> salary and we do not negotiate against it.
>
> Hybrid: Tuesdays and Thursdays in our London office, the rest wherever you work best.
> 27 days holiday plus bank holidays. Six months full pay parental leave for all parents.
> Roughly one trip a quarter to our Dublin engineering office.

**Strong (commission role — Enterprise Account Executive)**

> £70,000 base, £140,000 on-target earnings, uncapped.
>
> Of the seven AEs who have been here a full year, five hit or beat target. Average quota
> attainment across the team last year was 94%. Accelerators start at 100% and the top
> earner made £210,000.
>
> Territory is UK financial services, inherited with four existing accounts worth £1.1m in
> current ARR. Average deal size £85,000, average cycle 4-5 months.

That second example is what a candidate in a commission role actually wants to know, and
almost no advert provides it. It is also the strongest case for the interactive HTML
variant — a live earnings model beats any paragraph describing one.

Rules:
- Ask which jurisdictions the role is posted in, and **verify the pay transparency
  requirement by web search at drafting time**, citing what you find. Several jurisdictions
  now require a range in the posting; the specifics vary and change.
- A published range is a commitment. Publish one you will honour, or the goodwill it buys
  is spent at offer stage with interest.
- Never ask for salary history. It is unlawful in several jurisdictions and it carries
  existing pay inequity into the new hire. Saying so in the advert is a genuine
  differentiator.
- State the working pattern precisely. "Hybrid" alone means five different things; name the
  days.

## 9. Process and timeline

**What it must achieve:** remove uncertainty, which is one of the main reasons a passive
candidate does not start. Someone weighing an application against a busy month needs to
know what it costs them.

**How it fails:** absent, or "our recruitment process consists of several stages".

**Weak**

> Successful applicants will be contacted to arrange an initial screening call. The
> interview process consists of several stages.

**Strong**

> **How this works**
> 1. 30 minutes with Sam, our recruiter — the role, your background, pay expectations both ways.
> 2. 45 minutes with Priya, the hiring manager. Bring a payments decision you made and would
>    make differently now.
> 3. A 90-minute working session with two of the squad. We give you a real (anonymised)
>    failure dataset and talk through what you would do. No take-home.
> 4. 30 minutes with our CFO, who is the internal customer for the numbers.
>
> Four stages, normally three weeks end to end. We reply to every application within five
> working days. Ask for adjustments at any point — mail sam@example.com and it is arranged,
> no explanation needed.

Notes:
- Naming people makes it real and makes the company accountable to its own process.
- Saying "no take-home" is a competitive advantage in most markets and costs nothing to say
  when it is true.
- A stated timeline is a promise. Write the true one. Seven weeks written honestly beats
  three weeks written optimistically and missed.

## 10. The company section

**What it must achieve:** enough context to make the role legible. Nothing more.

**How it fails:** it goes first, it is 200 words, and it is written by marketing for a
different audience entirely.

**Weak (and first on the page)**

> Acme is a leading provider of cloud-based workflow solutions, trusted by over 3,000
> businesses worldwide. Founded in 2014 with a mission to empower teams to do their best
> work, we have grown to over 400 employees across five offices...

**Strong (and near the end)**

> **About Acme**
> We make workflow software for mid-size logistics companies — 3,000 customers, 400 people,
> profitable since 2022. Payments is how our customers pay their subcontractors, which is
> why the numbers are large relative to our size.

Three sentences, all of them load-bearing for understanding the role. The mission
statement, the awards and the values live on the careers site, one link away, for the
candidate who wants them.

## 11. Inclusion, adjustments and the close

**What it must achieve:** tell someone deciding whether they will be treated fairly here
something they can actually verify.

**How it fails:** a boilerplate equal-opportunity paragraph that every competitor also has,
which candidates have learned to read as legally required wallpaper.

**Weak**

> Acme is an equal opportunities employer. We celebrate diversity and are committed to
> creating an inclusive environment for all employees. All qualified applicants will receive
> consideration for employment without regard to race, colour, religion, sex, national
> origin, disability status or any other protected characteristic.

**Strong**

> Every candidate gets the same four stages and the same questions, scored against the same
> written standard. We publish our ranges and do not negotiate against your current salary.
>
> If you need anything adjusted — extra time, a different interview format, questions in
> advance, a specific accessibility need — mail sam@example.com. You do not need to explain
> why, and it does not go to the hiring manager.
>
> If you meet most of what's above and this sounds like your kind of problem, apply. If
> you're not sure, apply anyway and let us decide.

Keep whatever equal-opportunity statement legal requires — it is often mandatory — but put
the described practices above it, because those are the ones that are read and believed.

The close should be one sentence, specific, and low-friction. "Apply" beats "submit your
application via our portal, quoting reference PDT-4471".

## The 40-second test

Before delivering the advert, read it as the reader will:

1. Read only the first two sentences. Do you know what the job is and what would be yours?
2. Read only the headings. Do they tell the story on their own?
3. Count sentences about what the company wants versus what the candidate gets. If the first
   number is much larger, the advert is still an internal document.
4. Find the requirements list. Would a capable person who matches four of five items apply?
5. Search for the pay. How many seconds did it take?
6. Ask: could a competitor publish this advert unchanged by swapping the company name? If
   yes, nothing in it is doing persuasive work.
7. Read it on a phone-width column. Where does the eye stop?

Anything that fails one of these gets rewritten before delivery, not flagged for the user.
