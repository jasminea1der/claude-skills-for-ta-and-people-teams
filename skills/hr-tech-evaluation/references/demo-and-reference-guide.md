# Demos that differentiate, and reference calls that tell the truth

Two steps carry most of the evidence in a system selection, and both are usually run badly:
the demo, because the vendor controls it, and the reference call, because the questions are
too polite to produce information.

This file covers how to take control of both.

## Part 1 — Demos

### Why the default demo tells you nothing

A standard demo is a rehearsed performance built from the paths where the product is
strongest, using data curated to make those paths look clean, presented by someone who has
delivered it hundreds of times and can absorb almost any question without losing the
thread. Nothing about that is dishonest. It is simply not evidence about your organisation.

Three specific distortions to design against:

- **The clean-tenant illusion.** Demo environments have four job titles, twelve tidy
  candidates and no legacy mess. Your environment has eleven years of accumulated
  exceptions. Products differ enormously at volume and mess, and not at all on twelve
  records.
- **The happy path.** Demos show the task done correctly the first time. Real work is
  mostly correction, exception and bulk. Where products differ is the unhappy path.
- **Presenter skill laundering.** A confident presenter makes a mediocre product look
  smooth and a strong product look effortless. Unless every vendor performs the same tasks,
  you are scoring presenters.

### The five moves that make a demo evidence

1. **Send identical scenarios in advance.** Three to five, one to two weeks ahead, same for
   every vendor. Same order, same time budget. Say plainly that the session will follow
   them and that time for a general product overview is capped — otherwise you will get the
   overview anyway and lose half the session.
2. **Require a configured environment.** Ask them to set up something resembling your
   structure: your stage names, a few of your departments, your approval chain, a realistic
   volume of records. A vendor who will not configure a demo will not configure well later,
   and how they respond to the request is itself a data point.
3. **Insist on the ugly workflows.** Named below. This is where the score comes from.
4. **Put the daily users in the room.** The coordinator, the recruiter, the HR ops
   administrator, one hiring manager who is not a fan of process, and one person who will
   have to report out of it. They notice things the buyer cannot.
5. **Score within an hour, independently, before discussion.** Otherwise the group converges
   on the most senior or most confident opinion in the room, and the scorecard records that
   instead of the product.

### Writing the scenarios

Draw them from real work in the last month, not hypotheticals. Good scenarios share four
properties: they are frequent, they are currently painful, they span more than one screen,
and they have a defined end state you can check.

Choose from these shapes, adapted to the category:

- **The volume day.** The thing your team does forty times a day. Have them do it three
  times in a row and watch the click count.
- **The correction.** Somebody made a mistake three weeks ago — wrong stage, wrong salary,
  wrong start date, a rejection sent in error. Show me fixing it, and show me the audit
  trail afterwards.
- **The bulk action.** Move or reject forty candidates. Update a field on two hundred
  employees. Then undo it.
- **The untrained user.** A hiring manager who logs in twice a quarter needs to review a
  shortlist and submit feedback. No training, no help from you. Ask the vendor to hand the
  mouse to someone in the room who has not seen the system.
- **The unanticipated report.** Give them a question during the session, not before.
  Anything that requires joining two things they did not expect you to join.
- **The exception.** The candidate who reapplies, the employee with two concurrent
  contracts, the mid-cycle joiner, the country with the odd rule, the confidential
  requisition.
- **The handoff.** Where the workflow crosses into another system — offer to HRIS,
  performance to comp, ATS to payroll.
- **The end-to-end.** One record from creation to conclusion, with the clock running.

Send them with a note that says: please run these in a configured environment, in this
order, and expect us to ask to drive.

### The demo script structure

Use the same script for every vendor. Suggested shape for a 90-minute session:

| Block | Time | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Framing | 5 min | You state the agenda and the scenarios. Not the vendor. |
| Vendor overview | 10 min | Capped, deliberately. Say the cap in advance. |
| Scenarios 1–4 | 50 min | Run in order, timed. Interrupt to ask "how would an admin change that?" |
| Hands on keyboard | 10 min | Someone from your side drives one task, unassisted. |
| Unanticipated report | 5 min | The question you did not send. |
| Open questions | 10 min | From the probing bank, chosen for this vendor's claims. |
| Follow-ups | — | Log what they must send in writing. |

Rules that hold the session together:

- One person from your side chairs and keeps time. Vendors overrun the overview by design.
- Ask "show me" every time you hear "you can". The gap between those two phrases is the
  whole evaluation.
- Ask "who does that — us, you, or professional services?" every time something is
  configured on screen.
- Write follow-ups down as you go and send them within a day, asking for written answers.
  A capability confirmed only verbally in a demo is not a capability.

### Running the session

- **Do not schedule three demos in one day.** They blur, and the last one wins on recency.
- **Same length, same attendees where possible.** Uneven attendance produces uneven scores
  for reasons unrelated to product.
- **Nominate one person to watch the candidate or employee-facing experience only**, on a
  phone, in real time.
- **Ask for a trial or sandbox after the demo** for the final two. An hour in the product
  beats three hours of watching it. Use synthetic or anonymised data if you load anything.
- **If the vendor refuses the scenarios or arrives with the standard deck**, note it. It is
  the most predictive signal you get about the implementation to come.

### Reading the room: signals worth noting

Not scores — notes for the decision paper.

- The presenter switches to a slide when asked to show something live.
- "That's on the roadmap" — ask when, and whether it is contractually committed. Then score
  it as absent.
- "We'd handle that in implementation" — ask what it costs and who does it.
- Sales engineer cannot answer an admin question and has to take it away. Fine once; a
  pattern means the configurability claim is thin.
- Every answer is yes. Nothing does everything; a vendor with no "no" is not being straight
  and you will find the "no" after signature.
- They will not let you drive.
- They cannot produce the requested report and pivot to a dashboard that answers a
  different question.
- They know their own product's limits and say so plainly. This is a positive signal and
  worth recording as one.

### Scoring immediately afterwards

Fifteen minutes, individually, before anyone speaks. Each scorer records for each
requirement: the score, and one line of evidence — what they saw, not what they were told.
Mark each score as **demonstrated**, **claimed** or **not covered**.

Then compare. Where scorers are two or more points apart, discuss that requirement
specifically; the disagreement usually means one person saw something the other missed, and
that is the most valuable ten minutes of the whole process. Anything still marked "claimed"
after all demos goes on the follow-up list to be confirmed in writing or tested in a trial
before signature.

## Part 2 — The probing question bank

Pick the ten to fifteen that matter for the vendor and category in front of you. Asking all
of them turns the session into an interrogation and produces worse information.

### Cross-category questions

- Show me the same task done by an administrator, and then by an untrained occasional user.
- What is the most common reason a customer raises a support ticket about this workflow?
- What here can we configure ourselves, and what needs you?
- Show me the audit trail for the change you just made.
- Show me this data exported. Now show me the same thing via the API documentation.
- What has your team told you they wish this product did better?
- Which of your customers at our size and in our countries can we speak to?
- What went wrong in your last implementation of our size, and what did you change after?
- If we ran a report tomorrow that you have never been asked for, would we build it or
  would we raise a ticket?
- What does a customer typically underestimate when moving to you?

### ATS

- Show me rejecting forty candidates, then show me undoing that.
- A candidate applied in 2021 and again today. Show me what the recruiter sees.
- Show me a hiring manager's view on a phone, with no training.
- Show me an interviewer submitting a scorecard, and show me whether they can see other
  feedback first.
- Show me the approval chain for a requisition where the second approver is on holiday.
- Show me the candidate-facing application on a phone, start to finish, timed.
- Show me stage-conversion by department for the last two quarters.
- Show me how a candidate exercises a data deletion request and what it does to reporting.
- Show me a confidential requisition and prove who cannot see it.
- Show me how an agency submission is claimed and how a duplicate is resolved.
- What happens to interview feedback when a candidate moves to a different requisition?

### HRIS / core HR

- Show me the org chart as it stood on a date last year.
- Show me a promotion effective next month, and what a report run today shows.
- Show me a reorganisation moving thirty people to a new manager, then reversing it.
- Show me an employee with two concurrent positions in two entities.
- Show me what a manager can see about a skip-level report, and what they cannot.
- Show me a custom field, then show me that field in a report and in the API.
- Show me how leave accrual differs between two of our countries.
- Show me a correction to a historic record and what the audit trail records.
- Show me offboarding: what is revoked, what is retained, and on what schedule.

### Payroll

Have the payroll owner ask these; do not run this category from the HR seat alone.

- Which of our countries run on your own engine and which run through a partner? Name them.
- Show me a retro pay correction crossing a tax year boundary.
- Show me the gross-to-net breakdown for a single employee and explain each element.
- Show me an off-cycle run and what it does to reporting and to the GL posting.
- How are statutory changes applied, by whom, and what is the commitment in the contract?
- Show me the parallel run reconciliation output.
- What happens when a run fails on the day, and who is accountable?
- Show me the payslip archive and tell me what we can extract if we leave.
- Show me segregation of duties preventing someone approving their own pay change.

### Performance

- Show me a cycle where two populations have different forms and different timelines.
- Show me a calibration session: adjusting ratings live, and the audit trail afterwards.
- Show me what an employee sees at each stage and when that visibility changes.
- Show me chasing the eleven managers who have not completed, and escalating to their
  manager.
- Show me an employee who changed manager mid-cycle.
- Show me rating distribution by manager and by level.
- Show me a mid-cycle joiner and a mid-cycle leaver.
- Show me how a rating flows to compensation, and what happens if the rating changes after.

### Engagement

- What is the minimum group size for reporting, and can we change it? Show me what happens
  at the threshold.
- Show me exactly what a manager of six people sees, including free text.
- Show me what happens to a comment describing harassment or a safety concern.
- How is anonymity technically protected, and what could an administrator see if they
  tried?
- Show me the same question tracked across three cycles.
- If you offer benchmarks: how is the comparison group built, how current is it, and can we
  see its definition?
- Show me how the survey reaches employees without a company email or a desk.
- If we leave, what do we take with us — items, raw responses, historic scores?

### Sourcing / CRM

- Where does your contact data come from and on what lawful basis do you supply it?
- Show me deduplication against an existing ATS record.
- Show me a sequence pausing when someone replies, and the unsubscribe path.
- Show me suppression lists and how consent is recorded per jurisdiction.
- Show me reporting from first touch through to hire, not just messages sent.
- Show me what happens when a sourced contact asks what data you hold on them.

### Assessment

- What does this instrument measure, and what evidence links it to performance in roles like
  ours? Send us the technical manual.
- What adverse impact evidence exists, on which populations, and will you share it?
- Show me the candidate experience end to end on a phone, timed.
- How does a candidate request an adjustment, and who sees that request?
- Who sets the cut score, on what basis, and can we change it?
- What does a hiring team see — a number, a band, or the reasoning?
- What is your retest policy and score validity period?
- What happens to assessment data when a candidate is rejected?

### AI feature questions

- Describe what this does as a workflow, not a benefit. Now show it running on our scenario.
- Does it make, rank, score, filter or influence a decision about a person? Answer in
  writing.
- What is it trained on? Does our data train your models? Can we opt out, and what do we
  lose if we do?
- Has it been audited for bias? By whom, when, against what, and what will you share?
- Where is the human in the loop by design rather than by configuration, and what do they
  see?
- Show me a case where it got it wrong, and how we would find out that it had.
- Can we turn this feature off entirely and keep the rest of the product?
- What happens when you update the model — do we get notice, and can we defer?
- Can you explain a single output well enough for us to answer a candidate who challenges
  it?

Verify the current regulatory position for the relevant jurisdictions by search at runtime,
cite what you find with dates, and pass it to legal. Do not assert obligations from memory.

### Commercial questions to ask in the room

Ask these in the demo so you get an unrehearsed answer, then get everything in writing.

- What exactly is in this price, and what is the first thing customers find is not?
- How does the price change if our headcount grows 30%? Shrinks 20%?
- What is the uplift mechanism at renewal, and is it capped?
- What is the notice period, and does it auto-renew?
- Is there a sandbox, and does it cost extra?
- What implementation days are included, what they cover, and what is billed beyond them?
- What happens if implementation runs over — who bears that cost?
- If we leave in three years, what do we get, in what format, and what does it cost?

Contract terms go to legal and procurement. You are collecting answers, not agreeing them.

## Part 3 — Reference calls

The most under-used step. A reference call costs thirty minutes and routinely changes a
decision. It is the only point in the process where you hear from someone with nothing to
sell you.

### Who to ask for

Ask the vendor for four, and expect three:

1. **Your scale and your jurisdictions.** A product that works at 400 employees in one
   country may behave differently at 4,000 across six.
2. **Implemented in the last twelve months.** Both the product and the implementation team
   change; a reference from four years ago describes a different experience.
3. **Migrated from your incumbent.** This is your migration reference and the most useful
   call you will have.
4. **Similar operating model.** Distributed, high-volume, unionised, multi-entity —
   whichever of your characteristics is most awkward.

Then find one the vendor did not give you: your own network, a peer group, a former
colleague. It is not an ambush — a customer speaking without the vendor in the room is
simply the least filtered source available.

### How to run the call

- **Thirty minutes, one interviewer plus one note-taker.** Two interviewers turns it into a
  panel and reduces candour.
- **Open by saying you are not looking for a testimonial.** "We've seen the demo. What I'm
  after is what it's actually like to live with." That framing does most of the work.
- **Do not ask the vendor to join.** If they insist, the value of the call has already gone;
  run it anyway and weight it lower.
- **Ask the same core questions of every reference**, so you can compare across vendors
  rather than collecting anecdotes.
- **Leave silence after the awkward questions.** The pause is where the useful answer
  arrives.

### The question set

**Context, so you can weight the answer**
- How long have you been live, and how many employees and users?
- Which modules are you actually using, and which did you buy and not turn on?
- Who runs it day to day on your side?

**Implementation**
- What took longer than you expected?
- What went wrong, and how did the vendor behave when it did?
- How much of your own team's time did it take, and who did that work instead of their day
  job?
- How did the data migration go, and what did not come across?
- What would you do differently if you were implementing it again?
- Did you go live on the original date and the original scope?

**Living with it**
- What does your team complain about?
- Who does the day-to-day admin, and how much of their week is it?
- What do you still do in a spreadsheet?
- What did you have to change about how you work to fit the tool?
- What did you assume it would do that it turned out not to do?
- How do your hiring managers or employees actually find it — not what you'd like them to
  say?
- How is support when something breaks properly? Give me the last real example.
- What has the product done in the last year — improved, stayed still, or gone backwards?

**Commercial**
- Any surprises on cost after signature?
- What happened at your first renewal?
- Anything you'd negotiate differently now?

**The closers** — ask all three, in this order
- Would you buy it again?
- What would have to be true for you to switch?
- If you were me, what would you check before signing that I probably haven't thought of?

That last question produces more usable information than any other on the list, because it
invites the reference to tell you the thing they wish someone had told them.

### Reading the answers

- **"It's been fine"** with no detail usually means fine. Push once — "what's the one thing
  you'd change?" — then move on.
- **Long hesitation before the admin-time question** is the classic tell that the admin load
  is higher than anyone planned for.
- **"We had to change our process"** is not automatically bad — sometimes the process needed
  changing. Ask whether the change was an improvement or a concession.
- **Enthusiasm about the account manager, vagueness about the product** is a signal about
  the product.
- **The same complaint from two independent references** is a finding. Put it in the
  decision paper as a named risk with a mitigation.
- **Nobody available at your scale or in your jurisdictions** is itself the answer. Record
  it as a risk rather than an absence of evidence.

```
Vendor:                        Date:
Reference: [org type, size, jurisdictions, modules, months live]
Sourced by: [vendor-supplied / found independently]

Implementation

- Their own resource load:

- Daily admin owner and time:
- What the team complains about:
- Process changes they had to make:
- What it does not do that they expected:
- End-user (candidate / employee / manager) view:
- Support, last real incident:

Commercial
- Post-signature surprises:
- Renewal experience:

Closers
- Buy again:
- Would switch if:
- Check before you sign:

- Requirements this call confirms:
- Requirements this call contradicts:

```

Feed the last block back into the scorecard as evidence, and mark which requirement scores
moved as a result. A reference call that changes no score was either a very good product or
a badly run call.
