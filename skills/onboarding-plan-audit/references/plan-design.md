## The outcome test

Every line in the outcomes column has to survive one question:

> **Could someone complete this and still not be able to do anything they could not do
> before?**

If yes, it is an activity. Rewrite it or move it to the support column.

| Activity | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Attend product training | Can demo the core product and answer the five most common objections without help |
| Meet the engineering team | Knows who owns each service and can route a production issue to the right person first time |
| Read the strategy deck | Can explain, in their own words, why we chose this segment over the adjacent one |
| Shadow three support calls | Has independently resolved five tier-1 tickets to the standard quality bar |
| Get access to the data warehouse | Has produced the weekly revenue report unaided and it was published |
| Have one-to-ones with stakeholders | Has a written view of the top three risks in the function, shared with the manager |
| Complete onboarding checklist | — (delete; this is administrative and belongs in the pre-boarding section) |

Two rules that keep the column honest:

**Write outcomes in the form "can do X" or "has delivered X".** Those are the only two
shapes an outcome takes: a demonstrated capability, or a completed thing. Anything phrased
as "understands", "is familiar with", "has been introduced to" is an activity in disguise —
push it one step further until it says what the understanding enables.

**Keep the number small.** Three to five outcomes per phase. A phase with eleven has none,
because nobody prioritises eleven things and the plan becomes a list to feel guilty about.
The outcomes are the things you would defend if the person could only achieve some of them.

**Activities still belong in the plan** — a demo can only be given by someone who attended
the training. They sit in the "what they need from others" column, as the support that makes
the outcome achievable. That placement is the point: it makes them the organisation's
obligation rather than the new hire's homework.

## Anatomy of a phase

Each of the three phases carries five elements. Never fewer.

**1. Outcomes.** Three to five. Capability or delivery. Written so that at the checkpoint it
is obvious whether they happened.

**2. What they need from others to get there.** Access, introductions, context, training,
decisions, budget, sign-off. This column is what turns a plan from a list of demands into a
mutual contract, and it is where most plans quietly fail — the new hire is held to outcomes
that depended on an access request nobody submitted. Name the person who owes each item.

**3. People to meet, and why.** Named, with the reason and the question to ask. Twelve
purposeless coffees teach nothing; six with a question each build the map of how the
organisation actually works. For each, note who makes the introduction — the manager should,
not the new hire.

**4. Checkpoints.** Date, participants, what is reviewed. In the diary before day one.

**5. What "on track" looks like — and what off-track looks like.** The observable signal.
Write both, because the value is a manager being able to tell the difference in week five
rather than month five. Off-track signals are things like: still asking questions they were
told the answer to in week one; not yet delivered anything; has not been invited to the
forums where their work is discussed; the team is still routing around them.

## Calibrating the plan to role and level

The 90-day shape is not the same for every hire. Adjust on three dimensions.

**Seniority.** A junior hire's plan is weighted towards capability and supervised delivery;
outcomes are concrete and close. A senior hire's plan is weighted towards diagnosis,
relationships and one or two visible early decisions; their outcomes are judgements and
plans, not tickets closed. The more senior, the more the plan is about the organisation's
obligations to them (access, context, mandate) and the less about training.

**Complexity of domain.** Regulated, technical or highly-contextual domains take longer to
first independent delivery, and the plan should say so rather than compress it into 90 days
and produce a failure. Where genuine competence takes six months, write a 30/60/90 that is
honest about being the first third.

**New role versus backfill.** A backfill inherits a defined job, an existing team's
expectations and often a handover. A new role means the first 90 days include defining the
job, and the plan should carry an explicit outcome about the scope being agreed and
communicated — otherwise the person spends six months negotiating their own remit while
being judged on delivery.

**Internal move.** Cut the administrative and company-context strands almost entirely, keep
role productivity, and *do not* cut integration — an internal mover joining a new function
has a new set of relationships and a new set of unwritten norms, and the assumption that
they are already onboarded is why internal moves fail.

## Defining "productive" for this role

Do this in week one, as an agreement between manager and new hire, and write it into the
plan. It anchors the checkpoints and it is the only way the time-to-productivity measure
means anything.

The definition should be:

- **One observable milestone**, not a basket of things.
- **Role-specific.** Generic definitions are why the metric is usually useless.
- **Independent.** The milestone is reached when they do the thing without supervision, not
  when they do it with help.
- **Agreed by both**, at the start, before anyone has an interest in where the line sits.

Shapes that work: handles a tier-1 escalation end to end without escalating; ships to
production unsupervised; runs the monthly close with review rather than instruction; closes
a deal they sourced and worked themselves; presents at the leadership forum on their own
area; signs off a design without a second reviewer; takes the on-call rota.

Then measure elapsed time to it, and track the median by role family. Where the milestone
takes materially longer or shorter than expected, the interesting question is which of the
three strands failed — not whether the person was any good.

## Worked archetype — individual contributor

Mid-level, established team, backfill. Adapt the outcomes to the function; the shape holds.

### Days 1–30 — learn and land

**Outcomes**
- Has delivered one small piece of real work end to end, reviewed and accepted.
- Can navigate the core tools and systems for the role without assistance.
- Can explain what the team is responsible for, what it is currently trying to achieve this
  quarter, and where their own work fits.
- Has documented the gaps they hit in the existing onboarding material and where they got
  stuck.

**What they need from others** — all access working on day one; the first piece of work
identified before they arrive, with a named reviewer; a walkthrough of the systems from
someone who uses them daily; the team's current quarter goals in writing.

**People to meet** — the manager (daily, short, through week one); the buddy; each immediate
team member, with a question about what they own and what they wish worked better; the two
or three closest internal partners the team depends on or serves.

**Checkpoint at day 30** — the first delivered work reviewed; what was missing from
onboarding; is the manager available enough; what surprised you.

**On track** — has shipped something; asks fewer questions than in week one and better ones;
is being included by the team without prompting. **Off track** — still no delivered work; the
team is routing work around them; questions are still about basic mechanics.

### Days 31–60 — contribute

**Outcomes**
- Owns a defined area of work and is delivering it at the team's normal cadence.
- Can handle the routine case in that area without supervision.
- Has taken part in the team's review or quality process as a reviewer, not just a reviewee.
- Knows who to go to for the five most common blockers, and does so without routing through
  the manager.

**What they need from others** — a defined area handed over explicitly; inclusion in the
relevant forums; feedback on the first weeks of delivery that is specific enough to act on.

**Checkpoint at day 60** — is the ownership real; where is the quality bar not yet met; what
is still slower than it should be and why.

### Days 61–90 — own and improve

**Outcomes**
- Delivers independently in the core of the role at the expected standard and pace — the
  agreed definition of "productive" is met.
- Has identified and made one improvement to how the team works, based on what they saw
  arriving from outside.
- Has updated the onboarding material for the next person in this role.

**Checkpoint at day 90** — against the agreed productivity milestone; two-way feedback; is
the job what they were told it would be; what the next 90 days hold.

## Worked archetype — first-line manager

The hardest plan to write, because the temptation is to have them change things in week two.
The outcomes below are deliberately weighted towards diagnosis before action — a manager who
restructures a team they do not yet understand spends months undoing it.

### Days 1–30 — understand the team and the work

**Outcomes**
- Has held a first one-to-one with every direct report and can state, for each, what they
  work on, what they want next, and what they think is broken.
- Can describe what the team delivers, its current commitments, and its two biggest
  constraints.
- Has taken over the team's existing rhythms without changing them, and can run them.
- Has a written view of the team's strengths and gaps, shared with their own manager.

**What they need from others** — a handover from the predecessor or interim if one exists;
clarity on their own decision rights, budget and hiring authority *in writing*; performance
and comp context for each report from HR, with the previous ratings and any live issues; the
history of anything currently contentious in the team.

**People to meet** — every direct report; their own manager (weekly, protected); peer
managers, with the question "what do you need from my team that you are not getting"; the
team's main internal customers; the HR or People partner.

**Checkpoint at day 30** — the written team view; anything urgent surfaced in the
one-to-ones; is their mandate clear enough to act on.

**On track** — the team is bringing them problems; they have not yet reorganised anything.
**Off track** — has made structural changes before day 30; has not met everyone; is still
doing the individual contributor work they were hired away from.

### Days 31–60 — take ownership and set direction

**Outcomes**
- Owns the team's delivery commitments and is accountable for them in the normal forums.
- Has set and communicated the team's priorities for the coming quarter, aligned with their
  manager.
- Has run at least one difficult conversation that was waiting for them — a performance
  issue, a stalled decision, an unresolved conflict.
- Has made one deliberate change with a stated reason, and told the team why.

**What they need from others** — sign-off on the priorities; support on the difficult
conversation from HR if it is a performance matter; visible backing from their own manager
in the forums.

### Days 61–90 — lead

**Outcomes**
- The team's delivery is meeting commitments without escalation to their manager.
- Every report has clear objectives and knows how they are doing.
- Has a resourcing and development view for the team over the next two quarters, agreed with
  their manager.
- Meets the agreed definition of "productive" for the role.

## Worked archetype — senior or executive hire

Different problem entirely. A senior hire is not learning to do the job; they are building
the political and organisational understanding required to use skills they already have. The
common failure is either paralysis — six months of listening — or a strategy presented in
week three that lands badly because it was built without context. The plan should force
early visible value alongside genuine diagnosis.

Note that pre-boarding matters more here than anywhere: long notice periods, and a
relationship that decays to nothing across three months of silence.

### Days 1–30 — listen, map, and be visible

**Outcomes**
- Has met the leadership team, the function, and the key external stakeholders, and can
  describe how decisions actually get made here — including which forums matter and who
  holds informal veto.
- Can state the top three problems in their remit as the organisation sees them, and where
  those views conflict.
- Has confirmed their mandate in writing with the CEO or their manager: decision rights,
  budget, headcount, what they may change unilaterally and what needs agreement.
- Has been visible to their own function — has spoken to the whole team about how they work
  and what they will do first.

**What they need from others** — a stakeholder map with the history; access to the numbers
before day one where confidentiality allows; their manager's honest read on where the
function is failing; an introduction from the CEO or manager that confers authority; a
standing weekly with their manager for the first quarter.

**People to meet** — the exec peers, individually, each with "what do you need from my
function that you are not getting, and what should I not break"; their direct reports; a
sample of the function two levels down; the board member or investor with the closest
interest, if relevant; key customers or partners.

**On track** — is being consulted on decisions in their remit by day 30. **Off track** — has
announced a restructure; or has been in the building a month and no one outside their
function can say what they think.

### Days 31–60 — diagnose and propose

**Outcomes**
- Has a written diagnosis of the function — what is working, what is not, what it costs —
  shared with their manager and tested with peers before it is presented widely.
- Has made one or two early decisions that were waiting for a decision-maker, demonstrating
  how they operate.
- Has an initial view on the team: who is in the right role, where the gaps are, what needs
  to change and on what timescale.

**What they need from others** — honest data, including the numbers that are embarrassing;
time with their manager to test the diagnosis privately before it is public; HR support on
anything touching people changes.

### Days 61–90 — commit to a plan

**Outcomes**
- Has an agreed plan for the function with named priorities, resourcing and a timeline,
  signed off by their manager and understood by their peers.
- Has communicated it to their own function, and the function can repeat it back.
- Has made the people changes that are clearly necessary, or has a dated plan for them with
  HR and legal engaged.
- Is running their part of the business without their manager needing to be in the detail.

**Checkpoint at day 90** — the plan; the relationships (ask the peers, not only the hire);
whether the mandate turned out to be real; whether the role is what was described in the
process.

Any people changes at this stage — restructures, exits, role changes — go through local HR
and employment counsel before anything is communicated. Consultation obligations and notice
requirements vary sharply by jurisdiction and a new executive moving fast is a well-worn
route to a claim.

## Worked archetype — sales role with measurable ramp

The one archetype where ramp is genuinely quantifiable, which makes the plan both easier to
write and easier to write badly — activity metrics are seductive because they are available
early.

**Derive the ramp expectation from the organisation's own historic data**, not from a
general benchmark: look at the last several hires into the same role and segment, and see
how long they took to reach a full quota month and their first closed deal. If there is no
history, say so and set the expectation with the sales leader as a stated assumption to be
revised after the first cohort. Sales cycle length is the dominant variable — a role with a
nine-month enterprise cycle cannot show closed revenue in 90 days and a plan that demands it
is a plan to fail.

### Days 1–30 — capability and pipeline entry

**Outcomes**
- Can deliver the core pitch and demo unaided, and has passed a certification or role-play
  against the bar the team uses.
- Can handle the five most common objections without help.
- Understands the ideal customer profile well enough to disqualify a bad-fit prospect
  without asking.
- Has begun generating pipeline: first meetings booked, and first qualified opportunities
  created, at a level agreed with the manager.
- Is operating the CRM to the team's data standard.

**What they need from others** — territory or book assigned before day one, not in week
three; a defined pitch certification with a date; call recordings of the team's best
performers; product and competitive training; marketing support and any inherited pipeline
handed over explicitly.

**People to meet** — manager (daily in week one); two top-performing peers to shadow, with
live call listening; sales engineering; marketing; customer success, with the question "what
do we sell that we then struggle to deliver"; a current customer.

**On track** — certified, and pipeline being created. **Off track** — no first meetings by
week four; still needs help with the standard objection.

### Days 31–60 — working the pipeline independently

**Outcomes**
- Runs discovery calls independently to the standard used in deal reviews.
- Has built pipeline coverage against their ramped target at the multiple the team uses.
- Has advanced opportunities through the stages that fit within the cycle length — for a
  short-cycle role, has closed first business; for a long-cycle role, has qualified
  opportunities at mid-stage with a credible close plan.
- Forecasts their own deals in the team's process and the forecast is broadly credible.

**What they need from others** — deal reviews with real coaching, not status reporting;
pricing and discount authority clarified; sales engineering time.

### Days 61–90 — producing

**Outcomes**
- Meets the ramped quota for the period, set from the organisation's own ramp history.
- Has closed business, or, for a long-cycle role, has a weighted pipeline that supports the
  first full-quota period.
- Runs their own deals end to end, involving the manager for approval rather than execution.
- Meets the agreed definition of "productive" — for most sales roles, an independently
  sourced, worked and closed deal.

**Checkpoint at day 90** — attainment against ramp; pipeline quality, not just quantity; win
and loss reasons on early deals; whether the territory and the quota were as described in
the hiring process. That last question matters: sales hires who leave early very often leave
because the number, the territory or the inbound support turned out to be different from the
sell, and that is a hiring problem this checkpoint can catch.

## The manager obligations brief

Written to be lifted out and sent to the manager as-is, at offer acceptance. Adapt the
detail; keep the specificity and the times.

> ### Your obligations for [Name]'s first 90 days
>
> You are the largest single factor in whether this hire works. Most onboarding failures are
> a manager who was busy in week one. Here is what is being asked of you, concretely.
>
> **Before they start**
> - Block your diary now: their first morning, a short daily check-in through week one,
>   weekly one-to-ones from week two, and the 30, 60 and 90-day checkpoints. Do this before
>   the start date is confirmed. If you cannot protect this time, move the start date —
>   that is a normal thing to do and a better outcome than a bad first month.
> - Identify the first real piece of work they will do. Small, completable in the first
>   weeks, genuinely useful to someone. Not busywork, and not a three-month project.
> - Confirm their access, equipment and accounts are set up and tested. Not requested —
>   working.
> - Write the list of people they should meet in the first 30 days, with the reason for each.
>   You will make these introductions; do not tell them to reach out.
> - Choose and brief a buddy: a peer outside your reporting line who volunteered, is well
>   connected, and has time.
> - Tell the team who is joining, when, what they will own, and what you want the team to do
>   in their first weeks.
>
> **Week one**
> - Be there on the first morning. Personally.
> - Short check-in every day. Ten minutes is enough; the point is availability, not content.
> - Walk them through the 30/60/90 plan and **agree it with them rather than issuing it**.
>   They will improve it, and a plan they helped write is one they own.
> - Agree the definition of "productive" for this role — one observable milestone you both
>   sign up to. This is what the 90-day checkpoint is measured against.
> - Hand over the first piece of work, with the context and the name of who will review it.
> - Tell them how things actually work here: who decides what, which meetings matter, how
>   disagreement is handled, what "urgent" means, what gets rewarded.
>
> **Weeks two to twelve**
> - Weekly one-to-one, protected. Do not cancel it in the first quarter; a cancelled
>   one-to-one in week three carries a message you do not intend.
> - Give feedback early and specifically. A small correction in week two is a conversation.
>   The same correction in month four is a performance discussion.
> - Ask for feedback in the other direction, and act on something. "What have you not had
>   that you needed?" is the most useful question you will ask this quarter.
> - Run the 30, 60 and 90-day checkpoints on the dates in the diary. Review against the
>   outcomes in the plan, not against a feeling.
> - Introduce them actively in the forums where their work is discussed. Being invited is
>   not the same as being included.
>
> **If you are away**
> - Name a deputy, brief them, hand over the plan, and reassign the checkpoints rather than
>   postponing them.
>
> **What to escalate, early**
> - The plan is wrong for what the job turned out to be.
> - They are materially off track at day 30. Raise it then, not at the probation review.
> - Something they were told during hiring turns out not to be true. That needs fixing
>   upstream and someone should hear about it.

## Common ways a 30/60/90 goes wrong

**It is a checklist in a table.** Activities dressed as a plan. Apply the outcome test to
every line.

**It is written for the new hire only.** No column for what the organisation owes them, so
the plan sets them up to be judged on outcomes that depended on access they never got.

**It is issued, not agreed.** Handed over on day one and never discussed. A plan the new
hire helped shape is a shared contract; one they received is a list of demands.

**It is never opened again.** Written for onboarding theatre, filed, and rediscovered at the
probation review. The fix is the checkpoints being in the diary before day one.

**It ignores the sell.** The plan assumes the job as designed, and never asks whether the job
is what the person was told it would be. Put that question in the 90-day checkpoint; it is
the cheapest way to find a broken hiring promise while it can still be fixed.

**It confuses a development plan with a performance plan.** A 30/60/90 written for a
struggling hire mid-probation is a different instrument with different consequences. If that
is what is being written, say so and get local HR and legal review before it is issued.

**It stops at 90 days for a role that ramps in nine months.** Honest is better. Write the
first 90 days as the first third and say what the next stages are.
