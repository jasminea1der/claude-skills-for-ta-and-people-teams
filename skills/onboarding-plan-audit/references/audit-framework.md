# Onboarding audit framework

The ten lenses, in the order to run them. For each: what you are actually assessing, the
diagnostic questions to ask, the failure patterns you will find and the fix for each.

Work through them in order — pre-boarding first, measurement last — because the sequence
mirrors the new hire's experience and answers to the early lenses set up the later ones.

## How to run the audit

**The opening question that does most of the work:** *"Walk me through what happened to your
last new starter, from the day they accepted to the end of week four."* A narrative of one
real person populates most of the lenses and surfaces the gap between the documented process
and the lived one, which is where every finding lives. Ask for a second story from a
different team if you can get it — variance between two managers is itself the finding.

**Ask what happens, not what is supposed to happen.** The written programme is evidence of
intent. Only the story is evidence of practice. When the user answers from the document,
push once: "and did that actually happen for the last person?"

**Look for the two structural tells early.** First, who owns onboarding — if the answer is
"HR ops" or "IT", strands two and three are almost certainly unowned. Second, when the
process ends — if it ends at day one, week one, or "when the induction session is done",
the programme is an induction, not onboarding, and the finding writes itself.

**Rate each lens `Absent` / `Unreliable` / `Working`**, not out of ten. `Unreliable` is the
most common and most useful rating: it means the thing exists but depends on which manager
you happen to get, which is a design problem rather than an effort problem and has a
different fix.

**Do not rate a lens you have no information on.** Mark it `Not assessed` and list what you
would need. Invented ratings destroy the credibility of the real ones.

## Lens 1 — Pre-boarding

**Assessing:** whether anything deliberate happens between offer acceptance and day one, and
whether the administrative load has been moved into this window where it belongs.

### Diagnostic questions

- Who owns the relationship between acceptance and start date? Name the person.
- What contact does the new hire receive in that window, and on what cadence?
- Is the handoff from recruiter to hiring manager explicit and dated, or does it just fade?
- What proportion of the administrative strand is complete before day one — contract,
  checks, payroll, equipment, accounts, systems access?
- Is equipment tested and are accounts verified as working before the start date, or created
  on the day?
- Have you had a renege in the last year? What happened, and when did you first notice
  something was wrong?
- Does the new hire know where to go, when, who is meeting them, and what day one looks like?
- For anyone with a long notice period, what happens across those weeks?

### Failure patterns and fixes

**Silence.** The recruiter's job ended at acceptance, the manager's job starts on day one,
and nobody owns the eight weeks between. The new hire's only contact is a contract PDF and a
laptop-collection email. *Fix:* name a single owner for the window with a defined contact
cadence, and make the recruiter-to-manager handoff a dated event with the new hire copied in
so they can see who has them.

**Admin left on the doorstep.** Equipment ordered the week they start; accounts requested on
day one; the contract still unsigned at the induction. *Fix:* set a pre-boarding cut-off —
a date by which contract, checks, payroll, equipment and access are complete and tested —
and make the start date contingent on it rather than the reverse. This is the highest-leverage
single change in most programmes and it costs nothing but sequencing.

**Swag instead of substance.** A hoodie and a welcome video, no information. *Fix:* send what
makes week one intelligible — the team's current priorities, the product, who they will meet,
the vocabulary. Voluntary reading, clearly marked as optional.

**Asking for work before the start date.** Pre-reading that is really preparation, a "quick
call" that is really a meeting, a system they are asked to log into. *Fix:* keep it
voluntary and non-work. In most jurisdictions work performed before the employment start
date creates pay and liability questions; route anything that looks like work to HR ops.

**The counter-offer nobody saw coming.** *Fix:* a direct conversation two to three weeks
out — how did the resignation go, what did they say, how are you feeling. The signal is
almost always a drop in responsiveness before it is a withdrawal.

**Senior hires on long notice.** A three-month notice period with no contact means the
relationship built during the process has fully decayed by day one. *Fix:* a monthly
touchpoint with the hiring manager, plus early sight of the strategic context they will walk
into.

## Lens 2 — The administrative strand

**Assessing:** what the admin strand contains, when it happens, and how much of the new
hire's first week it consumes.

### Diagnostic questions

- List everything administrative a new hire must complete. When does each happen?
- How many hours of week one go to admin and compliance training?
- Is compliance training front-loaded into week one because it is easy to schedule, or
  placed where it makes sense?
- Who chases the incomplete items, and what happens if nobody does?
- How often does a new starter lack an account or a tool they need in week one? Which one is
  it, usually?
- Is there a single owner for the admin strand end to end, or is it split across HR ops, IT,
  facilities and payroll with nobody holding the whole?

### Failure patterns and fixes

**Admin is the programme.** The onboarding document is a checklist, and every item on it is
administrative. This is the most common finding in the whole audit. *Fix:* separate the three
strands explicitly in the programme document itself — the moment they are on separate pages
with separate owners, the emptiness of strands two and three becomes visible to everyone.

**Compliance training used as filler.** Six modules in week one because the person has
nothing else to do and it is easy to assign. This teaches the new hire that the organisation
does not know what to do with them. *Fix:* spread mandatory training across the first 90
days at a defined cadence, keeping week one for the work — unless a specific module is
genuinely a prerequisite to doing the job or a legal precondition to starting, in which case
name it as such.

**Fragmented ownership.** Four systems, four teams, no single view of whether a given new
hire is actually set up. *Fix:* one owner accountable for "ready on day one" as a single
status, whatever the underlying teams. A pre-day-one readiness check two days out, run by a
person, catches nearly all of it.

**No day-minus-one check.** Nobody verifies the setup worked until the new hire discovers it
did not. *Fix:* have someone log into the accounts and switch the laptop on before the
start date.

## Lens 3 — The role productivity strand

**Assessing:** whether there is a designed path from day one to doing the job, and whether
anyone has said what doing the job actually means.

### Diagnostic questions

- Is there a role-specific onboarding path, or does everyone get the same generic programme?
- What does "productive" mean for this role, and who decided? Is it written down anywhere
  the new hire can see?
- How long does it take a new hire in this role to become productive, and how do you know?
- What does the new hire learn in week one that they could not have learned from the careers
  site?
- Who teaches the domain and the customer context — is there an owner, or is it absorbed?
- Is there a map of the systems and tools with what each is for, or is it discovered?
- When a new hire has a question in week two, who do they ask, and do they know that?
- What do your new hires say was missing when you ask them at 30 days? (If nobody asks, that
  is lens 8.)

### Failure patterns and fixes

**Generic programme, role-shaped problem.** Everyone gets the same three-day induction
regardless of function, and the role-specific part is left entirely to the manager. *Fix:*
a shared core (company, values, systems, policies) plus a mandatory role-specific track owned
by the function. The function owns the content; People owns the standard that it must exist
and must be outcome-based.

**Information transfer mistaken for capability.** A week of presentations. The new hire has
been told a great deal and can do nothing. *Fix:* convert the calendar into outcomes and
insert a doing-task after each block of context. A person who has shipped one small thing
in week one has learned more than one who has attended nine sessions.

**"Productive" undefined.** Nobody can say what productive means for the role, so nobody can
say whether onboarding worked or when it ended. *Fix:* make the definition an explicit
agreement between manager and new hire in week one — one observable milestone, role-specific
(see lens 10). This single conversation improves the plan, the checkpoints and the metric at
once.

**Onboarding stops at week two.** The programme ends before the job begins. *Fix:* extend
the designed period to at least 90 days, with checkpoints. Ramp for a complex or senior role
runs well beyond that, and the plan should say so rather than pretend otherwise.

**No documentation, or documentation nobody trusts.** Every new hire rebuilds the same
understanding by asking the same people the same questions. *Fix:* make updating the
onboarding documentation an explicit outcome in the new hire's own 30-day phase — they are
the only person who can see what is missing, and the window closes fast.

## Lens 4 — Social and organisational integration

**Assessing:** whether relationships and organisational understanding are designed, or left
to chance and personality.

This is the lens most likely to be `Absent`, and the one most likely to explain a month-five
resignation from someone who was doing fine on paper.

### Diagnostic questions

- Who does a new hire meet in the first 30 days, and is that list designed or improvised?
- Who makes the introductions — the manager, or is the new hire told to reach out?
- Does anyone explain how decisions actually get made here, as opposed to the org chart?
- How does a new hire learn the unwritten norms — meeting culture, how disagreement is
  handled, what "urgent" means, whether people reply at night?
- Who does a new hire eat lunch with in week one, and who arranged that?
- Do new hires from different teams meet each other, or does each one onboard alone?
- Where does a new joiner ask a question they are slightly embarrassed to ask?
- For anyone remote: see lens 9, where this lens does most of its failing.

### Failure patterns and fixes

**Left to personality.** Extroverts integrate, everyone else does not, and the organisation
concludes the quiet ones were not a fit. This is also where onboarding quietly becomes an
inclusion problem: whoever most resembles the existing team integrates fastest by default.
*Fix:* design it. A named list of people to meet with a stated reason and a question to ask
each, introductions sent by the manager, and a check at 30 days on which ones happened.

**The org chart substituted for the map.** The new hire is given the reporting structure and
left to discover that the real decisions happen in a Thursday forum they were not told about.
*Fix:* an explicit "how things actually work here" session with the manager or a senior peer
— who decides what, where decisions are made, which forums matter, who to consult before
proposing something, what gets rewarded, what gets quietly punished. Uncomfortable to write
down, which is exactly why it is valuable.

**Twelve meaningless coffees.** A list of names with no purpose produces polite conversations
that teach nothing. *Fix:* every introduction carries a reason and a question. "Meet Priya —
she owns the data pipeline your reports depend on; ask her what breaks most often."

**Cohort of one.** Each new hire onboards alone and never meets a peer at the same stage.
*Fix:* where hiring volume supports it, a light cohort — a monthly new-joiner session, a
shared channel. Where it does not, pair new joiners across teams.

**Nowhere to ask the embarrassing question.** *Fix:* this is what the buddy is for (lens 6),
and it is why the buddy must not be the manager.

## Lens 5 — Manager accountability

**Assessing:** what is actually required of the manager, whether they are equipped to do it,
and whether anyone checks.

The manager is the single largest variable in whether onboarding succeeds. Most programmes
ask nothing enforceable of them.

### Diagnostic questions

- What is the manager formally required to do, and where is that written?
- Does anyone check whether they did it? What happens if they did not?
- Is time blocked in the manager's diary before day one — welcome, daily check-ins in week
  one, weekly one-to-ones, the checkpoints?
- Do managers get any guidance or training on onboarding someone, or is it assumed?
- Has this manager onboarded anyone before? Are they new themselves?
- What happens when the manager is on holiday, travelling, or in a crisis during week one?
- How much variance is there between the best and worst manager in your organisation on
  this? Can the user name both?

### Failure patterns and fixes

**Nothing is asked.** The programme is addressed entirely to the new hire and to HR ops.
*Fix:* a written manager brief with specific, time-attached obligations, issued at offer
acceptance rather than on day one. `references/plan-design.md` carries the full brief.

**Asked but not checked.** The obligations exist and nobody verifies them. *Fix:* a light
check that is genuinely light — the 30-day new-hire question "was your manager available in
week one" plus a completion check on the checkpoints. Publish the variance between teams to
the leadership team, not the individual scores. Managers respond to visible variance.

**Week one collides with the manager's worst week.** Start dates set by payroll convenience
land in quarter-end, a launch, or the manager's holiday. *Fix:* the manager confirms the
start date against their own diary, and moving the start date by a week is treated as
normal, not as a failure. A start date the manager cannot support is worse than a later one.

**The new manager onboarding a new hire.** Both are learning the organisation. *Fix:* name
it and add support — a peer manager or the skip-level takes part of the integration strand
explicitly.

**No cover.** Manager away in week one with no deputy. *Fix:* a named deputy holding the
plan, briefed, with the checkpoints reassigned rather than postponed.

## Lens 6 — Buddy or mentor

**Assessing:** whether a peer relationship exists, is defined, and is supported.

### Diagnostic questions

- Is there a buddy or mentor scheme? Is it consistent or team-by-team?
- How is the buddy chosen, and does the buddy get a say?
- Is the buddy's role written down, and does the buddy know what is expected — cadence,
  duration, what is in scope?
- Is the buddy in the new hire's reporting line? (They should not be.)
- Does the buddy get any recognition or time allowance for it?
- What is the buddy for — logistics, the job, or the culture? Do the buddy and the new hire
  agree on that?
- Does the relationship have a defined end, or does it just fade?

### Failure patterns and fixes

**Buddy in name only.** Assigned on day one, one coffee, never again. *Fix:* define the
role, the cadence (frequent and short early, tapering), and the duration, with a stated end.
Give the buddy three or four things they are specifically there for.

**Buddy chosen for availability, not suitability.** The person with the emptiest calendar,
who is often the least connected. *Fix:* choose someone well-networked, reasonably tenured,
doing similar work, who volunteered. A reluctant buddy is worse than none.

**Buddy is the manager, or reports to them.** The whole value of the buddy is being a safe
place for questions the new hire will not ask their manager. *Fix:* peer, outside the
reporting line, ideally one step sideways.

**No time given.** The buddy is asked to add it to a full workload with no acknowledgement.
*Fix:* recognise it — time allowance, mention in their own objectives, or at minimum their
manager knowing they are doing it.

**Nobody briefed the buddy.** *Fix:* a short buddy brief. What they are for, what they are
not for (they are not the manager, they do not assess), how long, and what to escalate.

## Lens 7 — The first meaningful work

**Assessing:** whether a real, completable, consequential piece of work is waiting.

The first shipped thing is where a new hire's confidence and their credibility with the team
both come from. It is also the fastest diagnostic available: if the first piece of work goes
badly, you learn something in week two that you would otherwise learn in month four.

### Diagnostic questions

- What is the first real piece of work, and was it identified before the person arrived?
- How long into the job before a new hire delivers something that matters to someone else?
- Is the first task sized to be completable in the first weeks, and does it touch the core
  of the role rather than sitting adjacent to it?
- Who reviews it, and is that review a genuine feedback conversation?
- Is there a fallback if the intended first task is blocked?

### Failure patterns and fixes

**Nothing waiting.** The new hire spends two weeks reading and asking for something to do,
which is corrosive in a way that is hard to recover from. *Fix:* the manager identifies the
first piece of work before the start date, as a named obligation in the manager brief.

**Busywork.** A task that matters to nobody. The new hire can tell. *Fix:* small and real
beats large and symbolic. Fixing a genuine bug, running one real customer call, producing one
analysis someone actually asked for.

**Too big.** A three-month project as the first assignment means no completion signal for a
quarter and no early diagnostic. *Fix:* a completable first thing, then the larger piece.

**No review.** It gets done and disappears. *Fix:* an explicit review conversation — what
went well, what to do differently, what it revealed about gaps to close. This is the first
feedback the person receives and it sets the norm for whether feedback happens here at all.

## Lens 8 — Feedback checkpoints

**Assessing:** whether scheduled two-way checkpoints exist, happen, and change anything.

### Diagnostic questions

- What checkpoints exist, when, and who owns each?
- Are they two-way — does the organisation ask how it is doing, or only assess the hire?
- Do they actually happen, or do they slip? What is the completion rate?
- Does anything change as a result? Name one thing that changed because of new-hire feedback.
- Is there a route for feedback about the manager that does not go through the manager?
- How does the checkpoint sequence relate to probation and to the first formal review?

### Failure patterns and fixes

**No checkpoints, or only the probation review.** The first structured conversation is the
one with consequences attached, at month three or six. *Fix:* checkpoints at 30, 60 and 90
days, in the diary before day one, separate from and preceding any probation decision.

**One-way.** The organisation assesses the new hire and asks nothing of itself. *Fix:* every
checkpoint carries two questions in the other direction — what did you need that you did not
have, and what surprised you. The 90-day version adds the sharpest question available: is
this job what you were told it would be?

**Feedback collected, nothing changes.** New hires notice within one cycle and stop giving
real answers. *Fix:* close the loop visibly — tell the next cohort what changed because of
the last one. This costs almost nothing and is the difference between a survey and a system.

**No safe route past the manager.** Where the manager is the problem, the checkpoint that
runs through the manager will not find it. *Fix:* the 30-day check-in is run by People or
the recruiter, not the manager, and the manager sees themes rather than transcripts.

**Checkpoint conflated with probation.** A development conversation and a probation decision
in the same meeting produces a guarded new hire. *Fix:* separate them, and where a
checkpoint starts to carry performance consequences, treat it as a formal instrument with
local HR and legal review.

## Lens 9 — Remote and distributed

**Assessing:** whether the remote case has been designed, or whether the office process was
put on video calls.

Remote onboarding is materially harder and almost always an afterthought. Everything that
happened incidentally in an office — overhearing, hallway questions, reading the room,
lunch — now has to be deliberate or it does not happen. Strand three (integration) takes
nearly all of the damage, and it is the strand tied to whether people stay.

### Diagnostic questions

- Is there a distinct remote version of the programme, or the same one delivered over video?
- How does a remote new hire ask a small question they would have asked a neighbour?
- What does day one look like remotely, hour by hour?
- Does equipment arrive before the start date, tested, at the right address, with a fallback
  if the courier fails?
- Are hybrid new hires' in-office days coordinated with the people they need to meet, or do
  they come in and sit alone?
- For distributed teams: how much of the new hire's key context lives in someone's head in a
  different time zone?
- Is documentation good enough that a remote hire can self-serve, or does the culture rely
  on asking?
- Does the manager over-communicate deliberately in the first weeks, or apply the same
  cadence they use for tenured staff?

### Failure patterns and fixes

**The office process on video.** A day of back-to-back calls, which is exhausting in a way
the in-person equivalent is not, and none of the incidental contact that made the in-person
version work. *Fix:* redesign rather than port. Shorter sessions, real gaps, asynchronous
material to read at their own pace, and deliberate replacements for what was incidental.

**No way to ask a small question.** The remote new hire sits on a question for two days
because interrupting someone on video feels like a big ask. *Fix:* a named person on call
for exactly this, a low-stakes channel for it, and short daily open sessions in the first
two weeks — a standing fifteen minutes where the buddy or manager is simply available.

**Equipment logistics.** Hardware arriving late or to the wrong address is a much bigger
failure remotely — the person literally cannot start. *Fix:* ship early, confirm receipt,
test before day one, and hold a fallback path (a stipend for a local purchase, a temporary
machine).

**Hybrid days uncoordinated.** The new hire comes into an empty office. *Fix:* coordinate
their first in-office days with the people they most need to meet, deliberately, for the
first month at least.

**Time zones.** Key context concentrated in a region the new hire barely overlaps with.
*Fix:* name the overlap constraint explicitly in the plan, front-load recorded and written
material, and assign a buddy in a workable time zone even if the manager is not.

**Undocumented tribal knowledge.** Remote makes the cost of an oral culture immediately
visible. *Fix:* treat the new hire's first-30-days documentation contribution as a real
outcome, and route it somewhere permanent.

**No social contact by default.** In an office some social contact happens whether or not
anyone plans it. Remotely, zero happens by default. *Fix:* schedule it, keep it small and
optional, and have the manager or buddy initiate rather than waiting for the new hire to.

## Lens 10 — Measurement

**Assessing:** whether anyone measures anything, whether the measures are defined in a way
that means something, and whether the results reach anyone who can act.

### Diagnostic questions

- What is measured about onboarding today? Anything at all?
- Do you know your 90-day and 6-month attrition, split voluntary and involuntary?
- Do you know your time to productivity by role family, and how is "productive" defined?
- Do you ask new hires anything at 30 and 90 days? What is the response rate?
- Do you ask hiring managers anything at 90 days?
- Who sees the results, and has anything ever changed because of them?
- Can you see variance by manager, by team, by location, by remote versus office, by source?

### Failure patterns and fixes

**Nothing measured.** Common, and the honest finding is that the programme's effectiveness is
unknown rather than poor. *Fix:* start with the two cheapest and highest-signal measures —
90-day voluntary attrition, and a three-question 30-day check-in. Add the rest once those
run.

**Completion measured instead of outcome.** A checklist completion rate says the admin strand
works and nothing else. *Fix:* replace completion metrics with the four in the SKILL.md measurement
section, and keep completion only as an operational check on the admin strand.

**Time to productivity defined generically, or not at all.** A single organisation-wide
number is meaningless because the underlying roles share nothing. *Fix:* define it per role
family as a specific observable milestone agreed between manager and new hire at the start
of the plan, then measure elapsed time to that milestone and track the median by family. The
agreement is what makes it non-arbitrary; the conversation that produces it is worth more
than the number.

**Small numbers treated as statistics.** Ten hires do not produce a rate. *Fix:* say so, read
them as direction plus anecdote, and lean on the qualitative answers, which at that volume
are the better evidence anyway.

**Results go nowhere.** Reported into a People dashboard nobody opens. *Fix:* report to the
people who can act — hiring managers see their own, the exec team sees the variance between
teams, and the recruitment team sees 90-day attrition by source and by hiring manager
because it is telling them something about selection and about how the role was sold.

**Nobody looks at early attrition against the hiring process.** Voluntary exits before six
months carry information about the intake, the advert and the interview process, not only
about onboarding. *Fix:* review every early exit against what the person was told during
hiring. The gap between the sell and the reality is a recurring cause and it is fixable
upstream.

## Grading and sequencing the findings

**Severity is consequence, not effort.**

- **Critical** — plausibly causes early attrition or months of lost productivity. Typical
  criticals: nobody owns the pre-boarding window; no manager time in week one; no first
  piece of work; integration entirely unowned for a remote population; no checkpoints
  before the probation decision.
- **Significant** — degrades ramp or experience materially, but people get through it.
- **Minor** — friction and polish. Real, but keep it off the top of the list.

**Sequencing rule: consequence over effort.** Order the fix list so that the top three or
four are the ones with the highest consequence-to-effort ratio, and say plainly that these
are the ones to do. A list of thirty observations is an audit; a list of four with owners is
a change. Everything else goes in an appendix, honestly labelled as later.

**The fixes that are almost always cheap and high-consequence**, and worth checking for
first because they need no budget and no system:

- Move the administrative strand into pre-boarding.
- Put the manager's week-one time in the diary before the offer is even signed.
- Identify the first piece of work before the start date.
- Give the manager a written brief of what is expected of them.
- Design the "who you meet and why" list instead of leaving it to chance.
- Book the 30/60/90 checkpoints before day one.

**For each finding, write four things:** what is happening, what it costs (in ramp time,
attrition risk or manager time), the fix, and who owns it. A finding without an owner does
not survive contact with the next quarter.

**Name the variance, not just the average.** In most organisations the gap between the best
and the worst manager's onboarding is wider than the gap between the company's programme and
a good one. If that is what you find, say it: the fix is making the good version the
default, not designing a new programme.
