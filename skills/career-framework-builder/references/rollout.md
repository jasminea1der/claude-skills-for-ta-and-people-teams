# Rollout

Frameworks die at rollout, not at design. A good framework that nobody was mapped onto,
that managers cannot explain, and that made three senior people feel demoted in public is
worse than no framework — it burns the credibility needed to try again.

Read this before writing the rollout section, and always when the user asks about mapping
people or about someone whose title exceeds their scope.

## Sequence

Order matters more than speed. The common error is announcing the framework before mapping
anyone, which means every person in the function spends the following week asking their
manager "so what level am I?" and getting an improvised answer that later turns out to be
wrong.

A sequence that works:

1. **Draft the framework** with two or three respected practitioners in the function, not
   only managers. Practitioner input is what makes the functional depth section credible.
2. **Map everyone privately**, managers first, then calibrate across managers. Nobody is
   told their level yet.
3. **Resolve the hard cases** — over-titled, under-levelled, and anyone whose mapping
   changes their pay or title — with HR, the function head and, where pay or title changes,
   counsel.
4. **Train managers**, including scripts for the conversations they are about to have.
   Before any employee hears about it.
5. **Announce the framework**, with levels published, and tell people their own level in a
   1:1 within a defined short window — days, not weeks.
6. **Run the first promotion cycle** against it, and publish what happened in aggregate.

Steps 2 and 3 are what people skip. They are the entire project.

## Mapping existing people to levels

### Run it as a calibration, not a survey

Do not ask each manager to level their own people in isolation and collect the results.
Managers apply different bars, and the generous ones create a permanent problem: their
people are levelled higher for the same work, and once a level is communicated it is close
to impossible to take back.

Instead:

1. Each manager drafts levels for their people against the descriptors, with one line of
   evidence per person. The evidence requirement is what stops it being a gut call.
2. Managers meet as a group and walk through people level by level — everyone proposed at
   level 4, then everyone at level 5 — rather than manager by manager. Comparing across the
   function is the whole point, and going manager by manager makes that impossible.
3. Where two people at the same proposed level look clearly different in scope, one of them
   is mapped wrong. Resolve it in the room.
4. The function head and an HR partner sit in the whole way through, watching for
   consistency of bar rather than judging individuals.

### Map to current scope, not potential

The framework describes what someone is doing now. Managers will want to level people at
where they are heading, particularly people they like or are worried about losing. Levelling
on potential guarantees a cohort of people carrying a level they are not operating at,
which distorts every comparison afterwards and makes their next real promotion impossible
to justify.

The rule to give managers: *if you had to write the promotion case today, with evidence,
would it hold?* If not, that is the level below.

### Handle the edges deliberately

- **Genuinely between two levels.** Map down and name the specific gap. This is
  motivating when it is specific ("you are one cross-team project away") and demoralising
  when it is vague. It also gives the person a real promotion within a cycle or two, which
  is a better outcome than an inflated starting level.
- **Recent joiners.** Map at the level they were hired into unless there is clear evidence
  otherwise. Re-levelling someone three months in usually reveals a hiring problem, not a
  levelling one.
- **Long-tenured people with narrow scope.** The framework will map some very loyal
  people lower than their tenure implies. This is the point of the framework, but handle
  the conversation with care and never let tenure creep back in as a tiebreaker.
- **People in unusual roles.** Every function has one or two roles that do not fit —
  the person who holds together a legacy system, the operator who runs an odd process.
  Level them on the differentiators anyway. If the framework genuinely cannot describe
  them, note it as a known exception rather than distorting the framework to fit one
  person.

### Watch the distribution before you finalise

Once mapped, look at the shape. Two things to check:

- **Does the distribution make sense?** A function where three quarters of people map to
  the top two levels has either a level count problem or a bar problem. Fix it now, not
  after the levels are communicated.
- **Does anything look skewed by group?** If women, or any group defined by a protected
  characteristic, cluster at lower levels than their tenure and role would suggest, that is
  a signal to re-examine the criteria and the historic decisions that got people here. It
  is not a signal to adjust individuals' levels to balance the distribution — doing that is
  both a legal problem and an unfair one. Raise it with counsel and treat it as a question
  about the process.

Use employee identifiers rather than names in any mapping file that will be analysed or
circulated. It reduces exposure and the analysis does not need names.

## The over-titled person

The hardest part of the whole project, and the reason frameworks stall in month six. Every
company has them: someone carrying a title — often "Head of", "Director", "Senior
Manager", "Principal" — that the new framework maps below where the title implies.

They usually got there honestly. Titles were given during hiring to close a candidate,
during a retention conversation, in lieu of a raise, or simply because the company had ten
people and someone had to be Head of Something. The person did nothing wrong, and that
framing is the foundation of every conversation below.

### Decide the three things separately

The single most useful move: split what feels like one decision into three, because they
have different consequences and different urgency.

1. **Level** — where they map against the descriptors. Internal, drives band and promotion.
2. **Title** — what appears in email signatures and on LinkedIn. External signalling.
3. **Pay** — what they earn.

You almost never need to change all three, and the temptation to be "consistent" by doing
so is what turns a manageable problem into a resignation.

### The options, in ascending order of pain

**Map to actual scope, keep the title, keep the pay.** The default and the right answer in
most cases. The person is levelled honestly for internal purposes, nothing visible changes,
and pay sits above the band as a red-circle case (see below). It costs some internal
consistency and buys the entire rollout. The cost is real but bounded, and the alternative
is losing people over a document.

**Map to actual scope, keep the title, and agree a path.** For someone genuinely close.
Name what would close the gap, in the same behaviour/scope/evidence terms as the framework,
and set a review point. This converts a demotion conversation into a development one —
provided the path is honest. Do not offer one to someone who will not get there; you are
just moving the conversation six months out and making it worse.

**Change the title.** Only where the title is causing active damage — it misrepresents
authority to customers, it blocks hiring someone above them, or it makes a whole level
meaningless because a "Director" is doing an individual contributor's job. Where you do
it, do it as part of a broader structural change rather than singling the person out, give
significant notice, and never let it become public as a demotion. In several jurisdictions
a title change may be a contractual change requiring consent or consultation — this is one
of the points to put to counsel before doing anything.

**Change the pay.** Almost never, and not as part of this exercise. Cutting someone's pay
to fit a band is a legal question in most jurisdictions, a resignation trigger in all of
them, and it makes every other person in the function distrust the framework. Use
red-circling instead: pay stays, the person sits above their band, and future increases are
smaller or zero until the band catches up or their scope grows into it. Document the
reasoning.

### The conversation

Manager-led, in private, before any announcement. The shape that works:

- **Start with what is not changing.** Pay, title, job. Say it first, because it is what
  they are worried about and they will not hear anything else until they know.
- **Explain the framework's purpose honestly.** It exists so promotion decisions are
  consistent and defensible. It maps scope, and scope is not the same as title history.
- **State the level and the reasoning**, against the descriptors, in specifics.
- **Say what it means practically.** For most people: nothing today, and a clearer path
  than they had yesterday.
- **Do not apologise for the framework or blame it on HR.** Managers who present it as
  something imposed on them signal that it can be argued away, and it will be.

What to avoid: doing this over email, doing it after the announcement, doing it in a group
setting, or letting the manager improvise. Give managers the actual words — see manager
training below.

### Where the person is a manager

An over-titled manager whose scope maps low is a harder case, because their own reports can
see it. Resist the instinct to solve it quickly with a reorg. Usually the right answer is
the same — level honestly, keep the title, agree a real development path — with the
addition that the function head, not just their manager, should be visibly invested in
that path.

## The under-levelled person

Less politically dangerous, more commonly missed, and the mapping exercise will find
several: people operating a full level above their title, usually quiet ones who took on
scope without asking for anything.

Handle them fast, and handle them first. They are the strongest evidence that the framework
is a fair instrument rather than a cost-control exercise, and they are also — because they
are good and under-recognised — the people most likely to leave in the next six months.

Two ways to do it:

- **Correct at rollout.** Level them where they actually are as part of the mapping, with
  the pay consequence applied. Cleanest, most credible, and costs budget you should have
  planned for — ask about this budget early, because "we found four people who are
  under-levelled and there is no money" is a bad conversation to have late.
- **Promote in the first cycle.** Slower but defensible, and it demonstrates that the
  cycle works. Only acceptable if the first cycle is close and the person is told.

Do not level them correctly and delay the pay indefinitely. That is the version people talk
about, and it teaches the function that the framework changes labels rather than outcomes.

## Grandfathering titles

The decision: do people keep titles that no longer match the new title scheme?

**Grandfather by default.** Taking titles away creates maximum disruption for a
presentational gain, and the framework's value comes from levels being right, not titles
being tidy. Let existing titles stand, apply the new scheme to new hires and to anyone
promoted from now on, and accept a transition period of two or three years where both
exist.

**The exception: where the old title actively breaks the new structure.** If "Director" is
held by four people at three different levels, the title carries no information and every
new Director hire creates confusion. There, converting titles is worth the cost — but do it
as a single announced structural change affecting a defined group, never person by person.

**Where you grandfather, say so explicitly and publicly.** Otherwise people assume their
title is under review and spend months anxious about it. One line in the announcement:
existing titles are unchanged; the new scheme applies to new hires and future promotions.

**Do not create a second promotion event out of it.** If someone's title changes to match
the new scheme without a change in scope, say clearly that it is a mapping, not a
promotion, or you will have set a precedent that titles move for free.

## Training managers

Managers are the framework in practice. If they cannot explain it, it is a document.

Run a working session, not a presentation. What it needs to cover:

- **Why the framework exists**, in terms managers care about: fewer arguments, faster
  promotion decisions, a real answer to "what do I need to do to get promoted", and cover
  when they have to say no.
- **How to level someone**, practised live. Level two or three anonymised real people as a
  group and discuss the disagreements. This is the part that actually transfers the bar,
  and it is the part that gets cut for time. Do not cut it.
- **How to write a promotion case** in the behaviour/scope/evidence pattern, with one
  worked example the group critiques.
- **The conversations they are about to have** — the over-titled person, the person who
  thinks they are a level higher, the person who is a level lower than a peer they consider
  equivalent. Give them the words. Have them practise the hardest one aloud.
- **What they must not say**: promising a promotion, giving a level informally before
  calibration, blaming HR, or comparing one person's level to a named colleague's.

The single most useful artefact for managers is a one-page level matrix. The full
descriptors are for promotion cases; the matrix is what gets used in a 1:1.

## Communicating it

**Publish the whole framework.** Partial publication — levels visible but descriptors
restricted — reads as something to hide, and the framework's main benefit is that people
can see what the next level requires. Publish it.

**Sequence: managers, then individuals, then the function.** People should hear their own
level from their manager before or at the same time as the framework goes out, not two
weeks after. The gap between announcement and individual conversations is where anxiety and
rumour live; keep it to days.

**Say what it does and does not change.** Explicitly: this does not change anyone's pay
today (if true); existing titles are unchanged (if true); the next promotion cycle will run
against this framework; here is when.

**Do not oversell it.** "This gives everyone a clear path to progress" invites a promotion
request from anyone who reads their descriptor and thinks they qualify. The honest version:
this makes the criteria explicit and the decisions consistent. It does not mean more
promotions, and in a company where the bar has been loose, it may mean fewer.

**Publish the promotion process alongside it.** A framework without a stated process —
when cycles run, who decides, what a case contains, how someone is told no — leaves the
most important question unanswered and invites people to lobby continuously.

**Say something about pay.** People will assume the framework is a pay exercise whatever
you say, so address it directly: whether bands exist, whether they will be published, and
when. If bands are coming later, give a date. Silence here is read as bad news, and where
pay transparency obligations apply in the user's jurisdictions, some of this may not be
optional — have the user confirm current requirements with counsel rather than assuming.

## First-cycle mistakes

The first promotion cycle run against a new framework sets whether anyone believes it.

**Promoting to prove the framework works.** The pressure to show the framework produces
promotions is real, and giving in to it teaches everyone the bar is soft. If the honest
answer in cycle one is two promotions, promote two.

**Letting managers advocate rather than evidence.** In the first cycle, the strongest
advocate wins unless the process forces otherwise. Require written cases in the same format,
read them before discussion, and make the person presenting cite the descriptor. Discuss
cases in a random or alphabetical order rather than starting with the loudest manager.

**Treating the framework as complete.** The first cycle will find descriptors that are
ambiguous and criteria that cannot be evidenced. Schedule a revision immediately after,
say publicly that it is scheduled, and make the changes small. A framework that is never
revised loses credibility; one revised constantly has none to begin with.

**Not closing the loop with people who were not promoted.** Every unsuccessful case gets a
specific answer against the criteria — what was missing, what evidence would change it, and
when it will be looked at again. Without this the framework has, from the perspective of
the person who did not get promoted, changed nothing at all except adding paperwork.

**Silence about the outcome.** Publish the aggregate: how many cases, how many promotions,
at which levels. Not names. It demonstrates the process happened and had a bar, which is
the only way people come to trust it.

**Letting the framework drift out of hiring.** If external hires are levelled generously
to close offers while internal promotions are held to the written bar, the framework is
dead within two cycles — and internal people will notice, because they work alongside the
new hire. Level external candidates against the same descriptors, in the same words, using
the same evidence standard. This is where the interview kit and the framework have to be
the same instrument.
