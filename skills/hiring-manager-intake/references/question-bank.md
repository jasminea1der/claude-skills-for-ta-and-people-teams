# Intake question bank

Every question here comes with the signal to listen for and the follow-up probe. The
signal is the point — a question you ask without knowing what a good answer sounds like is
just conversation.

Do not ask all of these. A 45-minute intake supports roughly 20 to 25 questions including
follow-ups. Pick the ones this role needs and let the answers steer you.

## 1. The business problem

**"If this seat is still empty in six months, what doesn't happen?"**
*Signal:* A specific consequence — a launch slips, a region goes uncovered, a director keeps
doing IC work. Vagueness ("we'd be stretched") means the role is a headcount allocation
rather than a designed job, which predicts shifting requirements later.
*Probe:* "What's happening instead right now, and what does that cost you?"

**"Why now rather than six months ago or six months from now?"**
*Signal:* A trigger event — funding, a departure, a new product line, a board commitment.
A clear trigger usually means clear urgency and clear sponsorship.
*Probe:* "Is there a date this has to be done by, and what's driving that date?"

**"What have you already tried instead of hiring?"**
*Signal:* Contractors, an internal move, an agency, redistributing the work. Tells you what
the alternative is if the search stalls, which tells you how much leverage you have on comp
and process.
*Probe:* "Why didn't that work?"

**"Who asked for this role — you, or someone above you?"**
*Signal:* Distinguishes a role the manager wants from one they were given. A role imposed
from above tends to get less of the manager's diary and more requirement drift.
*Probe:* "Who else has a view on who we hire?"

**"Is the headcount signed off and in the plan?"**
*Signal:* An unfunded req that goes to market and then freezes destroys candidate trust and
the recruiter's credibility. Get a straight answer.
*Probe:* "Who signs the offer, and is there anything between here and there?"

## 2. History of the role

**Backfill: "Why did the last person leave?"**
*Signal:* Resignation to a competitor, internal move, performance exit and a managed
departure are four different roles. Also listen for whether the manager takes any share of
the reason.
*Probe:* "Would you hire them again? What would you want different this time?" — this is
the single fastest way to get real requirements out of a manager.

**Backfill: "What did they actually spend their time on versus what the JD says?"**
*Signal:* The gap between the two is where the job description is lying. Recruit for the
first.
*Probe:* "Which parts of that are you keeping in the role and which are you moving?"

**New role: "Who is doing this work today?"**
*Signal:* If nobody is, the role is genuinely new and you should expect the definition to
move. If the manager is, expect them to hire someone like themselves unless challenged.
*Probe:* "What are they going to stop doing when this person starts?"

**"Has this req been open before?"**
*Signal:* A failed prior search is the highest-value information in the room, and managers
rarely volunteer it.
*Probe:* "How many people did you see, and what was wrong with them?" Then push: "Was that
a pool problem or a brief problem?"

**"Is there anyone already in the frame — an internal candidate, a referral, someone you've
been talking to?"**
*Signal:* Ask this every single time. An undisclosed internal candidate or a founder's
favourite changes the entire economics of the search and, if it surfaces in week five,
burns external candidates and the recruiter's reputation with them.
*Probe:* "Are they a real contender or a courtesy process? I'll run it differently
depending on the answer."

## 3. Success at 6 and 12 months

**"It's twelve months in and this hire has gone brilliantly. What is true that isn't true
today?"**
*Signal:* Outcomes ("the onboarding funnel converts at a rate we're happy with and the team
ships without me in the room") beat activities ("they're running the team"). Activities mean
there is nothing for the interview loop to assess against.
*Probe:* "How would you know? What would you be looking at?"

**"What does month three look like — what have they done by then?"**
*Signal:* Reveals ramp expectations and whether the manager has thought about onboarding.
An expectation of full productivity at month three usually means they actually want
domain experience, whatever they said in the requirements conversation.
*Probe:* "Who teaches them the things they won't arrive knowing?"

**"What's the first thing you'd put in front of them?"**
*Signal:* A concrete first project. This is often the best raw material for the interview
loop's work-sample exercise, so capture the detail.
*Probe:* "What would a great first ninety days on that look like versus a mediocre one?"

**"What would 'this hire didn't work out' look like at month nine?"**
*Signal:* The failure mode the manager fears is usually the thing they will over-index on
in interviews. Knowing it lets you design a stage that actually tests for it rather than
letting it show up as an unexplained no.
*Probe:* "Have you seen that happen before in this team?"

## 4. The actual work

**"Walk me through a typical week. What's the split?"**
*Signal:* Percentages. A role described as strategic that is 70% firefighting will lose the
candidates it attracts within a year.
*Probe:* "How much of that is what you want it to be versus what it currently is?"

**"What does this person own outright, and where do they need someone else's agreement?"**
*Signal:* Real scope. Senior candidates screen on this harder than on title or comp.
*Probe:* "What's the biggest decision they can make without asking you?"

**"Who do they work with most outside your team, and what makes that hard?"**
*Signal:* Cross-functional friction is a genuine, testable requirement, and it is where
technically strong hires most often fail.
*Probe:* "Has anyone struggled with that here before?"

**"Team, budget, targets — what do they actually control?"**
*Signal:* Managers routinely describe a role as bigger than it is. Get the numbers.
*Probe:* "Is the team in place today or are they building it?" — a hire who inherits a team
and one who builds one are different people.

**"What's genuinely hard about this job that isn't obvious from the outside?"**
*Signal:* Legacy systems, a difficult stakeholder, an under-resourced remit, a founder who
changes direction. You need this to brief candidates honestly, and honesty here is what
stops offer-stage collapse.
*Probe:* "How do we talk about that to candidates without scaring them off?"

## 5. Must-haves versus the wish list

**"Give me the five things this person absolutely must arrive with."**
*Signal:* Start by capping it at five. A manager who produces ten has not prioritised, and
you will do the prioritising for them in the next question.
*Probe:* For each one: "Has anyone ever done this job, here or anywhere, without that?" If
yes, it moves to nice-to-have. This is the whole test.

**"Which of these can be learned in the first three months?"**
*Signal:* Tool knowledge, process familiarity and company context are almost always
learnable. Judgement, operating altitude and having been through a comparable situation are
usually not.
*Probe:* "Who would teach them, and do they have time?"

**"What's the hardest thing on this list to find?"**
*Signal:* The manager's own read on scarcity. Useful because it opens the trade-off
conversation without you having to introduce it.
*Probe:* "If that turns out to be the blocker, what's the fallback?"

**On any specific credential, tool or certification:**
*Signal:* Whether it is a genuine requirement or a screening shortcut a previous recruiter
was given.
*Probe:* "Is that a hard requirement or a way of describing the kind of person you want?"
This wording lets the manager back down without losing face.

**"Describe someone in the market you'd hire on the spot."**
*Signal:* Gets an integrated picture rather than a decomposed checklist. Managers describe
people far more accurately than they describe criteria.
*Probe:* "And someone who looks similar on paper but you'd pass on — what's different?"

## 6. The trade-off ranking

**"Every search trades between seniority, domain experience, comp and speed. If in week six
the strongest person available is short on one, which one do we accept?"**
*Signal:* The whole point of the intake. Push until you get an ordering, not a "they're all
important".
*Probe:* Use head-to-heads if they resist: "One has done it at twice the scale but wants
fifteen percent over band. One is in band and it's a stretch role for them. Which do you
interview first?"

**"It's week ten, the search has failed, we're doing the post-mortem. What went wrong?"**
*Signal:* The pre-mortem. People name the constraint they would not name directly — comp,
their own diary, an impossible profile.
*Probe:* "So what do we change now to stop that?"

**"If I could get you someone excellent in three weeks who is a stretch on seniority, or
someone perfect in twelve weeks, which do you take?"**
*Signal:* Isolates the speed lever, which is the one managers most often claim to care
about and least often behave as if they do.
*Probe:* "What actually changes for you between week three and week twelve?"

**"What would you give up to get this closed?"**
*Signal:* The blunt version. Works with commercially-minded managers and wastes time with
everyone else. Read the room.

## 7. Target profile and pool

**"Name me five companies where this person is working right now."**
*Signal:* If the manager cannot, they have not thought about the pool and the profile is
probably assembled from adjectives. If they name only three household names, the pool is
too narrow and you will need the adjacency questions below.
*Probe:* "Who else looks like those but isn't on the list — smaller, adjacent industry,
different geography?"

**"What are they called there? Titles vary a lot."**
*Signal:* Title mapping. Missing this loses you a large share of the pool for no reason.
*Probe:* "Would someone one level down at a bigger company be doing the same job?"

**Scale up / scale down: "Would you take someone doing this at half the scale? At ten
times?"**
*Signal:* Tests whether scale is a proxy for capability or a genuine requirement. Someone
from a much larger company often owned a narrow slice; someone from a much smaller one did
everything with no support. Both are real risks and both are testable in the loop.
*Probe:* "What would you need to see to be comfortable?"

**Industry: "Does the sector matter, or is it the problem that matters?"**
*Signal:* Domain requirements are the most commonly overstated. Separate regulated-domain
knowledge (often genuinely required) from general industry familiarity (usually not).
*Probe:* "Which parts of the context would they have to arrive with, and which could they
pick up here?"

**"Where are they allowed to sit, and how often do they need to be in a room with you?"**
*Signal:* Location and onsite expectations are the constraint most likely to be stated
loosely at intake and enforced strictly at offer stage. Pin it down now.
*Probe:* "Is that your preference or a company policy? What happens if someone brilliant is
two hours away?"

**"Anywhere I shouldn't approach — customers, partners, a no-poach agreement?"**
*Signal:* Off-limits list. Cheap to ask, expensive to discover later.

**"Anyone specific you already want me to reach out to?"**
*Signal:* Managers often have a mental shortlist. Get the names, and get permission on who
makes the approach.

## 8. Compensation

**"What's the band, and where in it would a typical offer land?"**
*Signal:* A manager who only knows the top of the band, or only the bottom, has not
discussed this with anyone. The midpoint is the number that matters.
*Probe:* "What would it take for someone to come in at the top?"

**"When was this band last checked against the market?"**
*Signal:* A band set at the last funding round or inherited from a previous org design is
a risk, not a fact. Log it.
*Probe:* "If the market comes back higher, who has the conversation and how long does it
take?"

**"Beyond base — bonus, equity, and what's the actual total?"**
*Signal:* Total package framing, and whether the manager can talk about equity credibly.
Candidates will ask; the manager should be able to answer.
*Probe:* "How do we talk about the equity to someone who's been burned by it before?"

**"If the person you want is fifteen percent above the band, what happens?"**
*Signal:* Distinguishes a hard band from a soft one. Also identifies the real approver.
*Probe:* "Who approves that, and how many days does it take?" A three-week exception process
loses candidates regardless of the answer.

**"What are people already in the team paid for comparable work?"**
*Signal:* Internal relativity. Bringing a new hire in materially above an existing team
member creates a retention problem the manager owns later.
*Probe:* "Does that create a problem we need to solve before we make an offer?"

Set the band from the role's value and internal relativities. Do not anchor it to a
candidate's current salary — that is unlawful to ask in several jurisdictions and it carries
existing pay inequity into the hire.

## 9. The interview loop and decision rights

**"Who needs to meet this person, and what is each of them assessing?"**
*Signal:* If two stages give the same answer, one of them is ceremony. If a stage has no
answer, that panellist is there for politics.
*Probe:* "What would you cut if we had to lose a stage?"

**"Who makes the call?"**
*Signal:* One name. "We decide together" is how searches stall at final stage.
*Probe:* "If the panel splits three-two, what happens?"

**"Who could veto this hire who isn't on that list?"**
*Signal:* Skip-level, founder, a peer whose objection carries weight informally. Find them
now and put them in the loop properly rather than discovering them at offer.
*Probe:* "Should they meet candidates early rather than at the end?"

**"What are you testing with a take-home or exercise, and could an interview test it
faster?"**
*Signal:* Exercises are the biggest source of drop-out among strong candidates who have
options. Keep them only where they genuinely assess something an interview cannot.
*Probe:* "How long would it take a candidate, honestly?"

**"How structured do you want this? Same questions, scored the same way, for everyone?"**
*Signal:* Sets up the handoff to the interview kit. Structured, consistent evaluation is
both the more defensible position legally and the one that predicts performance better than
unstructured conversation.
*Probe:* "Would you be up for a fifteen-minute panel briefing before we start?"

## 10. Service levels and capacity

**"How quickly can you give me feedback after an interview?"**
*Signal:* Get a number they say themselves. A named commitment is enforceable in a way that
"quickly" is not.
*Probe:* "And if you're travelling — who covers, or do we hold?"

**"How much interview time a week can you protect?"**
*Signal:* The most common hidden cause of a slow search is the hiring manager's diary, not
the pipeline.
*Probe:* "Can we block recurring slots now rather than scheduling ad hoc?"

**"What's in your diary in the next eight weeks — leave, offsites, board weeks?"**
*Signal:* Two weeks of annual leave in the middle of a search adds far more than two weeks.
Plan around it or plan a delegate.
*Probe:* "Who can run a first stage without you if that lands badly?"

**"From final interview to decision — how long?"**
*Signal:* The stage where candidates are lost to competing offers.
*Probe:* "Who else needs to sign before we can verbally offer?"

## 11. Rejection criteria

**"What would make you reject a candidate who looks perfect on paper?"**
*Signal:* Ask it in those words. It surfaces the real disqualifiers — the ones that never
make it into a job description and instead show up as an unexplained no in week five.
*Probe:* "Has that actually happened? Tell me about them."

**"Show me a CV you'd pass on that most people would say looks strong."**
*Signal:* Concrete calibration material. If they can produce one, you have a head start on
the calibration session.
*Probe:* "What specifically stopped you?"

**"Is there a background or a career shape that's an automatic no for you?"**
*Signal:* This is where consultancy backgrounds, big-company backgrounds, career gaps,
short tenures and non-traditional routes come out. Some of these are legitimate signals to
probe in an interview; none of them are legitimate as automatic screens, and some carry
real legal exposure. When one appears, do not accept it as a filter — convert it into
something the loop tests for.
*Probe:* "What's the underlying worry? Let's test for that directly rather than screening
on the proxy."

**"When you've got it wrong before, what was the pattern?"**
*Signal:* The manager's own failure history is the best predictor of what they will
over-weight. Useful for both the loop design and for knowing which of their objections to
take seriously.

## 12. Selling the role

**"Why would someone leave a good job for this one?"**
*Signal:* If the manager cannot answer, the pitch does not exist and outreach will convert
badly. Build the answer with them on the call.
*Probe:* "What did the last person who joined your team say made them come?"

**"What will the best candidates push back on?"**
*Signal:* Comp, remit, reporting line, company stage, a recent round of layoffs. Prepare the
answers now.
*Probe:* "How do you want me to handle that when it comes up?"

**"How involved do you want to be in attracting people?"**
*Signal:* A hiring manager who will do a fifteen-minute informal call with a passive
candidate is worth more than any amount of sourcing volume at senior levels.
*Probe:* "Can I put you in front of people before they formally apply?"

## 13. Closing the call

**"Here's what I heard — correct me."** Read back: the business problem, the three
must-haves, the trade-off ranking in their words, and the comp band. Four items, thirty
seconds.

**"Before I source at volume, can I send you three to five profiles for a fifteen-minute
calibration?"** Get a date in the diary before the call ends. Getting the date is the point;
proposing the idea without one achieves nothing.

**"When do we speak again?"** A standing weekly slot beats ad hoc updates and it is far
easier to agree at intake than to request in week three.

**"Anything I haven't asked that I should have?"** Cheap, and it regularly surfaces the
reorganisation, the pending departure or the budget review nobody mentioned.

## 14. Level-specific additions

**Junior and entry-level.** What can genuinely be learned here versus what must arrive?
Who mentors them? Are the stated requirements ones that anyone at that career stage could
plausibly have, or are they a mid-level profile at a junior price? What does the loop test
when there is little track record to assess?

**Senior IC.** What is the technical bar and who is qualified to assess it? Does this person
have a team, and if not, how do they get things done? What is the growth path — senior ICs
turn down roles that look like dead ends.

**First-line manager.** Are they inheriting a team or building one? What is the state of
that team — any performance issues or flight risks the new manager inherits on day one? Is
the manager willing to say that out loud to a final-stage candidate? Being honest here is
what stops a six-month regret.

**Director and above.** What is the mandate, and who agreed it? What is the reporting line
and is it stable? Who else applied or was considered internally, and how will they react?
What does the board or exec team expect from this seat? At this level the interview process
is also the candidate's due diligence — plan what they need to see, not just what you need
to assess.

## 15. Retrofit mode — re-briefing a stalled search

Use these when the search is already running and failing.

**"How many people have you seen, and what happened to each?"**
*Signal:* A pattern in the rejections. Ten rejections with ten different reasons means the
brief is wrong. Ten with the same reason means one requirement is doing all the work and
was never surfaced at intake.

**"Of everyone you've rejected, who came closest?"**
*Signal:* The most useful single question in a rescue. The near-miss defines the real bar
far better than any requirement list.
*Probe:* "What would that person have needed for you to say yes?"

**"Has anyone withdrawn, and why?"**
*Signal:* Candidate-side failure — comp, process length, the pitch — is a different problem
from a pool problem and needs a different fix.

**"What's changed since we started?"**
*Signal:* Reorganisations, budget changes, a shifted strategy. Managers often do not connect
these to the search until asked directly.

**"If we can't find this person, what's plan B?"**
*Signal:* Restructure the role, split it, promote internally, hire a contractor. Having plan
B named makes the trade-off conversation concrete rather than theoretical.
