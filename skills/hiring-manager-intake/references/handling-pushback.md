# Handling pushback

The positions hiring managers take that break searches, why each one breaks it, and how to
reframe it without damaging the relationship.

## How to disagree with a hiring manager

Four moves do almost all the work. Use them rather than arguing.

**Name the cost, not the error.** "That's wrong" starts a fight. "That'll work — it takes
the pool from roughly forty people to roughly eight, so we're looking at a longer search"
starts a decision. Managers are usually happy to pay a cost they can see; they resist being
told they are wrong.

**Always offer the alternative in the same breath.** A challenge without a route forward
reads as obstruction. A challenge with one reads as expertise.

**Convert the proxy into the underlying worry, then test for it.** Almost every bad
requirement is a shortcut for a legitimate concern. "Big company only" usually means "I
don't want someone who needs process built for them". Find the worry, agree how the loop
will test it, and the proxy becomes unnecessary — the manager keeps the protection and you
keep the pool.

**Do not try to win in the intake.** Get the trade-off recorded in their words. Evidence
wins the argument in week three, and it costs the relationship nothing. If the manager
holds, write it in the brief as an accepted risk with the consequence stated and move on.

One thing worth saying out loud early in a first intake with a new manager: *"I'm going to
push on a few of these. It's not to be difficult — it's so we don't find out in week six
that we were building the wrong search."* Permission asked for in advance is rarely refused,
and it changes how every later challenge lands.

## 1. "I'll know it when I see it"

**What is actually going on.** Either the manager has a clear picture they cannot
articulate, or they have no picture at all. Both are common; they need different handling.

**Why it breaks the search.** There is nothing to source against and nothing for the loop
to score. Every rejection arrives as taste rather than criteria, so nothing is learned from
it, and the recruiter cannot narrow. This is the classic route to a search that runs twelve
weeks and produces no offer.

**The reframe.** Stop asking for criteria and start asking for people. "Fair enough —
that's usually a sign the picture's clearer than the words. Let me try it the other way:
describe someone you'd hire on the spot. Where do they work, what have they done, what
would you notice first?" Then the negative: "And someone who looks similar on paper but
you'd pass on — what's different?"

Then close on calibration, which is the real answer to this position: "Let me send you four
real profiles before I start. You tell me yes, no and why, and I'll turn that into the
criteria. Fifteen minutes now saves us both six weeks."

**If they hold.** Fine — this is the one position where calibration substitutes entirely for
articulation. Run the calibration session earlier and with more profiles, and write the
criteria yourself from the manager's reactions.

## 2. "We need someone from a top-tier company"

**What is actually going on.** Sometimes risk transfer — a recognisable logo is defensible
if the hire fails. Sometimes it stands in for a real capability: operating at scale, working
inside real engineering rigour, having seen a mature version of the function.

**Why it breaks the search.** It shrinks the pool to the most heavily recruited and most
expensive population, and it is a weak predictor either way — those companies hire well but
they also carry people, and a strong performer inside a well-resourced machine may never
have built anything from nothing, which is often exactly the job.

**The reframe.** "What is it about people from there that you want? If it's rigour at scale,
that's testable and there are twenty companies where people get it. If it's the brand for
the exec team, tell me — that's a different conversation and I'd rather have it now."

Then offer the trade: "I'll include them, and I'll also bring you two from companies you
haven't named who I think match on the substance. Interview one. If I'm wrong, I'll drop
it."

**If they hold.** Log it, and put an off-list candidate into the calibration set. Revealed
preference does the arguing for you.

## 3. "5+ years of experience"

**What is actually going on.** A shorthand for "senior enough". Years are used because they
are easy to count, not because they predict anything.

**Why it breaks the search.** Time served correlates weakly with capability — someone can
have one year of experience five times. It screens out career changers and fast risers, both
of which are often the best value in the market. Rigid year thresholds can also disadvantage
people with career breaks, which brings avoidable legal exposure for no gain in signal.

**The reframe.** "What's the five years there to guarantee? If it's that they've owned a
budget and survived a re-plan, let's write that — someone might have done it in three, and
someone else might have done ten years without ever doing it."

Convert to evidence: instead of "5+ years in product management", write "has owned a product
line end to end, including at least one launch that missed and had to be recovered". That is
sourceable, it is interviewable, and it says what the manager actually meant.

**If they hold.** Treat the number as a soft signal for sourcing, not a screen, and say so
plainly: "I'll use it to prioritise, not to exclude. If someone brilliant is at four years,
I'll bring them and flag it."

## 4. "They must have done this exact thing before"

**What is actually going on.** A wish to avoid ramp-up risk, usually driven by pressure to
show results fast. Occasionally legitimate — a genuinely regulated or highly specialised
domain, or a role with no support around it.

**Why it breaks the search.** The pool of people who have done exactly this, at this stage,
in this sector, and want to do it again for you at your comp, is frequently in single
figures. It also selects for repetition over judgement: someone who has done it once may be
about to do it the same way regardless of whether that fits here.

**The reframe.** Separate the layers: "Let's split this into domain knowledge, the problem
shape, and the operating environment. Which of the three do they have to arrive with?"
Usually only one is genuine. The others are learnable in weeks, and the manager will
concede that once the layers are separate.

Then the cost, stated neutrally: "If we need all three, we're realistically talking about a
small number of people, most of whom are employed and comfortable. That's a longer, more
expensive search. I'm happy to run it — I just want us both going in with the same
expectation."

**If they hold and the domain really is specialised.** Accept it, and change the search
rather than the profile: build a named-target map, plan for a longer timeline, secure comp
flexibility up front, and get the hiring manager doing the outreach personally. Write the
timeline into the brief so nobody is surprised in week eight.

## 5. The profile that does not exist at that price

**What is actually going on.** The band was set by finance, by an old benchmark, or by
internal relativity, and the profile was written by the hiring manager. Nobody put them side
by side.

**Why it breaks the search.** It is the most common cause of a dead search and the most
predictable. Everything looks fine until week five, when the strongest candidates decline at
the comp conversation and the pipeline is exhausted.

**The reframe.** Do not assert the gap — evidence it, and give the manager the choice rather
than the verdict. If you have internal data on recent offers, declines and stated
expectations for comparable roles, bring it. If you do not, say so honestly and propose
getting it: "Give me four days. I'll come back with what the last five people at this level
actually wanted, and what we paid the last two we hired."

Then present it as a choice between four doors, all of which are legitimate:
1. Move the band.
2. Reduce the profile — take the stretch candidate and invest in ramp.
3. Change the offer in ways that do not cost base — remit, scope, title, flexibility, a
   clear path to the next level.
4. Accept a longer search and hunt for the person for whom your specific role is worth a
   pay cut. They exist; there are just not many, and finding them takes time.

**If they hold.** Log it as the top risk in the brief with the consequence named, and agree
a review trigger before you start: "If we're at week four with no candidate in band, we come
back to this conversation." A pre-agreed trigger converts a future argument into a diary
item.

## 6. "Just send me some CVs and I'll tell you"

**What is actually going on.** Usually time pressure and a belief that the intake is
recruiter process rather than the manager's own risk management. Sometimes a bad experience
with a previous intake that went nowhere.

**Why it breaks the search.** It outsources the definition to the recruiter and then
evaluates them on it. Rejections come with no reusable reason, and each round of CVs costs
a week.

**The reframe.** Trade time for time, and be specific about the exchange: "Twenty minutes now
or six weeks later — genuinely your call. Give me twenty and I'll bring you a shortlist you
recognise. Skip it and I'll bring you a longlist we'll both have to interpret."

If the manager truly has no time, take the compressed version — five questions, ten minutes:
the business problem, three must-haves, the trade-off, the comp band, the rejection
criteria. That is enough to start, and the brief you send back afterwards will generate the
corrections the meeting would have.

---

## 7. "We need this filled yesterday" plus a six-stage loop

**What is actually going on.** Urgency is real; the process is inherited and nobody has
looked at it against the urgency.

**Why it breaks the search.** Process length is one of the few time-to-hire levers entirely
inside your control, and long loops lose the candidates with options first — which is to say
the ones you want.

**The reframe.** Put the arithmetic in front of them without editorialising: "Six stages,
each needing a slot in four diaries — that's realistically several weeks of elapsed time
before we account for candidates having jobs. If speed is genuinely the priority, what do we
cut?"

Then offer specifics rather than a general complaint: combine two stages into a single
panel; replace the take-home with a live working session; give one panellist a written
recommendation instead of an interview slot; pre-block recurring interview slots so
scheduling is not the bottleneck. Do not simply delete stages — replace what each was
assessing, or you have traded quality for speed without saying so.

**If they hold.** Then speed is not actually the top priority, which is itself useful and
should go in the brief: "Loop kept at six stages; speed ranked third by agreement."

## 8. "Culture fit", "hungry", "high energy"

**What is actually going on.** Almost always a real preference about how someone works,
expressed in a way that has never been examined.

**Why it breaks the search.** Undefined culture fit is unscoreable, which means it becomes
the reason given whenever a panellist cannot articulate a real one. It also correlates with
similarity to the panel, and terms like "hungry", "high energy", "digital native" and
"recent graduate" function as proxies for age and other protected characteristics. That is
both a legal exposure and a straightforward loss of good candidates.

**The reframe.** Ask for the behaviour underneath: "Describe someone in the team who has it.
What do they do that someone without it doesn't?" You will get something concrete —
they push back on their own stakeholders, they ship without waiting for permission, they
chase the answer down themselves. Those are testable, and they can go in a scorecard.

Then make the substitution explicit: "Let's replace 'hungry' with what you just described,
because we can actually interview for that and everyone on the panel will score it the same
way."

Where a term is a clear proxy for age or another protected characteristic, say so once,
plainly, without moralising: "I'd rather not write that one down — it reads as a proxy for
age and it doesn't say what you mean. What you described is stronger anyway."

**If they hold.** The brief records the behaviours, not the adjective. What goes into the
scorecard is what the loop scores.

## 9. "I want to see ten candidates before I decide"

**What is actually going on.** Volume as a proxy for confidence, usually from a manager who
does not yet trust the process or the recruiter, or who has been burned by a thin pipeline
before.

**Why it breaks the search.** It forces the recruiter to pad the shortlist with people who
should have been screened out, which trains the manager to distrust the shortlist, which
increases the volume demanded. It also burns the manager's interview capacity on candidates
nobody believed in.

**The reframe.** Reframe from volume to confidence: "What would make you confident with
three? If it's knowing I've seen the market properly, I'll show you the map — everyone
approached, everyone who declined and why, and where the three came from. You get the
coverage without spending your diary on it."

Offer the market map as a standing artefact. Most managers asking for volume are actually
asking for evidence of coverage, and a map delivers that at a fraction of the cost.

**If they hold.** Agree it once and revisit after the first shortlist. Managers who see two
strong candidates rarely insist on ten.

---

## 10. "They're overqualified, they'll get bored"

**What is actually going on.** A real risk expressed as an assumption. Occasionally it is
something less comfortable — a worry about managing someone more experienced, or an
unexamined view about age.

**Why it breaks the search.** It rejects on a hypothesis about motivation without ever
testing it, and it disproportionately screens out people returning after a break, people who
have deliberately downshifted, and people leaving larger organisations on purpose. Motivation
is knowable — you can just ask.

**The reframe.** "It's a fair risk and it's testable. Let's ask them directly: why this role
now, what would make it worth their time, what would make them leave in a year. If the
answer's weak, that's a real no with a real reason. If it's strong, we've got someone above
the bar for the price."

**If they hold with a specific candidate in mind.** Push for the specific worry rather than
the category. "Is it that they'd be bored, or that they'd be hard to manage?" The second is
a legitimate thing to assess and a very different conversation.

## 11. Career gaps and short tenures as automatic screen-outs

**What is actually going on.** A shortcut for "will they stay" and "were they pushed", built
from a era when linear careers were the norm.

**Why it breaks the search.** Gaps disproportionately affect people with caring
responsibilities, health conditions and disabilities — screening on them creates real legal
exposure and removes strong people for a reason unrelated to the job. Short tenures often
reflect acquisitions, layoffs, startup failures and deliberate contract work, none of which
say anything about the candidate.

**The reframe.** "The worry is retention or performance, right? Both are askable. Let's ask
what happened and what they're looking for now, rather than screening on the shape of the
CV." One question in a screening call replaces the filter and gives you far better
information.

Also worth naming once: "I'd rather we don't filter on gaps — it's a group of people we'd
lose for reasons that have nothing to do with the job, and it's an area where we'd have a
hard time defending the process."

## 12. "I want a diverse slate" alongside requirements that prevent one

**What is actually going on.** A genuine commitment sitting next to requirements — a
specific company list, a specific university tier, an unbroken career, a narrow geography —
that structurally excludes the people the commitment is about. The manager has not connected
the two.

**Why it breaks the search.** The recruiter is asked to deliver an outcome the brief makes
impossible, and the failure lands on them.

**The reframe.** Connect them explicitly and without accusation: "I can do that, and to do
it I need us to look at two of these requirements together. The company list and the
degree requirement between them cut out most of the pool where the people you're describing
actually are. If we open those two, the slate builds itself."

Fix the process and the pool, which is where the change actually comes from: widen the
sourcing map beyond the obvious companies, remove screens that are proxies rather than
requirements, structure the loop so everyone is scored the same way, and check the advert
wording. Do not set selection targets that make a protected characteristic part of the
hiring decision — that is a legal question, not a recruiting one, and it goes to counsel.

---

## 13. "My band is fine"

**What is actually going on.** The manager is repeating what compensation or finance told
them, and may have no independent view.

**Why it breaks the search.** Bands drift out of date quietly. The search finds out first,
and by then five weeks are gone.

**The reframe.** Do not litigate it at intake — set the trigger. "Understood. Let's do this:
I'll track what candidates tell me they're on and what they're asking for. If it's
consistently above band by week four, I'll bring you the evidence and we can decide then."

That converts a stand-off into a data collection exercise, and the data collection is
something you should be doing anyway. When the evidence arrives it is your own, current, and
specific to this role — which is far more persuasive than any external benchmark.

---

## 14. "I'll do interviews when I have time"

**What is actually going on.** A genuinely busy manager who has not yet accepted that the
search costs their diary, not just the recruiter's.

**Why it breaks the search.** Scheduling delay is one of the largest components of elapsed
time in most searches and the most common single reason strong candidates go elsewhere.
There is no sourcing strategy that survives a two-week gap between stages.

**The reframe.** Make it concrete and make it now: "Can we block two one-hour slots a week
for the next six weeks? If we don't need them, I'll give them back — but if we're finding
them when we need them, we lose people." Blocking the diary while the manager is on the call
is worth more than any commitment they make about future intentions.

Then agree the fallback: who runs a first stage when the manager is unavailable, and what
happens when feedback is late. "If I don't hear back within two days, I'll assume it's a yes
to move forward" is a device some managers accept and it works remarkably well.

## When to hold and when to let it go

Not every bad requirement is worth challenging in one conversation. Pick two, maybe three.
A recruiter who challenges everything is heard as obstructive and loses the ones that
mattered.

**Always challenge**, because these kill searches or create legal exposure:
- A comp band that cannot buy the profile
- Requirements that are proxies for protected characteristics
- No decision maker, or a veto holder who is not in the loop
- A refusal to give any feedback commitment

**Challenge once, then log it** as an accepted risk with the consequence named:
- Brand and company-list requirements
- Years of experience
- Loop length
- Volume expectations

**Let it go and handle it through calibration**, because revealed preference will resolve it
faster than argument:
- "I'll know it when I see it"
- Vague culture language that you have already converted into behaviours
- A stated profile you believe is narrower than what they will actually accept

Anything logged rather than resolved goes in the open-risks section of the brief with the
consequence written next to it. That is what makes the week-three conversation a review of
an agreed risk rather than an argument about who said what.
