# Cost model

The financial spine of a headcount case: what a person actually costs, what the alternatives
actually cost, what doing nothing actually costs, and how to state a return that survives
being checked a year later.

## Principles

Four rules govern everything below.

**Every figure is either supplied, derived, or bracketed.** Supplied means the user gave it.
Derived means it was calculated from something supplied, with the working shown. Bracketed
means it is missing and marked as `[TO CONFIRM: description — owner]`. There is no fourth
category. Do not produce a plausible-looking number to fill a gap; a case that is discovered
to contain an invented figure loses the argument and takes the next three cases with it.

**Show the working for anything material.** One line under the number: "based on 14 agency
placements at an average 20% fee on an average £65k salary, from the invoices supplied".
Finance's job is to check numbers. Make it easy and they check quickly and move on; make it
opaque and they treat the whole document as advocacy.

**Round honestly and downward.** Precision implies confidence that a modelled figure does not
have. £180k is a better number than £183,470 when it comes from three assumptions. Where a
range is real, give the range and lead with the bottom of it.

**Prefer the smaller defensible number.** The case is not won by the size of the return. It is
won by the return being believed. A £200k claim that survives challenge beats a £900k claim
that does not, and the £900k claim also loses you the problem statement, the options analysis
and the timing argument, because everything in the document is now suspect.

## Fully-loaded cost

Base salary is roughly the largest single component and nowhere near the whole cost. A case
built on base salary gets recalculated in the meeting, in front of the approvers, and the
person who wrote it looks like they did not know their own numbers.

### Components

**Base salary.** Use the band midpoint or the offer you realistically expect to make, not the
band minimum. If the market is hot for this skill, say so and use the realistic figure —
being right beats looking cheap.

**Employer taxes and mandatory social contributions.** Statutory, unavoidable, and wildly
variable by country. Some markets add very little to the base; others add a third or more.
Get the actual rate from finance or payroll for the specific country and employment entity.

**Pension or retirement contribution.** Usually a defined percentage of salary, sometimes
matched to employee contribution rates, so ask what the actual take-up assumption is rather
than using the maximum.

**Benefits.** Healthcare, life and income protection insurance, allowances, wellbeing budget,
travel. Often held as a per-head annual figure by finance — ask for it rather than building it
up line by line.

**Bonus or variable pay,** at target, if the role carries it. Note whether it is provisioned
centrally or lands in the team's budget, because that changes whose number this is.

**Equity,** if relevant, expressed the way the business already accounts for it. Do not invent
a valuation method. If the company has a standard cost-per-grant figure, use it; if not, note
the grant size and say the cost treatment follows the existing policy.

**Recruitment cost.** Agency fee if external, or an allocated internal cost per hire if the
role goes through the internal team. Include advertising, assessment tooling and background
checks if they are separately charged. This is year-one only.

**Equipment, licences and workspace.** Laptop, phone, software seats, desk or office
allocation, home-working allowance. Year-one setup plus recurring licence cost. Small
individually and consistently forgotten.

**Management and onboarding load.** The manager and team time absorbed by onboarding. Do not
try to monetise it — it invites an argument you do not need — but name it, because it is part
of why the alternative of "just redistribute" is not free either.

### A useful shorthand, used carefully

Finance teams often carry a **loading factor**: a multiplier on base salary that produces
fully-loaded cost. Ask whether one exists before building anything up from components — if it
does, use it, because using the company's own number removes an entire argument.

Where no factor exists and one is needed as a placeholder, state it as a range to validate
locally, in the document, visibly: something in the region of 1.2× to 1.4× base is a common
starting assumption in several Western markets before recruitment and equipment costs, but
the true figure ranges well below and well above that depending on country, statutory regime,
benefits richness and whether variable pay is included. Never present a placeholder multiplier
as if it were the company's number. Write it as `[PLACEHOLDER: 1.3× loading — confirm with
finance]` so the reader can see exactly what is assumed.

### Present two numbers

- **Year one cost** — part-year salary from the expected start date, plus recruitment, plus
  equipment and setup, at the loaded rate.
- **Steady-state annual cost** — full-year loaded cost, excluding one-off recruitment and
  setup, including recurring licences.

Quote one and you will be asked for the other. Quote both and you look like you have run this
before.

## Ramp and first-year effective capacity

A person hired in month three is not eleven months of output. Modelling this yourself is
disarming, because it is the correction finance would otherwise make.

Model ramp as the proportion of full productivity delivered in each period. A simple
quarterly curve is enough — for example, a role ramping over two quarters might contribute
around a quarter of full output in its first quarter and around three-quarters in its second,
reaching full contribution after that. Use whatever shape fits the role and say what drove it:
complexity of the domain, length of any licensing or certification, how long the sales cycle
is, how long before a recruiter's first placements close.

Two things follow from this and both belong in the document:

**First-year effective capacity is well below one full-time equivalent.** Say so, and use the
ramped figure in any return calculation. Claiming a full year of output from a hire that
starts in April is the single most common overclaim in headcount cases.

**Ramp is an argument for deciding now, not later.** If the role takes eight weeks to hire and
two quarters to ramp, then a decision deferred by a quarter pushes full productivity most of
a year out. That is the strongest version of the timing argument and it belongs in the timing
section, not buried here.

Where the role is a backfill of a departing person with notice remaining, model the overlap or
the gap explicitly — a gap between leaver and joiner is lost capacity that has a cost, and it
is often the cheapest part of the case to make concrete.

## The alternatives, costed honestly

Write these as a comparison the reader can check. Give each option its genuine advantage
before its disadvantage — an options table where every alternative is obviously terrible
reads as theatre and tells the CFO the analysis was written backwards from the conclusion.

**Do nothing.** Costs nothing directly, which is precisely why it is the default and why the
cost of inaction section exists. Its real cost is whatever is currently being absorbed,
delayed or dropped. Sometimes this genuinely is the right answer for a quarter; if so, say
what changes to make it the wrong answer.

**Redistribute internally.** Free in cash terms, which makes it attractive to finance. Its
costs are real but indirect: something else stops, senior people spend time on work below
their level, and sustained overload has an attrition cost that dwarfs the salary in question.
To reject this credibly you need to name what has already been dropped. "The team will absorb
it" is only refuted by "the team has already absorbed the last two and here is what stopped".

**Contractor or interim.** Fast, no long-term commitment, no severance exposure, and comes off
a different budget line in many companies. Genuinely the right answer for defined-duration
work, specialist scarce skills, or covering a leave. Against it: the day rate is typically well
above the equivalent loaded salaried cost when annualised, knowledge leaves with the person,
they usually cannot own long-term relationships or line-manage, and in many jurisdictions
long-running contractor engagements on core work raise worker-status and tax questions that
need legal review. The honest comparison is annualised day rate against fully-loaded salary,
over the actual expected duration of the need. If the need is genuinely two quarters,
contractor may win — say so.

**Agency or outsourced provider.** Variable cost, scales up and down, no fixed commitment, and
someone else carries the management overhead. Against it: unit cost is higher, control over
quality and candidate experience is lower, and for TA specifically the spend recurs every time
the need recurs while an internal hire builds a compounding asset — a talent pool, employer
brand, hiring manager relationships. Model this over three years, not one; the first year
often favours the agency and the third rarely does.

**Offshore or nearshore.** Lower unit cost, sometimes materially. Against it: coordination
overhead, time zone drag on collaborative work, ramp is usually longer, and where existing
roles are affected there are consultation obligations that vary by jurisdiction and belong
with employment counsel, not in this document. Note where the work genuinely requires
co-location or local market knowledge — for recruiting roles, local market knowledge is often
the whole job.

**Automate or tool.** Sometimes the right answer, and a case that has genuinely considered it
is much stronger. Be precise about what a tool actually removes: most tools displace a portion
of a task, not a role, and they add implementation and administration work in the first year.
Where a tool has already been bought and is underused, expect to be asked about it, so address
it first. If the honest answer is that tooling handles part of the problem and the remainder
still needs a person, that is a credible, and smaller, ask.

**Hire at a lower level, part-time, or fixed-term.** Cheaper, and worth pricing rather than
dismissing. The question to answer is whether the work is genuinely divisible and whether a
more junior person needs supervision that consumes the capacity being released. If the case
survives at a lower level, proposing it yourself is a strong move — it shows the ask has been
right-sized rather than anchored high.

**Defer a quarter.** Always on the table, so cost it: the interim spend incurred, the outcome
delayed, and the ramp consequence from the section above.

## Cost of inaction

The argument most cases omit and the one that decides them. A CFO comparing a cost against
zero says no. A CFO comparing a cost against a larger cost engages with the trade-off.

### Tier the evidence and label it

Three tiers, and the document should make clear which one each figure sits in:

1. **Measured** — it happened and there is a record. Agency invoices paid, overtime run,
   penalties incurred, deadlines missed, tickets aged beyond SLA, people who left. This is
   the strongest material available and it is nearly always underused, because people reach
   for a projection when they already have a receipt.
2. **Modelled** — a calculation from measured inputs plus a stated assumption. "At the current
   backlog growth rate of X per month, the queue reaches Y by [date]". Legitimate, provided
   the assumption is on the page.
3. **Asserted** — professional judgement with no supporting figure. "Sustained overload in
   this team creates real attrition risk." Acceptable in small quantities and only when
   labelled as judgement. A case built mostly of assertions is an opinion with a cost
   attached.

Lead with measured. If there is any measured evidence at all, it goes near the top.

### The categories that carry weight

- **Cash already being spent to cover the gap** — agency fees, contractor rates, overtime,
  expedite charges, out-of-hours support. The best category available: it is measured, it is
  cash, and it is often close to or above the cost of the role, which makes the case almost
  self-proving.
- **Revenue delayed or at risk** — a launch that slips, a deal that cannot be serviced, a
  renewal at risk. Strong where the item has a name and a date. Weak when it is a share of a
  general growth target.
- **Contractual and regulatory exposure** — SLA penalties, remediation costs, audit findings.
  Express as exposure and likelihood, not as an expected loss, unless the business already
  models it that way.
- **Attrition of the overloaded team** — replacement cost plus lost capacity during vacancy
  and ramp. Keep it at team level and state the assumption about how many people, over what
  period. Do not name individuals or characterise anyone's health or personal circumstances.
- **Opportunity cost** — the work not being done. The weakest category, because it is
  unfalsifiable. Use it as colour, never as the headline.

### Do not do these things

- **Do not annualise a single bad month.** If last month's agency spend was unusually high,
  use an average across a stated period and say what the period is.
- **Do not count the same loss twice.** Capacity released, cost avoided and revenue enabled
  frequently describe the same hours from three angles. Pick the strongest framing and use it
  once. Double-counting is the easiest thing for a finance reader to spot and it discredits
  everything else on the page.
- **Do not attribute the entire outcome to the missing person.** If a launch slipped for four
  reasons and this gap was one of them, say so and claim a share. A partial claim that is
  obviously honest carries more weight than a total claim that is obviously not.
- **Do not extrapolate a trend past the point it is credible.** Backlogs do not grow linearly
  forever; people re-prioritise. Project to the next decision point, not to absurdity.

## Expressing return without overclaiming

### Type the return

State which kind of return this is, in the document, in these words. Credibility runs in this
order:

1. **Cash cost avoided.** Verifiable against invoices and budget lines. If this hire displaces
   £X of agency spend, £X is a real number that finance can check and take out of a budget.
   The strongest available claim.
2. **Revenue enabled or protected.** Strong when tied to a named deal, contract, launch or
   capacity constraint with a date. Weak when it is a percentage of a growth forecast, because
   the forecast is not yours and the attribution is arguable.
3. **Capacity released.** Real and worth stating — but hours are not money unless the business
   actually stops paying for something. Say what the released capacity will be used for. If
   the honest answer is "the team gets back to a sustainable load", that is a legitimate
   answer; converting it into a pound figure is not.
4. **Risk reduced.** Legitimate and sometimes decisive — key-person dependency, compliance
   exposure, single points of failure. Express as what the exposure is and how the hire
   changes it, not as a savings figure.

### Give a band, lead with the bottom

Where a return is modelled, present **conservative / expected / stretch** and put the
conservative figure in the summary line. It signals that the numbers were stress-tested, and
it means the twelve-month review compares against a bar you clear. The stretch case is there
to show the upside exists, not to be the headline.

### Net, not gross

Return should be net of the cost of the role and net of any cost that continues. If the hire
displaces 60% of agency spend, the remaining 40% stays in the model. If a contractor is being
replaced, the overlap period during handover is a real cost. Show the subtraction.

### Say what would make this wrong

One line, and it is worth more than another paragraph of upside: "This case depends on the
[X] plan holding. If [X] is cut, the role should be reconsidered at that point." Naming the
condition under which your own case fails is the most credible thing in the document, and it
is what makes an approver comfortable saying yes to the parts that are uncertain.

## Payback and the summary number

Where the return is cash-based, express it as a payback period: fully-loaded cost against
monthly net benefit, adjusted for ramp. "On the conservative case the role pays for itself at
month X" is the sentence that gets repeated when the user is not in the room, which is where
most decisions are actually made.

Rules for that sentence:

- Use the conservative figure, not the expected one.
- Include ramp — payback starts when contribution starts, not when the salary does.
- If payback runs beyond about eighteen months, lead with something else. A long payback is not
  a reason not to hire, but framing a strategic or risk-driven role as a financial return case
  invites it to be judged on the weakest available axis. Argue capacity, risk or delivery
  instead.
- If there is no honest cash return at all — many roles do not have one — do not manufacture
  one. Argue the role on cost of inaction, delivery risk and capacity. A case that says "this
  is a cost, here is what it buys, here is what happens without it" is entirely respectable.
  A case that invents a return is not.

## When the user has no numbers

Build the whole case anyway. Bracket every missing figure in a consistent format and collect
them into the assumptions block at the end, each with an owner. Typical list:

| Number | Usually held by |
| --- | --- |
| Employer on-cost / loading factor for this country | Finance business partner or payroll |
| Per-head benefits cost | Finance or People operations |
| Salary band midpoint for the level | Reward, or the People team |
| Agency spend for this team, trailing 12 months | Finance, or the TA team's own records |
| Contractor day rates currently being paid | Finance or procurement |
| Recruitment cost per hire | TA |
| Equipment and licence cost per head | IT |
| Revenue or margin per unit of capacity for this team | Finance, or the team's own reporting |
| Historic time to hire and time to full productivity for this role | TA and the hiring manager |

Two lines in the document beat ten. Tell the user which two or three figures would most change
the answer, and suggest they get those first — usually the loading factor, the trailing agency
or contractor spend, and whatever measures the outcome at risk. A finance business partner can
usually supply the first two in a single conversation, and those two alone convert most of a
placeholder case into a real one.
