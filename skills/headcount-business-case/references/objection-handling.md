# Objection handling

The objections that kill headcount cases are almost entirely predictable. This file lists
them, what is actually being asked underneath each one, and where the answer belongs in the
document.

## How to use this

Pre-empt inside the body of the document, not in an appendix. A "possible objections" section
at the end reads as defensive and invites the reader to treat the list as a menu. Instead, the
sentence that answers "can this wait a quarter" lives in the timing section, and the sentence
that answers "why not a contractor" lives in the options table.

Before finalising any case, walk the seven core objections and check each has an answer
somewhere a reader would find it. Where an objection has no good answer, that is worth knowing
before the meeting rather than during it — and occasionally it means the ask should change.

An objection is rarely a request for more detail. It is usually a proxy for one of three
things: *I do not believe this is the top use of this money*, *I do not believe the number*,
or *I have been burned by a hire like this before*. Answering the literal question while
missing the real one is why cases get deferred rather than refused — the approver has no
reason to say yes and no comfortable reason to say no.

## The core seven

### 1. "Can this wait a quarter?"

**Underneath:** cash timing, and a test of whether the urgency is real or rhetorical. Deferral
is the cheapest decision available to an approver and the default when a case does not close
it off.

**Pre-empt in the timing section.** Name the dated event the role is tied to. Show the lag:
time to hire plus time to ramp means a decision in Q1 produces full contribution in Q3.
Quantify the interim cost of the delay — usually contractor or agency spend that will be
incurred anyway, or the specific deliverable that moves. If a quarter's delay is genuinely
survivable, say so and name what it costs; volunteering that costs you this quarter and makes
every future case of yours more believable.

**Weakest answer:** "it's urgent". **Strongest:** "deferring to Q2 costs approximately £X in
contractor cover and moves [named deliverable] to [date]."

### 2. "Can we do this with a contractor?"

**Underneath:** avoiding permanent cost commitment and severance exposure, and a reasonable
belief that some needs are temporary.

**Pre-empt in the options table.** Give the annualised day rate against the fully-loaded
salary over the actual duration of the need. Name the things a contractor structurally cannot
do here: hold long-term relationships, line-manage, own a domain across years, retain
knowledge. If the need genuinely is time-bounded, propose the contractor — winning the
argument for a permanent role that then has nothing to do in year two is a worse outcome than
being right.

Where the work has been contractor-covered for a long time already, that fact is your
strongest evidence: sustained contractor spend on core work is both an argument about cost and,
in several jurisdictions, a worker-status question that finance would rather resolve.

### 3. "What gets dropped if we don't do this?"

**Underneath:** a legitimate test. If the answer is "nothing", the need was not real.

**Pre-empt in the problem and cost-of-inaction sections.** Answer it directly and specifically:
name the work that stops, slips or degrades, and if a prioritisation decision has already been
made, show it. This is a question you should want, because a specific answer is unusually
convincing — most cases cannot produce one.

Do not answer "the team will burn out" alone. It is true, it is not measurable, and it reads as
pressure rather than analysis. Pair it with the concrete thing that stops.

### 4. "Why does it have to be permanent?"

**Underneath:** permanent headcount is a multi-year commitment that is politically and legally
hard to reverse, and it lands on a headcount number the CFO reports upward.

**Pre-empt in the recommendation.** Show the need is structural, not a peak: is the volume
driver ongoing, is it tied to a permanent product, market or regulatory obligation, does it
recur every cycle. If the driver is a genuine peak, propose fixed-term and say why — a
right-sized ask approved beats a permanent ask deferred.

### 5. "What did the last hire in this team deliver?"

**Underneath:** pattern-matching. Somebody previously argued for a role in this team and the
approver either cannot remember what came of it or remembers it badly.

**Pre-empt in the measures section, and before the meeting.** If there was a recent hire, get
their outcome into a sentence — what changed, with a number where possible. If it did not go
well, say so and what is different this time; the approver often already knows, and
acknowledging it converts a trap into credibility. This is the reason the measures section
matters beyond this document: it is the answer to this question the next time round, so tell
the user to keep it and review against it.

### 6. "Why can't the team absorb it? / Why can't we redistribute?"

**Underneath:** a suspicion that capacity exists and is not being managed, which is sometimes
correct.

**Pre-empt in the options table with evidence, not assertion.** Show what has already been
absorbed and what was dropped to absorb it. Where there is a capacity or utilisation model,
use it. Where there is not, use the observable: overtime, queue length, delivery dates,
weekend working, the list of deprioritised items.

Be ready for the harder version: "should this team be doing this work at all?" Sometimes it is
a fair challenge. If the honest answer is that some of the work should stop, proposing that
alongside a smaller ask is a stronger position than defending the whole workload.

### 7. "What if the plan/forecast doesn't hold?"

**Underneath:** the approver has seen headcount added against a growth number that did not
arrive, and had to unpick it.

**Pre-empt in the return and assumptions sections.** State the dependency explicitly and name
the trigger that would make the role wrong. Offering a review point — "revisit at [milestone]
if [condition] has not held" — gives an approver a way to say yes to something uncertain. It
costs almost nothing and it removes the most common reason for a deferral.

## Objections specific to TA and People headcount

### "Recruiters are overhead. Why not just use agencies and pay per hire?"

Answer on total cost over three years, not one, and on what the internal hire builds that the
agency does not: pipeline, employer brand, hiring manager capability, candidate experience,
data. Use actual trailing agency spend, and model an honest displacement rate — an internal
recruiter does not replace every external placement and some searches should stay external.
Overpromise here and it is remembered in every subsequent budget round.

### "Hiring is slowing down, so surely you need fewer recruiters?"

Test whether it is true against the actual approved plan rather than sentiment. Where volume
has genuinely dropped, argue on the work that does not scale with req count — pipeline
building for hard roles, internal mobility, hiring manager enablement, systems and data — and
be prepared to right-size the ask. Where volume has not dropped, put the plan's hire count and
your realistic capacity side by side; that comparison does the arguing.

### "Why can't hiring managers do more of this themselves?"

Some can and should, and saying so is disarming. The counter-argument is time: manager hours
spent screening are more expensive than recruiter hours spent screening, and the outcome is
usually worse. Express it as capacity returned to the revenue-generating side of the house,
and be honest that it is capacity rather than cash.

### "Can't the ATS/AI tooling do this now?"

Take it seriously; a dismissive answer is heard as defensiveness about the function. Be
specific about which parts of the process tooling genuinely compresses and which it does not
touch, and note that tooling adds administration and vendor management in year one. If tooling
plus a smaller ask is the honest answer, propose it — and note that someone has to own the
tooling.

### "This is a cost centre. Where is the return?"

The available cash argument is agency and search displacement plus cost per hire. The
delivery-risk argument is usually the more powerful one: the hiring plan has already been
approved and funded elsewhere in the business, and TA capacity is the constraint on delivering
it. Framed that way the question stops being "should we spend more on HR" and becomes "do we
want the plan we already approved to happen".

## Objections in a freeze or a downturn

When there is a hiring freeze, "no" is the policy rather than a judgement about your case. The
case still has a job to do, but it changes shape.

- **Ask for the exception explicitly**, and find out what the exception criteria are before
  writing. Most freezes have them: revenue-generating, regulatory, contractually required,
  or a critical single point of failure.
- **Lead with cost of inaction**, because the comparison is now against a policy, not against
  another investment.
- **Look for a swap.** A case that is net-neutral to headcount or to cost — funded by an
  agency line, a contractor already engaged, a vacancy elsewhere, a tool being retired — is a
  fundamentally different conversation and is often approved when a net-new ask is not.
- **Ask for a decision that is not a hire.** Approval in principle, a place in the queue when
  the freeze lifts, or a fixed-term bridge. Getting the case on the record now means it is
  first in line rather than being written from scratch later.

## Reading the room

Different approvers ask different questions, and the same document has to survive all of them.

- **The CFO** asks about cash, timing, commitment and reversibility. They want the number,
  the payback, and to know what happens if it is wrong.
- **The CEO** asks whether this is the most important thing this money could do. Strategic
  framing and the cost of inaction matter more than the itemised cost table.
- **The functional exec whose budget it lands in** asks what they are giving up elsewhere.
  Know whether this displaces something in their plan before the meeting.
- **The board** asks about the shape of the whole plan, not a single role. Individual reqs do
  not go to boards; aggregate cases do, so if the ask is part of a wider plan, position it
  there.

Write the document for the CFO and put the CEO's answer in the recommendation paragraph. The
recommendation is the only part everyone reads.

## When the case has already been rejected once

A resubmitted case that looks like the first one gets the same answer faster. Establish what
actually happened before rewriting:

1. **What was the stated reason, and what was the real one?** Deferrals are frequently about
   timing or a competing priority rather than the merits, and the fix is different in each case.
2. **What has changed since?** New evidence, a worsened metric, a materialised cost, a
   departure, a contract win. If nothing has changed, the case is unlikely to change either —
   consider a smaller ask instead.
3. **Has the interim cost accumulated?** Agency or contractor spend incurred since the
   rejection is the most persuasive material available, because it is the cost of the previous
   no, measured. Lead with it.
4. **Can the ask be reshaped?** Lower level, fixed-term, part-time, phased, or funded by a
   swap. Approvers find it much easier to say yes to something they have already said no to
   when the shape of the thing has genuinely changed.

Say plainly in the reopened document that this was previously deferred, why, and what is
different. Pretending the first attempt did not happen is transparent, and the approver
remembers it better than the user expects.
