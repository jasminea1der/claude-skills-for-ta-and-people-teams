# Stage patterns

A catalogue of the stages that appear in most in-house loops. For each: what it is
genuinely good at assessing, what it is bad at, when to keep it, when to cut it, and how to
fix it if it stays.

Use this to argue a specific stage in or out. The arguments matter more than the verdicts —
every stage in a real loop has someone who added it and someone who defends it, and a
recommendation without reasoning loses that conversation.

Two framing points to carry into every stage decision:

- **No stage is good or bad in the abstract.** A panel is excellent with assigned
  competencies and a written rubric, and close to worthless without. Most recommendations
  in an audit are "fix it" rather than "cut it", and fixing is a much easier sell.
- **A stage earns its place by assessing something no other stage assesses.** That is the
  test. Write the sentence for each stage; if you cannot, you have your finding.

## Quick reference

| Stage | Best at | Worst at | Most common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Application screen | Hard requirements, volume reduction | Anything requiring judgement | Proxy criteria nobody wrote down |
| Recruiter screen | Motivation, comp, logistics, level triage | Craft depth | Becoming an unstructured chat |
| Hiring manager screen | Craft, level, scope, the real bar | Objectivity | Repeating the recruiter screen |
| Technical assessment | Specific applied capability | Breadth, seniority, collaboration | Testing recall rather than capability |
| Work sample | How they actually work | Anything not observable in the sample | Unbounded scope, unpaid, too long |
| Panel | Multiple independent reads | Anything, without assigned competencies | Four people assessing impressiveness |
| Presentation | Structuring, synthesis, exec communication | Day-to-day craft; disadvantages some candidates | Standing in for a real work sample |
| Culture/values | Nothing, unstructured. Specific behaviours, structured | Everything, unstructured | Rewarding similarity to the interviewer |
| Founder/exec final | Mission, strategic judgement, closing | Consistency, comparability | Unrecorded veto at maximum sunk cost |
| References | Corroboration, confirming scope claims | Prediction; discovering new information | Perfunctory, or run too late to matter |

## 1. Application screen

The CV or application-form sift, whether by a recruiter, a coordinator, a filter, or a
tool.

**Genuinely good at.** Checking hard, stateable requirements — work authorisation,
location, licensure, a genuinely non-negotiable qualification. Reducing volume when volume
is the binding constraint.

**Bad at.** Anything requiring judgement. It has minimal information, applies it fast, and
is where most unexamined proxies live: university, employer brand, career gaps, graduation
year, non-linear paths, formatting quality. It is the stage with the highest decision volume
and the least structure, which is a bad combination.

**Keep it when.** Always — you cannot speak to everyone. The question is never whether but
how.

**Cut it when.** Never cut it. Where volume is low, downgrade it to a formality and speak to
almost everyone; the stage is then a scheduling step, not a filter.

**Fix it by.**

- Writing the criteria down. Three to five, job-relevant, applied identically. If a criterion
  cannot be written in a sentence, it is not a criterion, it is a preference.
- Separating must-haves from nice-to-haves, and rejecting only on must-haves.
- Timeboxing the sift and reviewing turnaround: this stage is frequently where the largest
  block of dead calendar time sits, with applications waiting days before anyone looks.
- Auditing rejections periodically — pull a sample of rejected applications and have someone
  else review them blind against the criteria. Disagreement rate tells you whether the
  criteria are real.
- If any automated screening or ranking is in use, checking what it scores on, whether it
  has been validated, and whether a human makes the rejection. This has direct regulatory
  exposure in several jurisdictions and belongs in the report's legal section.

## 2. Recruiter screen

The first conversation. Typically 20–30 minutes by phone or video.

**Genuinely good at.** Motivation and what the candidate is actually looking for. Comp
expectations and whether the range works — the single highest-value thing this stage does,
because it prevents everything downstream being wasted. Logistics: notice period, location,
working pattern, authorisation. A first read on level and scope. And critically, selling:
this is the first and often only chance to make the job real before the candidate has to
decide whether to invest more time.

**Bad at.** Craft depth. A recruiter cannot assess whether a staff engineer is actually
staff level, and asking them to try produces false confidence in both directions.

**Keep it when.** Almost always. It is the cheapest stage in the loop and it catches the
mismatches that would otherwise cost everyone hours.

**Cut it when.** Rarely — but consider merging it into the hiring manager stage for very
senior or very low-volume searches where the manager is doing the outreach anyway and a
separate screen adds a scheduling round trip for little signal.

**Fix it by.**

- Giving it a structure: the same questions, in the same order, for every candidate, with a
  short scorecard. It is the highest-volume conversation in the loop and structuring it pays
  back more than structuring anything else.
- Assigning it two competencies it owns outright — typically motivation and a level read —
  so the hiring manager stage does not repeat them.
- Making the comp conversation compulsory and early. A loop where the range surfaces at
  offer has a design fault.
- Requiring the hiring manager to read the notes before their own interview. If they do not,
  the duplication is not the recruiter's fault and the fix is a handover, not a stage change.
- Giving the candidate real information: the team, the problems, the working pattern, the
  timeline and what each stage assesses.

## 3. Hiring manager screen

The first conversation with the person the candidate would report to.

**Genuinely good at.** Craft depth and level. Scope — the size, budget, headcount and
ambiguity of what the candidate has owned, which is what actually separates a strong senior
from a lead. Whether the manager can work with this person, which is not a trivial
consideration. And selling, at a level of specificity the recruiter cannot reach.

**Bad at.** Objectivity, structurally: this person is under pressure to fill the role and
has the strongest priors. Also frequently bad at consistency, because managers improvise.

**Keep it when.** Always. This is the stage most worth investing structure in, because it
carries the most weight in the decision.

**Cut it when.** Never cut. But where a loop has both a hiring manager screen and a hiring
manager deep dive later, question whether both are needed — often one is a CV walkthrough
and the other is the real assessment.

**Fix it by.**

- Assigning explicit competencies and removing the CV walkthrough. Chronology is not
  assessment and the manager has read the CV.
- Requiring the recruiter's notes to be read first, and stating which competencies are
  already covered.
- Anchored scoring, submitted before any discussion with the panel.
- Where the manager is inexperienced, giving them the question set rather than trusting
  improvisation — most managers are relieved rather than offended.

## 4. Technical or functional assessment

A live assessment of applied capability: a coding exercise, a systems design discussion, a
sales role-play, a case study, a portfolio review, a modelling test.

**Genuinely good at.** Applied capability in a specific, observable area, when the task
resembles the job. A live version also shows reasoning, how the candidate handles being
stuck, and how they take input — often more predictive than the answer itself.

**Bad at.** Breadth, seniority, and anything collaborative unless deliberately designed in.
Also bad at assessing people whose strength is judgement rather than execution, which is
most of what you need from senior hires. Live technical tests carry a real performance-anxiety
penalty that is unrelated to job performance.

**Keep it when.** The role has an observable technical or functional core, and the exercise
resembles the actual work.

**Cut it when.** It tests recall of things the job would let you look up. It is a puzzle or
brainteaser — those measure puzzle skill. There are two of them assessing the same
competency. Or the candidate is senior enough that the role's real risk is judgement and
stakeholder management, and the technical exercise is both uninformative and insulting.

**Fix it by.**

- Rebuilding the task from a real problem the team has faced, sanitised.
- Publishing what is assessed and the format in advance. Testing whether a candidate can
  guess the format is not a competency.
- Anchored rubric, two independent assessors where the stakes justify it, scored before
  they speak.
- Allowing normal working tools, including documentation and whatever the team actually
  uses day to day. A test conducted under conditions no one works in measures the conditions.
- Scaling to level: at senior level replace the execution test with a design or judgement
  discussion built on a real decision.
- Offering an alternative format for candidates who need one, assessed against the same
  criteria.

## 5. Work sample and take-home

An exercise producing an artefact: a written strategy, a document, a plan, a piece of code,
an analysis. Done at home on the candidate's time, or live in a working session.

**Genuinely good at.** Observing the work instead of the candidate's account of the work,
which makes it one of the highest-value stages available. Assessing how someone structures
an ambiguous problem, what they choose to leave out, and the quality of their written
communication — which is hard to assess any other way and matters enormously in most roles.

**Bad at.** Anything not visible in the artefact: collaboration, influence, pace under real
constraints, how they respond to pushback. Also structurally bad at being fair on candidate
cost — this stage is the largest single source of withdrawal in most loops that have one.

**Keep it when.** The role has an observable output, the brief is tight, the time budget is
short and stated, and it replaces rather than supplements a conversational stage.

**Cut it when.** It is unbounded, unpaid and long. It duplicates a live technical stage. It
sits early in the loop before the candidate knows the comp or the job. Or it is used at
senior level as a screen rather than as a discussion input.

**Fix it by.**

- **Moving it live.** A 60–90 minute working session with one observer gets the artefact and
  the reasoning, costs the candidate less, removes the "who helped them" question, and is
  the single best fix available for this stage.
- **Stating the time box and assessing only what fits.** Write it in the brief: we assess
  what you produce in this time, do not spend longer. Without it, the candidate with the
  most free time wins, which selects for unemployment and against caring responsibilities.
- **Paying for anything substantial**, where budget allows and volume is low enough.
- **Never using live work you intend to ship.** It is an integrity problem and candidates
  notice.
- **Placing it after the hiring manager stage**, so nobody spends hours on a job they have
  not yet heard described properly.
- **Following it with a discussion of the work** rather than a silent grading. The
  discussion is usually more informative than the artefact and it makes the exercise feel
  like a conversation rather than a hurdle.
- **Offering an alternative format**, assessed against the same criteria.

## 6. Panel interview

Multiple interviewers in one session, or a set of back-to-back interviews with several
people.

**Genuinely good at.** Multiple independent reads on different competencies, which is
genuinely valuable when the competencies are assigned and the scores are independent. Giving
the candidate a real sense of the team. Assessing cross-functional collaboration, when the
panel includes the functions the role actually works with — a partner who has been on the
receiving end of this kind of role can judge influence far better than the hiring manager
can.

**Bad at.** Everything, when competencies are not assigned. Four people assessing general
impressiveness produce correlated impressions, and the agreement gets mistaken for
reliability. A simultaneous panel is also intimidating in a way that has nothing to do with
the job, and it disadvantages candidates who are not practised at performing in that
setting.

**Keep it when.** Each member owns distinct competencies, scores independently before any
discussion, and the panel is the only place a genuinely cross-functional competency can be
assessed.

**Cut it when.** Nobody can say what each member is assessing. It duplicates the hiring
manager stage. It exists because "the team should meet them", which is a valid goal that
does not require an assessment stage. Or it is four people where two would do, in which
case shrink rather than cut.

**Fix it by.**

- Assigning one or two competencies per interviewer, with no overlap.
- Sequential 45-minute conversations rather than a simultaneous panel, where the format is
  intimidating and the schedule allows — same signal, better candidate performance.
- Shrinking it. Two well-briefed interviewers beat four unbriefed ones and cost half as
  much. This is usually the single largest interviewer-load saving in the audit.
- Scorecards before the debrief, junior voices first in the room.
- Separating "meet the team" from assessment explicitly. If the goal is selling, make it a
  short, clearly non-assessed conversation and tell the candidate that is what it is.

## 7. Presentation

The candidate presents to a group: a 30/60/90 plan, a strategy, a case response, a portfolio.

**Genuinely good at.** Structuring and synthesising a large ambiguous question. Executive
communication and handling live challenge — genuinely predictive for roles where standing
up in front of a leadership team is the job. Seeing how someone handles disagreement in
public.

**Bad at.** Day-to-day craft, collaboration, and detail work. It also carries a substantial
preparation cost that falls unevenly, and it advantages practised presenters over capable
operators — which is a real problem when the role does not require presenting.

**Keep it when.** The role genuinely involves presenting to senior audiences, and the brief
is answerable in a stated, modest preparation time.

**Cut it when.** The role does not involve presenting. It is standing in for a work sample
you could design better. The brief requires information the candidate does not have, so it
becomes a test of guessing. Or it duplicates a case study already run.

**Fix it by.**

- Stating the preparation time budget and keeping it modest.
- Giving real material to work from rather than expecting research into a company they
  cannot see inside. A 30/60/90 built on public information is a creative-writing exercise.
- Assigning competencies and a rubric, scored independently.
- Weighting the Q&A more than the deck. Handling challenge is the predictive part.
- Keeping the audience small — a large audience raises the stakes without adding signal.
- Never asking for a plan you intend to use.

## 8. Culture or values interview

A stage assessing "fit", "values alignment", or "how they work".

**Genuinely good at.** Nothing, in its unstructured form. In its structured form — specific,
named behaviours that this team actually needs, with anchored evidence standards — it is
assessing behavioural competencies, which is valuable and which is what it should be called.

**Bad at.** Everything, unstructured. It is unanchored, so it rewards similarity to the
interviewer; it carries decisive weight because "I just didn't feel it" is hard to
challenge; and it is where most of the bias in a loop enters. It is also where the loop is
least defensible if a decision is ever questioned, because there is nothing written down.

**Keep it when.** It has been converted into a structured behavioural interview with named
competencies drawn from what the team actually needs — not from the values poster.

**Cut it when.** It cannot be defined well enough to anchor. If nobody can write down what
a fail looks like, the stage is producing noise and giving it a decisive weight.

**Fix it by.**

- Renaming it. "Values interview" invites impressionistic judgement; "behavioural interview:
  collaboration and ownership" does not.
- Deriving two or three behavioural competencies from the failure question — what would make
  a technically capable hire fail in this team? — rather than from the company values list.
  The answer is usually pace, ambiguity tolerance, or stakeholder friction, and it is often
  the competency the rest of the loop does not assess.
- Behavioural questions about past situations, with anchored scales and evidence standards.
- Explicitly ruling out similarity-based judgement: hobbies, background, school, "would I
  get a drink with them". Add-to-the-team, not fit-the-team.
- Where the stage cannot be structured and cannot be cut politically, at minimum strip its
  veto power: it can flag a concern for the debrief, it cannot reject alone.

## 9. Founder or executive final

The last conversation, with a founder, CEO or senior executive.

**Genuinely good at.** Closing — a senior candidate deciding between offers is often moved
by this conversation more than by anything else in the process. Communicating mission and
strategic context credibly. Assessing strategic judgement and ambition at genuinely senior
levels, where the exec is the only person who has done the job. For a small company, a
founder's read on whether someone will thrive in that specific environment carries real
information.

**Bad at.** Consistency and comparability. It is usually unstructured, unrecorded, and run
by someone who does not interview often. It sits at the point of maximum sunk cost, so a
rejection there wastes the entire process. And it frequently operates as a veto with no
criteria — which makes it both the highest-risk and the least examinable stage in the loop.

**Keep it when.** The company is small enough that the exec genuinely works with every hire,
the role is senior enough to warrant it, or the stage is doing real closing work.

**Cut it when.** It is applied to every role at every level in a company too large for that
to be meaningful, and it is a bottleneck on every hire. Or it has become a formality that
nobody has ever failed — in which case it is not an assessment and should be repositioned
as a selling conversation.

**Fix it by.**

- **Deciding what it is: assessment or selling.** Both are legitimate. Ambiguity is not. If
  it is selling, say so, tell the candidate, and take away its veto.
- **Moving it earlier** if it is an assessment. A veto at the end wastes the whole loop; the
  same veto at stage two costs an hour. This is usually easier to sell than removing it,
  because it does not take anything away from the exec.
- **Giving it one or two competencies only that person can assess** — strategic judgement,
  mission alignment at the level they can actually judge — and a scorecard like everyone
  else's.
- **Requiring a recorded reason for a rejection at this stage.** Non-negotiable. An
  unrecorded final veto is the largest consistency and bias exposure in most loops.
- **Capping the time** — 30 minutes with a defined purpose beats an open-ended conversation
  that runs to whatever the exec's calendar allows.
- **Naming the bottleneck cost** if this stage is the elapsed-time problem. Exec calendars
  are frequently the reason a process takes an extra ten days, and quantifying it is the
  argument.

## 10. References

Structured conversations with people who have worked with the candidate.

**Genuinely good at.** Corroborating specific claims, especially scope: whether they really
owned the team, the budget, the decision. Confirming the shape of what happened rather than
the candidate's framing. Occasionally, and valuably, giving the hiring manager real
onboarding information about how to work with this person.

**Bad at.** Prediction, and discovering anything new. Candidates choose their referees, and
the conversation is often perfunctory. Back-channel references — informal conversations
with people the candidate did not nominate — carry serious data-protection and fairness
problems, particularly where the candidate's current employer is not aware they are looking.

**Keep it when.** It is structured, run against specific competencies with real questions,
and used either to corroborate a specific doubt or to inform onboarding.

**Cut it when.** It is a formality that has never changed a decision, and the process is
slow. If nobody can name a hire that references stopped, the stage is costing days and
producing nothing.

**Fix it by.**

- Targeting them: pick the one or two competencies where the loop is least certain and ask
  about those specifically, rather than running a generic script.
- Asking for corroboration rather than opinion. "What was her scope — how many people,
  what budget, what did she decide alone?" gets facts. "Would you hire her again?" gets a
  ritual.
- Asking the hiring manager to run at least one of them personally. The onboarding value
  alone justifies the time.
- Running them in parallel with the offer approval rather than as a serial gate, where the
  risk profile allows. It removes days from the end of the process, which is where days are
  most expensive.
- Being explicit about consent and timing — when references are taken, whether the current
  employer is contacted, and what is recorded. This is personal data about the candidate and
  about the referee.
- Using nominated referees. Back-channelling should be a deliberate, documented exception
  with a stated reason, not a habit.

## Sequencing rules

Order matters as much as composition. Five rules, each with the reason:

1. **Cheap filters before expensive assessment.** Cost here means both interviewer hours
   and candidate hours. A loop that asks for three hours of unpaid work before anyone has
   spent thirty minutes on the candidate has its economics backwards.
2. **Tell the candidate something real before asking for something big.** Comp range, team,
   the actual problems — before any significant unpaid ask. Otherwise the withdrawal happens
   after the work, which wastes both sides.
3. **Vetoes early.** Any stage where one person can reject on their own judgement should
   happen early, or lose the veto. A late unrecorded veto discards the whole loop's
   investment and demoralises everyone who assessed before it.
4. **Independent reads stay independent.** Anything designed to give a second opinion is
   worthless if the second reader has seen the first's scorecard. Sequence and scorecard
   discipline together, or do not claim the benefit.
5. **The last stage should be the one that closes.** Whatever the candidate remembers going
   into an offer decision, make it the conversation most likely to make them want the job.
   Ending on an adversarial test and then asking someone to accept is a self-inflicted
   wound.

When the redesigned loop needs competencies, questions, probes, anchored scales and
scorecards written out for a specific role, that is `interview-kit-builder`.
